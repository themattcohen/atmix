Who Is a US Person FBAR Rules Apply To? Citizens, Residents, and Entities

Who is a US person FBAR rules apply to? That question determines whether you must file the Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts (FBAR), officially FinCEN Form 114. Under 31 CFR 1010.350(b), the Bank Secrecy Act (BSA) defines "United States person" to include US citizens, US residents, and entities formed under US law.

The FBAR threshold is $10,000 aggregate across all foreign financial accounts at any point during the calendar year. If you meet that threshold and qualify as a US person under , you must file an FBAR with FinCEN per IRS guidance

Misclassifying your status can lead to penalties up to $16,117 per violation for non-willful failures under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B), and up to $100,000 or 50% of the account balance for willful violations under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C).

This article covers every category of US person under the BSA, explains the substantial presence test, and addresses special cases such as dual citizens, DACA recipients, and US territory residents.

Who Is a US Person FBAR Requires Under 31 CFR 1010.350(b)?

A US person for FBAR purposes includes any US citizen, US resident, or domestic entity. Under 31 CFR 1010.350(b), the regulation identifies four categories: citizens of the United States, residents of the United States, domestic partnerships, and domestic corporations or other entities formed under US state law. If you fall into any of these categories and your foreign accounts exceed $10,000, you must file FBAR Form 114.

US Citizens

Every US citizen must file the FBAR if their foreign accounts exceed $10,000. This rule applies regardless of where you live. A citizen who has never set foot in the United States still qualifies as a US person. A dual citizen of the US and another country is also a US person. Living abroad or claiming a foreign domicile has no effect on your FBAR obligation. The BSA definition ties to citizenship, not to physical presence or tax residence.

Common situations where citizens may not realize they must file:

Born abroad to one or two US citizen parents

Dual nationals who grew up and live in another country

Citizens who moved abroad decades ago

Retirees living in Mexico, Panama, or Portugal on a US passport

If any of these applies to you and your foreign accounts exceed $10,000, you must file. For a step-by-step walkthrough, see our .

Green Card Holders (Lawful Permanent Residents)

Green card holders — also called lawful permanent residents — are US persons under 31 CFR 1010.350(b). Your obligation starts the calendar year you receive the I-551 card. Even if you got your green card in December, the entire year's accounts are reportable.

Conditional residents (holders of a two-year green card) are also US persons. The BSA does not separate conditional from unconditional permanent residents. You remain a US person until you formally surrender your status through Form I-407. Letting your green card expire or living abroad does not end the obligation. For a detailed look at these rules, see our article on FBAR for green card holders

Are Resident Aliens Required to File the FBAR?

Yes. A resident alien who meets the substantial presence test under IRC 7701(b) is a US person for FBAR purposes. FinCEN incorporates the IRC 7701(b) definition by reference through 31 CFR 1010.350(b)(2). This rule applies to FBAR IRS reporting even when you have no green card. Resident alien status depends on the days you spend in the United States, not on your visa type.

How the Substantial Presence Test Works

You meet the substantial presence test if you satisfy both of these conditions:

You are present in the United States for at least 31 days during the current calendar year

Your total weighted presence over three years equals 183 days or more

The weighted calculation:

Current year: Count all days at 100%

Prior year: Count days at 1/3

Two years ago: Count days at 1/6

For example: 150 days in 2025 (150) + 90 days in 2024 (30) + 60 days in 2023 (10) = 190 weighted days. This person meets substantial presence in 2025. They are a US person for FBAR in 2025.

A second example: 120 days in 2025 (120) + 90 days in 2024 (30) + 180 days in 2023 (30) = 180 weighted days. This falls short of 183, so this person does not meet substantial presence in 2025.

Closer Connection Exception and Form 8840

You may avoid US resident alien status for income tax by filing Form 8840 (Closer Connection Exception Statement). This is an IRS income tax election. However, it does not remove your FBAR obligation.

The FBAR is a BSA requirement under FinCEN, not an income tax requirement. The closer connection exception changes your income tax status. It does not change your status under 31 CFR 1010.350(b) for FBAR purposes. If you meet substantial presence, you are a US person for FBAR regardless of Form 8840.

Tax Treaty Tie-Breaker Positions and FBAR

Some income tax treaties let dual residents claim residency in the treaty country. This is a treaty tie-breaker election. The election reduces your US income tax obligations. It does not remove your FBAR requirement.

The FBAR rests on the BSA under 31 USC 5314, not on income tax law. Tax treaty provisions govern income taxes. They do not override BSA reporting rules. If you meet substantial presence under IRC 7701(b), you are a US person for FBAR even if a treaty tie-breaker positions you as a non-US resident for income tax purposes.

Who Is Considered a US Person for Tax Purposes Versus FBAR?

For income tax, a US person typically includes citizens and resident aliens under IRC 7701(a)(30). For FBAR, the definition under 31 CFR 1010.350(b) is similar but operates independently. A treaty tie-breaker election can affect income tax residency but not FBAR status. Form 8840 can affect income tax status but not FBAR status. The FBAR instructions and IRS guidance both confirm that the FBAR obligation follows the BSA definition, not the income tax definition. Additionally, FBAR Form PDF (FinCEN Form 114) requires a separate filing with FinCEN — not with the IRS.

Key differences between the two definitions:

Income tax: Treaties and elections can override US resident status

FBAR: BSA rules apply regardless of income tax elections or treaties

Filing destination: FBAR goes to FinCEN; income tax returns go to the IRS

Form used: FBAR uses FinCEN Form 114 (FBAR form), not any IRS income tax form

This distinction matters for anyone with a treaty tie-breaker position, a closer connection exception election, or dual citizenship. Your income tax position and your FBAR position may differ. See our FBAR vs. FATCA comparison for more on the difference between BSA and income tax obligations.

Are US Entities Required to File the FBAR?

Yes. Under 31 CFR 1010.350(b), domestic corporations, partnerships, limited liability companies (LLCs), and trusts formed under US law are US persons. The entity must file if it holds a financial interest in or signature authority over foreign accounts exceeding $10,000. Formation under US law is the only test. Business activity can be entirely overseas.

Corporations and LLCs

A corporation formed in Delaware, an LLC organized in Wyoming, or an S corporation formed in California — all are US persons. This applies even if the corporation conducts all its business in other countries. The FBAR instructions confirm that formation under any US state law makes an entity a US person. A foreign subsidiary of a US corporation is not a US person because the subsidiary is a separate entity formed under foreign law. However, the US parent may have FBAR duties if it has signature authority over the subsidiary's foreign accounts.

Partnerships and Trusts

A general partnership or limited partnership organized in any US state is a US person. The entity itself must file the FBAR for any foreign account it holds. Individual partners may have separate FBAR obligations if they personally hold or have authority over foreign accounts.

A trust formed under US law with a US trustee or grantor is also a US person under 31 CFR 1010.350(b). The trust's FBAR duty depends on financial interest and control. For details on entity reporting, see our article on FBAR for business accounts and signatory authority

What About DACA Recipients and Residents of US Territories?

DACA recipients and undocumented residents who meet the substantial presence test are US persons for FBAR. The BSA definition under 31 CFR 1010.350(b) does not require lawful immigration status. It requires residence under IRC 7701(b), which turns on days present in the United States. A DACA recipient who has lived in the United States for years will almost certainly meet the substantial presence test and must file.

Residents of US territories — Puerto Rico, Guam, the US Virgin Islands, American Samoa, and the Northern Mariana Islands — are US citizens under 8 USC 1401 and therefore US persons for FBAR. These residents have special income tax rules, but those rules do not change their FBAR obligations. The FBAR is a BSA requirement, separate from income tax. Territory residents with foreign accounts over $10,000 must file.

Decision Tree: Do You Need to File the FBAR?

Use this decision tree to determine whether you are a US person for FBAR purposes. Work through the questions in order and stop when you reach a conclusion. Each step maps to the definition in 31 CFR 1010.350(b) and the IRS FBAR guidelines.

Are you a US citizen? If yes — you are a US person. File if your foreign accounts exceed $10,000 at any point during the year.

Do you hold a US green card (I-551)? If yes — you are a US person. File if accounts exceed $10,000.

Are you present in the United States for at least 31 days this year? If no — you likely do not meet substantial presence. You are probably not a US person based on presence.

Does your weighted day count equal 183 or more? (Current year at 100% + prior year at 1/3 + two years ago at 1/6.) If yes — you are a US person under IRC 7701(b). File if accounts exceed $10,000.

Does a tax treaty tie-breaker apply? Even if yes — your FBAR obligation remains. File if accounts exceed $10,000.

Is your entity formed under US law? If yes — the entity is a US person. File if it holds foreign accounts over $10,000.

None of the above? You are likely not a US person for FBAR. If uncertain, consult a tax professional.

If you fall into any of categories 1 through 6 and your foreign accounts exceeded $10,000 at any point during the year, the FBAR deadline is April 15, with an automatic extension to October 15, per 31 CFR 1010.306 and IRS guidance. See our FBAR filing deadline guide for details.

What Penalties Apply If a US Person Does Not File?

Penalties are set by 31 USC 5321 and apply per account, per year. A US person who fails to file faces three levels of enforcement based on whether the failure was willful.

Non-willful violation: Up to $16,117 per account per year under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B) (2025 inflation-adjusted amount)

Willful violation: The greater of $100,000 or 50% of the account balance per year under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)

Criminal penalties: Up to $250,000 in fines and 5 years imprisonment under 31 USC 5322

Three unreported accounts for two years equals six violations. At the non-willful rate, that is up to $96,702 in penalties (6 × $16,117 under 31 USC 5321). For a full breakdown, see our FBAR penalties guide

These are the most frequently asked questions about who is a US person for FBAR purposes. The answers below cover the main categories — citizens, resident aliens, green card holders, and entities. For advice on your specific situation, consult a qualified tax professional.

Who is a US person for FBAR filing purposes?

A US person under 31 CFR 1010.350(b) includes US citizens, lawful permanent residents, and resident aliens who meet the substantial presence test under IRC 7701(b). It also includes domestic entities — corporations, partnerships, LLCs, and trusts formed under US law. Any US person with foreign financial accounts exceeding $10,000 must file FinCEN Form 114 with FinCEN.

Does a US citizen living abroad still need to file the FBAR?

Yes. US citizenship, not physical location, determines FBAR status. Under 31 CFR 1010.350(b), every US citizen is a US person regardless of where they live. Dual citizens, long-term expats, and citizens who have never lived in the United States must all file if their foreign accounts exceed $10,000 at any point under 31 USC 5314.

What is the substantial presence test for FBAR?

The substantial presence test under IRC 7701(b) counts your US presence over three years. You must be present at least 31 days in the current year. Your total weighted days — current year at 100%, prior year at 1/3, and two years ago at 1/6 — must reach 183 days. If you meet both conditions, you are a US resident alien and therefore a US person for FBAR purposes.

Does a tax treaty tie-breaker election remove my FBAR obligation?

No. A treaty tie-breaker election reduces your US income tax obligations but does not remove your FBAR duty. The FBAR is a Bank Secrecy Act requirement under 31 USC 5314, separate from income tax law. If you meet the substantial presence test, your FBAR obligation exists regardless of any treaty election. See our FBAR vs. FATCA comparison for more on how BSA and tax rules differ.

Are corporations and LLCs required to file the FBAR?

Yes. Under 31 CFR 1010.350(b), any domestic corporation, partnership, or LLC formed under the laws of any US state is a US person. If the entity holds a financial interest in or has signature authority over foreign financial accounts exceeding $10,000, it must file FBAR Form 114. Formation under US law is the only test — business activity can be entirely overseas.

Do I still have to file the FBAR if Form 8840 reduces my income tax residency?

Yes. Form 8840 (Closer Connection Exception) is an IRS income tax election. It affects your income tax residency status, not your FBAR status. The FBAR is governed by 31 CFR 1010.350, not the Internal Revenue Code. If you meet the substantial presence test, you are a US person for FBAR even if Form 8840 treats you as a non-resident for income tax.

Who is considered a US person for tax purposes?

For income tax, IRC 7701(a)(30) defines US persons as citizens and resident aliens. For FBAR, the definition under 31 CFR 1010.350(b) is similar but operates independently. A treaty tie-breaker can change your income tax residency but not your FBAR status. The FBAR instructions require a separate filing with FinCEN regardless of your income tax position with the IRS.

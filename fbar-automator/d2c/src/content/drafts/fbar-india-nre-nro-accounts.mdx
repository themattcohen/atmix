# FBAR India NRE NRO Accounts: What US Persons Must Report

More than four million Indian-Americans live in the United States. Many hold accounts in India — NRE savings accounts, NRO accounts, FCNR deposits, Public Provident Fund accounts, fixed deposits, and demat accounts for Indian equities. If the combined value of all your foreign financial accounts exceeded $10,000 at any point during the calendar year, you must file the Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts (FBAR) with the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350).

FBAR India NRE NRO accounts is the phrase many Indian-Americans search when they discover this rule. NRE, NRO, and FCNR accounts are all foreign financial accounts under the Bank Secrecy Act (BSA). Every account type discussed in this article is reportable on FinCEN Form 114. The only question is whether your total foreign account values cross the $10,000 threshold in aggregate value during the calendar year.

India signed a FATCA Model 1 Intergovernmental Agreement with the United States. Under this agreement, Indian banks report US person account data to India's Central Board of Direct Taxes (CBDT), which then shares it with the Internal Revenue Service (IRS). If you skip the FBAR, the IRS may already have records of your Indian bank accounts.

Any US person — including a US citizen, US resident, or green card holder — who has a financial interest in or signature authority over foreign financial accounts must file the FBAR if the aggregate value of those accounts exceeds $10,000 at any time during the calendar year. This applies whether you are a non-resident Indian (NRI) who became a US citizen, a green card holder working on an H-1B visa, or a person of Indian origin who naturalized decades ago.

## Which FBAR India NRE NRO Accounts Are Reportable?

All standard Indian bank accounts held by a US person are reportable under [31 CFR 1010.350(c)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). This includes NRE accounts, NRO accounts, FCNR accounts, fixed deposits, PPF accounts where you have a financial interest, and demat accounts. The account name and any Indian tax treatment do not reduce your US FBAR reporting duty. You report each foreign financial account separately and list the peak account value in USD.

The Bank Secrecy Act defines a foreign financial account broadly. Any bank account, securities account, or other financial account maintained at a financial institution located outside the United States qualifies. Indian banks — whether State Bank of India, HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank, Axis Bank, or Punjab National Bank — are all foreign financial institutions for FBAR purposes.

Here is a summary of the most common Indian account types and their FBAR status.

| Account Type | Full Name | FBAR Reportable? |
|---|---|---|
| NRE | Non-Resident External | Yes |
| NRO | Non-Resident Ordinary | Yes |
| FCNR | Foreign Currency Non-Resident | Yes |
| PPF | Public Provident Fund | Yes (if US person has financial interest) |
| Fixed Deposit | Fixed-term bank deposit (FD) | Yes |
| Demat Account | Dematerialized securities account | Yes |
| NPS | National Pension System | Yes |
| SCSS | Senior Citizens Savings Scheme | Yes |

### NRE Accounts and FBAR Reporting Requirements

An NRE account is a Non-Resident External account. Indian banks open NRE accounts for non-resident Indians to hold Indian rupees funded from earnings outside India. Interest earned on NRE accounts is tax-free under Indian law, and account holders can repatriate the principal and interest back to the US at any time without restriction.

NRE account status under Indian law does not affect US FBAR reporting duties. Under [31 USC 5314](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5314) and the Bank Secrecy Act, you must report every NRE account on the FBAR. An NRE savings account with a peak balance of ₹900,000 (about $10,800 at ₹83 per USD) clears the $10,000 threshold on its own.

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) allows NRIs to hold NRE accounts in savings, current, recurring deposit, and fixed deposit forms. Each of these sub-accounts is a separate foreign financial account for FBAR filing purposes. If you hold both an NRE savings account and an NRE fixed deposit at the same bank, report each one separately with its own maximum account value.

When you return to India permanently, the RBI requires you to convert your NRE account to a resident account. If you remain a US person after that change — for example, because you still hold a green card or US citizenship — the converted account is still a foreign bank account and still reportable on the FBAR.

### NRO Accounts and FBAR Reporting Requirements

An NRO account is a Non-Resident Ordinary account. NRO accounts hold Indian-source income — rental income, dividends, interest, pension payments, and other earnings generated within India. NRO accounts are subject to Indian income tax, and the RBI imposes repatriation limits on NRO balances.

NRO accounts are reportable on the FBAR regardless of those repatriation restrictions. The RBI limit on moving money out of India does not change your US FBAR reporting obligation. Many Indian-Americans hold NRO accounts to receive rent from Indian properties. A rental property generating ₹50,000 per month adds ₹600,000 ($7,229) to the NRO balance each year. Combined with an NRE account, the aggregate value of your foreign financial accounts often exceeds $10,000.

Unlike NRE accounts, NRO account interest is taxable in India at source. Indian banks deduct tax at source (TDS) on NRO interest at rates up to 30% for NRIs. This Indian tax does not reduce your FBAR filing obligation. However, you may claim a foreign tax credit on your US income tax return for taxes paid to India on NRO interest income.

The RBI currently allows NRIs to repatriate up to $1 million per financial year from NRO accounts after paying applicable Indian taxes. This repatriation cap is an Indian regulatory matter. It has no bearing on whether the account must appear on your FBAR.

### NRE vs NRO: Key Differences for FBAR Purposes

Both NRE and NRO accounts are foreign financial accounts reportable on the FBAR. However, the differences between these account types matter for your overall US tax compliance, even though both require FBAR reporting.

| Feature | NRE Account | NRO Account |
|---|---|---|
| **Funding source** | Foreign earnings (outside India) | Indian-source income |
| **Interest taxable in India?** | No (tax-free) | Yes (TDS at up to 30%) |
| **Repatriation** | Fully repatriable (principal + interest) | Limited to $1M/year after taxes |
| **Currency** | Indian rupees (INR) | Indian rupees (INR) |
| **FBAR reportable?** | Yes | Yes |
| **Typical holders** | NRIs sending money to India | NRIs with Indian rental income, pensions |

For FBAR filing purposes, the distinction between NRE and NRO does not change your reporting obligation. Both are foreign bank accounts held at a financial institution outside the United States. You must report each NRE account and each NRO account separately on your FBAR with the maximum account value during the calendar year.

The key difference arises on your US income tax return. NRE interest is tax-free in India but fully taxable in the US. NRO interest is taxed in India, and you can claim a foreign tax credit for the Indian TDS on your US return. Neither treatment affects whether you file the FBAR.

### FCNR Accounts (Foreign Currency Non-Resident)

An FCNR account is a Foreign Currency Non-Resident deposit account. Unlike NRE and NRO accounts, an FCNR account holds a foreign currency — USD, GBP, EUR, AUD, JPY, or other RBI-approved currencies. FCNR accounts are time deposits with fixed maturities.

If the FCNR account holds USD, the peak balance is the reportable amount directly — no currency conversion is needed. If it holds a different currency, apply the US Treasury year-end exchange rate. An FCNR deposit held in USD at SBI or HDFC Bank is a foreign financial account under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350) because the bank is located outside the United States.

FCNR deposits eliminate exchange rate risk for NRIs because the deposit stays in the original foreign currency. However, this feature does not change FBAR reporting. The account remains a foreign financial account regardless of what currency it holds.

## Indian Fixed Deposits on Your FBAR

Fixed deposits (FDs) at Indian banks are reportable foreign financial accounts on the FBAR. An FD is a time-bound deposit paying a fixed interest rate for a specified term, typically ranging from seven days to ten years. Common FD providers include State Bank of India, HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank, Axis Bank, Kotak Mahindra Bank, and Punjab National Bank.

Report the peak balance of each FD during the calendar year. If you reinvest a matured FD into a new FD, the new FD is a separate account for FBAR purposes. A laddered FD portfolio worth ₹2,000,000 converts to about $24,096 at ₹83 per USD — well above the $10,000 threshold under [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306).

Many NRIs hold multiple fixed deposits across different Indian banks for diversification and to stay within deposit insurance limits. Each FD is a separate foreign financial account. Even a small FD contributes to your aggregate value for the $10,000 threshold calculation.

### NRE Fixed Deposits and FBAR Reporting

An NRE fixed deposit is a term deposit funded from foreign earnings through your NRE account. NRE FDs earn interest that is tax-free in India and are fully repatriable. Indian banks offer NRE FD terms from one to ten years.

For FBAR reporting, each NRE fixed deposit is a separate foreign financial account. If you hold an NRE savings account and three NRE fixed deposits at HDFC Bank, you must report four accounts on your FBAR. The maximum account value for each NRE FD is the principal plus any accrued interest at its peak during the calendar year.

NRE FD interest rates are typically lower than NRO FD rates because of the tax-free status. Regardless of the interest rate or the Indian tax treatment, each NRE fixed deposit must appear on your FBAR with its peak value converted to USD.

### NRO Fixed Deposits and FBAR Reporting

An NRO fixed deposit is a term deposit funded from Indian-source income through your NRO account. NRO FDs earn interest subject to Indian TDS, and repatriation of the principal requires RBI approval within the annual $1 million limit.

Each NRO fixed deposit is a separate foreign financial account for FBAR filing. Report the maximum account value including accrued interest during the calendar year. If an NRO FD matures and you renew it, the renewed FD is a new account.

The repatriation limit on NRO fixed deposits does not affect your FBAR obligation. Even if you cannot move the money to the US, you still have a financial interest in the account. The FBAR requires reporting regardless of whether you can access or repatriate the funds.

## Is a PPF Account Reportable on FBAR?

Yes. A Public Provident Fund (PPF) account is a government-backed long-term savings scheme with a 15-year lock-in period. PPF accounts are reportable on the FBAR if you have a financial interest in the account. Under [31 CFR 1010.350(e)(1)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350), you have a financial interest if you own the account. A PPF account in your own name gives you a direct financial interest.

The RBI restricts NRIs from opening new PPF accounts. However, NRIs who opened PPF accounts before becoming non-resident Indians can continue the account until maturity. Those accounts remain reportable each year on the FBAR. A PPF balance of ₹1,500,000 ($18,072 at ₹83/USD) is reportable.

PPF accounts are among the most commonly missed foreign financial accounts because filers assume the Indian tax exemption extends to US reporting — it does not. The PPF interest exemption is an Indian income tax benefit under the Income Tax Act, 1961. It has no effect on Bank Secrecy Act reporting requirements.

Under current RBI rules, if a PPF holder becomes an NRI, the account continues on a non-repatriable basis until maturity. You cannot extend the PPF account beyond its original maturity after becoming an NRI. Despite these restrictions, the account remains a foreign financial account reportable on the FBAR for every calendar year you hold it.

## Indian Mutual Funds and Demat Accounts on FBAR

Indian mutual funds and demat accounts present unique FBAR reporting questions for NRIs and Indian-Americans. The answer depends on how you hold the investment — directly through a registrar, or through a dematerialized (demat) securities account.

Indian mutual funds purchased directly through fund houses are typically held in statement-of-account form by registrars like CAMS (Computer Age Management Services) or KFintech. The IRS has not issued specific guidance on whether a registrar account at CAMS or KFintech constitutes a "financial account" under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). However, a conservative FBAR filing approach treats each registrar account holding your mutual fund units as a reportable foreign financial account.

### Mutual Funds Held Through CAMS or KFintech

If you hold Indian mutual funds directly through CAMS or KFintech — not through a demat account — the safest approach is to report the registrar account on your FBAR. List the registrar (CAMS or KFintech) as the financial institution, provide your folio number as the account number, and report the peak aggregate value of all mutual fund holdings under that folio during the calendar year.

This conservative approach protects you from FBAR penalties in case the IRS or FinCEN later determines that registrar accounts are reportable foreign financial accounts. The cost of over-reporting is zero. The cost of under-reporting can be up to $16,117 per non-willful violation.

### Demat Accounts for Indian Securities

A demat (dematerialized) account holds Indian stocks, mutual funds, bonds, and exchange-traded funds in electronic form. If you have a financial interest in or signature authority over a demat account at HDFC Securities, Zerodha, ICICI Direct, or any other Indian broker, you must report it on the FBAR.

Demat accounts are securities accounts under [31 CFR 1010.350(c)(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). Report the peak value of all holdings in the demat account during the calendar year, converted to USD using the Treasury year-end exchange rate. A demat account holding Nifty 50 ETFs worth ₹800,000 ($9,639) does not trigger FBAR reporting alone. However, combined with an NRE savings account, the aggregate value crosses $10,000.

If you hold mutual funds both inside a demat account and directly through CAMS or KFintech, the demat holdings are part of the demat account value. Do not double-count mutual funds that appear in both your demat statement and your CAMS/KFintech consolidated account statement.

## Reserve Bank of India Regulations for NRI Account Holders

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) regulates foreign exchange and banking for non-resident Indians under the Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA), 1999. RBI regulations determine which account types NRIs can hold and how money flows between India and the US. These rules do not override or reduce your FBAR filing obligation, but they shape what accounts you may hold.

Key RBI regulations affecting NRI account holders and FBAR reporting include the following.

The RBI requires NRIs to redesignate resident accounts as NRO accounts when they leave India. If you held a resident savings account at ICICI Bank before moving to the US, the bank should have converted it to an NRO account. That NRO account is reportable on the FBAR.

The RBI permits NRIs to hold NRE, NRO, and FCNR accounts at authorized dealer banks. Each account type has different rules for funding, repatriation, and taxation. All of them are foreign financial accounts under the Bank Secrecy Act.

The RBI restricts NRIs from holding certain account types. For example, NRIs cannot open new PPF accounts or new resident fixed deposits. However, accounts opened before becoming an NRI may continue. If you hold a grandfathered account that the RBI allows you to maintain, it remains reportable on the FBAR.

RBI regulations also govern the maximum amount you can repatriate from India each year. The current limit is $1 million per financial year from NRO accounts. This repatriation cap has no impact on FBAR reporting. You must report the full account value regardless of how much you can move out of India.

## How to Calculate Maximum Account Value for Indian Accounts

The FBAR requires you to report the maximum account value of each foreign financial account during the calendar year. For Indian bank accounts denominated in INR, this means identifying the peak balance and converting it to USD. The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network requires a specific conversion method.

The maximum account value is the largest amount of currency or non-monetary assets in the account at any time during the calendar year. For a savings account, check monthly statements for the highest closing balance. For a fixed deposit, the maximum value is the principal plus any accrued interest at its peak during the year.

If you do not know the exact peak balance, the FBAR instructions allow you to use a reasonable approximation. Periodic account statements (monthly or quarterly) are generally sufficient to determine the maximum account value.

### INR to USD Conversion for FBAR

To convert Indian rupees to USD for FBAR reporting, use the US Treasury's end-of-year exchange rate. Apply the December 31 rate to the peak balance in INR for each account. Do not use the spot rate on the date of the peak or a yearly average. The FBAR instructions require the Treasury year-end rate for all currency conversion. For more detail, see our guide on [FBAR exchange rates and the Treasury Department](/blog/fbar-exchange-rates-treasury-department).

The Treasury publishes exchange rates including the Indian rupee on its [Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange page](https://fiscaldata.treasury.gov/datasets/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/treasury-reporting-rates-of-exchange). The rate is updated quarterly and the December 31 rate is the one you use for FBAR purposes.

If the Treasury does not publish a rate for a specific currency, use another verifiable exchange rate and note the source. For the Indian rupee, the Treasury has consistently published rates, so this exception rarely applies.

### Step-by-Step INR Conversion

Follow these steps to convert your Indian account balances for FBAR filing.

1. Find the peak INR balance of each Indian account during the calendar year.
2. Look up the Treasury's December 31 rate for INR/USD at [fiscal.treasury.gov](https://fiscaldata.treasury.gov/datasets/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/treasury-reporting-rates-of-exchange).
3. Divide the INR balance by the INR-per-USD rate (or multiply if quoted as USD-per-INR).
4. Enter the converted USD value for each account on the FBAR.
5. Sum the converted USD values for all foreign accounts worldwide.
6. If the total aggregate value exceeds $10,000, you must file the FBAR.

### Conversion Example: Three Indian Accounts

Priya is a US green card holder who moved from Chennai to California in 2020. She holds three Indian accounts:

- NRE savings account at SBI: peak balance ₹720,000
- NRO account at HDFC Bank: peak balance ₹380,000
- PPF account (opened before becoming NRI): balance ₹1,200,000

Using the Treasury December 31, 2025 rate of 1 USD = ₹85.50:

- NRE: ₹720,000 ÷ 85.50 = $8,421
- NRO: ₹380,000 ÷ 85.50 = $4,444
- PPF: ₹1,200,000 ÷ 85.50 = $14,035

Aggregate maximum value: **$26,900**. Priya must file an FBAR for 2025 under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350), reporting all three accounts. She should also check [Form 8938 (FATCA)](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-8938) — if she files jointly, the domestic FATCA threshold is $100,000.

## Step-by-Step Guide for Reporting Indian Accounts on FBAR

Filing the FBAR for Indian accounts involves gathering your Indian bank statements, calculating maximum account values, converting INR to USD, and submitting FinCEN Form 114 electronically. Here is how to complete the process.

**Step 1: Identify all foreign financial accounts.** List every Indian bank account you hold or have signature authority over. Include NRE accounts, NRO accounts, FCNR accounts, fixed deposits (both NRE FDs and NRO FDs), PPF accounts, demat accounts, and any other foreign financial accounts outside India.

**Step 2: Gather bank statements.** Obtain statements for each account covering the full calendar year. For Indian bank accounts, download statements from your bank's NRI portal (SBI, HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank, and most Indian banks offer online statement downloads for NRI accounts). For PPF accounts, request a statement from the post office or bank branch holding your PPF.

**Step 3: Determine the maximum account value.** For each account, identify the highest balance during the calendar year. For fixed deposits, include accrued interest if it was credited during the year. For demat accounts, determine the peak market value of all securities held in the account.

**Step 4: Convert INR to USD.** Use the US Treasury's December 31 exchange rate for the reporting year. Divide each INR peak balance by the rate. Record the USD value for each account.

**Step 5: Check the $10,000 threshold.** Add the maximum account values of all your foreign financial accounts worldwide (not just Indian accounts). If the aggregate value exceeded $10,000 at any point during the calendar year, you must file the FBAR.

**Step 6: File FinCEN Form 114.** Submit the FBAR electronically through the BSA E-Filing system at [bsaefiling.fincen.treas.gov](https://bsaefiling.fincen.treas.gov). For each Indian account, enter the bank name, bank address, account number, account type, and maximum account value in USD. The FBAR is due April 15 with an automatic extension to October 15.

**Step 7: Keep records.** Retain copies of your Indian bank statements and FBAR filings for at least six years from the FBAR due date, as required by [31 CFR 1010.420](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.420). Store your currency conversion calculations as supporting documentation.

## Common FBAR Mistakes for NRIs and Indian Americans

NRIs and Indian-Americans make several recurring FBAR filing mistakes with their Indian accounts. Avoiding these errors can prevent FBAR penalties and reduce the risk of an IRS inquiry.

**Mistake 1: Assuming NRE interest exemption means no US reporting.** The NRE account interest tax exemption is an Indian tax rule. It does not create a US reporting exemption. Many first-time filers skip the FBAR because they believe tax-free NRE interest means the account is invisible to US authorities. Under the Bank Secrecy Act, every foreign financial account is reportable regardless of how the foreign country taxes it.

**Mistake 2: Forgetting fixed deposits linked to NRE or NRO accounts.** NRE and NRO fixed deposits are separate foreign financial accounts. If you hold an NRE savings account and two NRE FDs at ICICI Bank, you have three reportable accounts — not one. Each FD must be listed individually on the FBAR with its own maximum account value.

**Mistake 3: Omitting PPF accounts.** PPF accounts are commonly missed because filers do not think of government savings schemes as bank accounts. A PPF account at a post office or Indian bank branch is a foreign financial account under the Bank Secrecy Act. The 15-year lock-in does not eliminate the FBAR reporting requirement.

**Mistake 4: Using the wrong exchange rate.** The FBAR requires the US Treasury's December 31 exchange rate — not the RBI reference rate, not the Google exchange rate, and not the rate on the date of the peak balance. Using the wrong rate can overstate or understate your account values.

**Mistake 5: Ignoring demat accounts.** Many NRIs hold Indian stocks or mutual funds in a demat account and do not realize the account is reportable. A demat account at Zerodha, HDFC Securities, or ICICI Direct is a securities account under the Bank Secrecy Act and must appear on the FBAR.

**Mistake 6: Not reporting closed accounts.** If you closed an Indian bank account during the calendar year, you must still report it if your aggregate foreign account values exceeded $10,000. Use the maximum balance the account held before closure.

**Mistake 7: Confusing FBAR with Form 8938.** The FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) is filed with FinCEN. Form 8938 is filed with the IRS as part of your tax return. Filing one does not satisfy the other. Many NRIs file Form 8938 but forget the FBAR, or vice versa.

**Mistake 8: Not aggregating accounts across banks.** The $10,000 threshold is based on the aggregate value of all foreign financial accounts. An NRE account at SBI with a peak of $6,000 and an NRO account at HDFC Bank with a peak of $5,000 together exceed $10,000 — both must be reported.

## US-India Tax Treaty and FBAR Obligations

The India-US Double Tax Avoidance Agreement (DTAA) does not reduce FBAR reporting obligations. The FBAR is a Bank Secrecy Act requirement under [31 USC 5314](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5314), not an income tax rule. The DTAA covers income tax credits, reduced withholding rates, and tie-breaker residency rules. It does not override BSA reporting duties enforced by the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network.

Many Indian-Americans use DTAA treaty positions to reduce US tax on NRO interest income. Under the DTAA, India may withhold tax on interest at a reduced rate, and you can claim a foreign tax credit on your US return. That treaty election is separate from FBAR. You can claim a DTAA treaty benefit on your income tax return and still need to file the FBAR for the same NRE and NRO accounts.

### DTAA Benefits Do Not Override BSA Requirements

The DTAA tiebreaker residency rule does not help with FBAR obligations. If you claim non-resident status under the DTAA tiebreaker, you are treated as a non-resident for income tax purposes. However, the FBAR is based on your status as a "US person" under the Bank Secrecy Act. That definition covers US citizens and green card holders regardless of treaty elections. A green card holder who claims DTAA treaty residency in India for income tax purposes remains a US person for FBAR filing. See our article on [FBAR rules for green card holders](/blog/fbar-green-card-holders) for more.

The DTAA also does not change the $10,000 threshold, the maximum account value reporting requirement, or the FBAR penalties. Treaty benefits apply only to income tax — not to the Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts.

## What Are the FBAR Penalties for Unreported Indian Accounts?

The IRS can assess civil FBAR penalties for each calendar year you fail to file the FBAR. For non-willful failures, the non-willful penalty is up to **$16,117 per violation** under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321) (2026 inflation-adjusted amount). The Supreme Court ruled in *United States v. Bittner* (2023) that non-willful penalties apply per report, not per account. One missed year equals one violation.

Willful violations carry higher FBAR penalties. A willful failure to file results in a penalty of the greater of **$100,000** or **50% of the unreported account balance** under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). Criminal penalties under [31 USC 5322](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5322) can reach **$250,000** and five years in prison.

Additionally, India's FATCA Model 1 IGA increases detection risk for unreported Indian bank accounts. Indian banks report US person account data to the CBDT, which shares it with the IRS. The IRS often holds records of NRE and NRO account balances before it checks FBAR compliance. Not filing the FBAR when the IRS already has matching data from Indian banks substantially increases your penalty risk.

For a full breakdown of the penalty structure, see our article on [FBAR penalties and what happens if you do not file](/blog/fbar-penalties-what-happens-if-you-dont-file).

## Real-World FBAR India NRE NRO Scenarios

These examples show how FBAR requirements apply to common Indian-American situations. All three filers hold India NRE, NRO, or related foreign financial accounts and must report them under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). Each example uses approximate INR-to-USD currency conversion at ₹85 per USD.

### Scenario 1: Software Engineer With NRE and Fixed Deposits

Arjun is a US citizen who moved from Hyderabad to Seattle in 2018. He keeps his State Bank of India NRE savings account and a ladder of fixed deposits at HDFC Bank.

- SBI NRE savings account: peak ₹1,500,000 ($17,647)
- HDFC Bank FD #1 (3-year NRE fixed deposit): ₹2,000,000 ($23,529)
- HDFC Bank FD #2 (5-year NRE fixed deposit): ₹3,500,000 ($41,176)

Aggregate maximum value: **$82,352**. Arjun must file an FBAR for 2025 under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350) and report all three foreign financial accounts. He should also verify whether his total foreign assets exceed the FATCA threshold under [26 USC 6038D](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D), which triggers Form 8938.

If your situation is similar but you missed prior years, the [Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/streamlined-filing-compliance-procedures) may let you catch up with reduced FBAR penalties.

### Scenario 2: Retiree With NRO Rental Income Account

Meena is a US citizen who retired in Florida. Her parents left her a house in Bangalore that she rents out. The rent flows into her NRO account at Axis Bank. She also holds an old PPF account opened before she immigrated.

- Axis Bank NRO account: peak ₹650,000 ($7,647)
- PPF account: balance ₹2,200,000 ($25,882)

Aggregate maximum value: **$33,529**. Meena must file an FBAR. The NRO account alone falls below the $10,000 threshold, but the aggregate value with the PPF account clears it. She must also report the NRO rental income on her US tax return and may claim a foreign tax credit for Indian TDS withheld on that income.

### Scenario 3: Green Card Holder With Demat Account and NRE Account

Vikram received his US green card in 2023. He has an HDFC Securities demat account holding Nifty 50 index funds and an ICICI Bank NRE savings account.

- HDFC Securities demat account: peak value ₹950,000 ($11,176)
- ICICI Bank NRE savings: peak ₹820,000 ($9,647)

Aggregate maximum value: **$20,823**. Vikram must file an FBAR for 2025. His FBAR filing obligation began the calendar year he received his green card, because a green card holder is a US person under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). See our article on [FBAR foreign pension and retirement accounts](/blog/fbar-foreign-pension-retirement-accounts) if Vikram also holds an NPS account.

## What Is the FBAR Deadline for Indian Account Holders?

The FBAR for the 2025 tax year is due on **April 15, 2026** under [31 CFR 1010.306(c)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306). FinCEN grants an automatic extension to **October 15, 2026** — you do not need to request it. No FBAR penalties apply between April 15 and October 15. After October 15, the IRS may assess penalties for late FBAR filing. Indian account holders who have never filed should act before that date.

If you have Indian bank accounts and have never filed an FBAR, three compliance paths exist:

1. **Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures**: For non-willful filers, you must file six years of FBARs and three years of tax returns under [IRS Rev. Proc. 2014-55](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/streamlined-filing-compliance-procedures), with reduced or zero penalties.
2. **Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures**: If you have no unreported income and no active IRS examination, file past FBARs with a statement explaining the late filing. Penalties are often waived under IRS delinquent filing procedures.
3. **Voluntary Disclosure Practice**: For willful cases, the formal IRS program that reduces criminal exposure.

Many Indian-Americans missed prior years because they assumed NRE accounts were tax-exempt under US law. The NRE interest exemption is an Indian tax rule — it does not create a US FBAR reporting exemption. Act before the IRS contacts you. Penalty reduction programs close once an examination begins.

## FBAR vs. Form 8938 for Indian Accounts

Many US persons with Indian accounts must file both the FBAR and [IRS Form 8938](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-8938) under IRC 6038D. The two reports have different thresholds, different filing destinations, and different penalties. Filing one does not satisfy the other. NRIs with large fixed deposit portfolios, PPF accounts, and NRE savings accounts frequently exceed both thresholds.

| | FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) | Form 8938 |
|---|---|---|
| **Filed with** | FinCEN via BSA E-Filing | IRS (attached to tax return) |
| **Threshold (domestic, single)** | $10,000 aggregate | $50,000 on last day or $75,000 any time |
| **Threshold (domestic, joint)** | $10,000 aggregate | $100,000 on last day or $150,000 any time |
| **Threshold (expat, single)** | $10,000 aggregate | $200,000 on last day or $300,000 any time |
| **Covers PPF?** | Yes | Yes |
| **Covers Demat accounts?** | Yes | Yes |
| **Non-willful penalty** | Up to $16,117 per violation ([31 USC 5321](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321)) | Up to $10,000 per failure ([26 USC 6038D](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D)) |

## Frequently Asked Questions About FBAR India NRE NRO Accounts

These frequently asked questions cover the most common FBAR India NRE NRO accounts issues. If your Indian bank accounts exceeded $10,000 in combined peak value at any point during the calendar year — whether NRE, NRO, FCNR, PPF, fixed deposits, or demat accounts — you must file the FBAR under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). The answers below address specific Indian account types and Indian-American situations.

**Do I report my NRE account on the FBAR if interest is tax-free in India?**

Yes. The NRE account interest exemption is an Indian income tax rule under the Income Tax Act, 1961. It has no effect on US FBAR reporting. Under [31 USC 5314](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5314) and [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350), the NRE account is a foreign financial account because a bank outside the United States holds it. The Indian tax treatment of the interest does not change your FBAR filing duty.

**Is a PPF account reportable if I cannot withdraw for 15 years?**

Yes. The 15-year lock-in period does not remove the FBAR obligation. Under [31 CFR 1010.350(e)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350), you must report accounts where you have a financial interest, even if access is restricted. The lock-in period is a withdrawal restriction, not a bar on financial interest. Report the full PPF balance each calendar year.

**What if my Indian accounts are in my spouse's name?**

If your spouse holds the account and you have no financial interest and no signature authority, you do not report it on your own FBAR. However, if you are a joint holder, or your spouse holds the account as your agent, you must report it. See our article on [FBAR joint account reporting rules](/blog/fbar-joint-account-reporting-rules) for how joint ownership works.

**Do I need to report an Indian account I closed during the year?**

Yes. If you held the account at any point during the calendar year and your aggregate foreign financial accounts exceeded $10,000, you must report the closed account. Use the maximum account value it held before you closed it. Closed accounts are reported the same way as open ones — enter the bank name, account number, and peak value.

**Can the India-US FATCA IGA affect my FBAR compliance?**

Yes. Under India's FATCA Model 1 IGA, Indian banks report US person account information to the CBDT, which shares it with the IRS. The Internal Revenue Service often holds records of your NRE, NRO, and FCNR account balances before you file anything. Not filing the FBAR when the IRS holds matching data from Indian banks increases your FBAR penalty risk.

**Are mutual funds in an Indian demat account separately reportable?**

No. You report mutual funds held inside a demat account as part of the total demat account value, not as separate line items. Report the demat account's peak total value — including all funds, stocks, and bonds within it. If you hold mutual funds directly through CAMS or KFintech outside a demat account, those are separate reportable foreign financial accounts.

**Does the DTAA between India and the US eliminate FBAR filing?**

No. The India-US DTAA (Double Tax Avoidance Agreement) applies to income tax only. The FBAR is a Bank Secrecy Act requirement enforced by the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network, not an income tax filing. DTAA treaty benefits — such as reduced withholding on NRO interest or foreign tax credits — have no effect on your FBAR reporting obligation for NRE, NRO, or other Indian accounts.

**Do I report Indian accounts held before I became a US person?**

You report Indian accounts for every calendar year in which you were a US person and the aggregate value of your foreign financial accounts exceeded $10,000. If you became a US citizen or received your green card on July 1, you are a US person for the remainder of that calendar year. Report the maximum account value of each Indian account from July 1 through December 31 of that year.

# FBAR Foreign Pension Accounts: What You Must Report

Many US persons with foreign pension and retirement accounts ask: does my pension need to go on the FBAR? The Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts (FBAR) — FinCEN Form 114 — covers foreign pension accounts in many cases.

The answer depends on the pension type, whether you hold a "financial interest" under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350), and whether the account carries a reportable balance. Foreign pension accounts are one of the most commonly overlooked foreign financial accounts for FBAR filing.

Under [31 USC 5314](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5314), any US person with a financial interest in foreign financial accounts with an aggregate value exceeding $10,000 at any point during the calendar year must file FinCEN Form 114. The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) administers this Bank Secrecy Act requirement.

Foreign pension FBAR reporting catches many US expats and green card holders off guard. Pension accounts at foreign financial institutions can easily exceed the $10,000 threshold when aggregated with other foreign financial accounts.

The key question is not whether an account is labeled a "pension." The question is whether that foreign retirement account constitutes a "financial account" at a foreign financial institution and whether you have a "financial interest" in it under [31 CFR 1010.350(e)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350).

## Who Must File an FBAR for Foreign Pension Accounts?

The FBAR filing requirement applies to every US person with a financial interest in or signature authority over foreign financial accounts exceeding $10,000 in aggregate value at any time during the calendar year. The Bank Secrecy Act defines "US person" broadly for FBAR purposes.

A US person includes any US citizen, US resident (including green card holders), and any domestic entity formed under US law. If you are a US citizen living abroad with foreign pension accounts, you are a US person subject to FBAR filing.

Green card holders are US persons for FBAR purposes regardless of where they live. A green card holder who returns to their home country must continue FBAR reporting for their foreign retirement accounts.

Dual citizens are also US persons. A dual US-Canadian citizen or dual US-UK citizen must report foreign pension accounts on the FBAR even if they have never lived in the United States. The FBAR obligation under 31 USC 5314 applies based on US person status, not residency.

## Does the Financial Interest Test Apply to FBAR Foreign Pension Accounts?

Yes, the financial interest test under 31 CFR 1010.350(e) applies to FBAR foreign pension accounts the same way it applies to bank or brokerage accounts. You have a financial interest if you are the owner of record or hold legal title, or if another person holds the account on your behalf.

For defined contribution pensions — where you own individual units or shares — you are the owner of record and have a financial interest reportable on the FBAR. The financial interest test is the critical threshold for determining whether a foreign pension account triggers FBAR filing.

### Defined Contribution vs. Defined Benefit Pensions

The distinction between defined contribution and defined benefit pensions is the most important factor in your FBAR analysis. These two pension plan structures create fundamentally different FBAR reporting obligations.

A **defined contribution pension** credits your account with specific contributions and investment returns. You own a measurable account balance.

Examples include UK Self-Invested Personal Pensions (SIPPs), UK workplace DC pensions, Australian superannuation funds, and Canadian RRSPs. You have a financial interest in a defined contribution pension plan, and the account is reportable once the $10,000 aggregate threshold is met under 31 CFR 1010.306.

A **defined benefit pension** pays a fixed monthly income based on salary and years of service. No individual account balance belongs to you. The employer bears the investment risk.

Most US tax practitioners hold that defined benefit pensions do not give rise to a reportable FBAR interest. There is no "maximum account value" to report because no personal account balance exists. See the [IRS FAQ on FBAR](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/report-of-foreign-bank-and-financial-accounts-fbar) for current guidance.

### Government Social Insurance Programs

Government-sponsored social insurance programs are not foreign financial accounts for FBAR purposes. These include:

- **UK State Pension** — a government benefit, not a financial institution account
- **Canada Pension Plan (CPP)** — a statutory government program
- **Australia Age Pension** — a means-tested government benefit
- **US Social Security equivalents abroad**
- **German statutory pension** — a government insurance program

None of these require FBAR reporting. You report government pension income for income tax purposes on your US tax return, but you do not report these programs on FinCEN Form 114.

The distinction matters because many countries have both government pension programs and private pension plans. Only the private pension accounts — held at foreign financial institutions — are potentially reportable on the FBAR.

## How to Determine If a Foreign Pension Is a Financial Account

The FBAR regulation at [31 CFR 1010.350(c)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350) defines "financial account" to include bank accounts, securities accounts, and other accounts. A foreign pension account qualifies if it is maintained by a foreign financial institution and you have an identifiable account balance.

### The Three-Part Test

Apply this test to each foreign pension account:

**Part 1: Is the account held at a foreign financial institution?** Most foreign pension providers qualify — any bank, securities firm, or insurance company holding financial assets outside the United States.

**Part 2: Do you have a financial interest?** Under 31 CFR 1010.350(e), you have a financial interest if you are the owner of record or hold legal title. For defined contribution retirement accounts, you are the owner.

**Part 3: Does the account have a determinable maximum account value?** Defined contribution pension plans have a determinable value. Defined benefit pension plans generally do not.

A foreign pension that fails any part is not reportable. A defined benefit pension fails Part 3. A government pension fails Part 1. Hybrid arrangements with both defined benefit and defined contribution components are analyzed separately — only the defined contribution portion is reportable.

## Types of Foreign Pension Accounts Subject to FBAR Reporting

Foreign pension accounts come in many forms across different countries. Understanding the common categories helps US persons determine their FBAR filing obligations for each type of foreign retirement account.

**Foreign government pensions** fall into two categories. Government social insurance programs — like the UK State Pension or Canada Pension Plan — are not reportable. However, some government employer pension plans maintain individual account balances. If a government employer pension plan has an individual account balance, it is reportable on the FBAR.

**Private employer pension plans** are the most common category requiring FBAR reporting. In defined contribution employer pension plans, you own the account balance at a foreign financial institution and have a financial interest under 31 CFR 1010.350(e). Private employer defined benefit pension plans, where no individual account balance exists, are generally not reportable.

**Self-directed foreign retirement accounts** — UK SIPPs, Canadian RRSPs, German Riester-Rente contracts — are almost always reportable. You have clear ownership and a financial interest in these foreign pension accounts. Self-directed foreign retirement accounts typically have the highest account values because they accumulate over many years.

## Country-Specific FBAR Rules for Pension Accounts

### UK Pensions and FBAR Reporting

If you have a UK SIPP or workplace DC pension, you must report it on your FBAR when your aggregate foreign financial account balances exceeded $10,000 at any time during the calendar year. Your account fund value is the "maximum account value" for FBAR purposes. Convert it to US dollars using the [Treasury's year-end exchange rates](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/reports-statements/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/current.html). See our [exchange rate article](/blog/fbar-exchange-rates-treasury-department).

UK defined benefit pensions — such as a final salary pension — are generally not reportable. However, if a DB scheme includes Additional Voluntary Contributions (AVC), that AVC sub-account is separately reportable as a foreign financial account. For UK accounts broadly, see [FBAR reporting for UK bank accounts](/blog/fbar-uk-bank-accounts).

### Australian Superannuation and FBAR

Australian superannuation (called "super") is a defined contribution retirement fund held at an Australian financial institution. Your employer contributes a percentage of your salary into your personal super account. You own that balance.

Super funds in accumulation phase are reportable on FBAR under 31 CFR 1010.350(e). The super fund provider is a foreign financial institution, and you have a financial interest in your individual retirement account.

If your super balance was AUD 180,000 at its peak and the Treasury rate for AUD on December 31 was 0.62, the reportable maximum account value is about $111,600 — well above the $10,000 threshold. For comprehensive coverage, see [FBAR reporting for Australian superannuation and bank accounts](/blog/fbar-australia-superannuation-bank-accounts).

### Canadian RRSP and RRIF Reporting

The Canadian Registered Retirement Savings Plan (RRSP) and its converted form, the Registered Retirement Income Fund (RRIF), are both reportable foreign financial accounts. You own the account and its balance at a Canadian financial institution.

The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network confirmed in 2014 that RRSPs and RRIFs are subject to FBAR reporting — even though the Canada-US tax treaty defers income tax on RRSP growth under Article XVIII. The FBAR filing obligation and income tax obligation are separate.

Many US persons mistakenly believe that treaty benefits eliminate their FBAR reporting duty. They do not. The Bank Secrecy Act requirement applies regardless of any tax treaty election.

The Canadian Tax-Free Savings Account (TFSA) is also reportable. It is a foreign financial account at a Canadian financial institution with a balance you own. See [FBAR reporting for Canadian RRSP and TFSA accounts](/blog/fbar-canada-rrsp-tfsa-reporting).

### German Riester and Rurup Pension Plans

German Riester-Rente and Rurup-Rente (Basisrente) are individual retirement account contracts at German financial institutions. The Riester-Rente is a defined contribution retirement plan with government subsidies — you have a financial interest and must include it in your FBAR filing when the $10,000 aggregate threshold is met.

The Rurup-Rente has an accumulation phase with a cash surrender value that serves as the maximum account value for FBAR reporting.

### Israeli Pension Funds and Provident Funds

Israeli Kupat Gemel (provident funds) are defined contribution accounts with an identifiable balance, reportable under 31 CFR 1010.350(e). Some Israeli pension arrangements include both defined benefit and defined contribution components — analyze each separately for FBAR filing.

## How Do You Calculate Maximum Account Value for Foreign Pensions?

For each reportable FBAR foreign pension account, you must determine the maximum account value during the calendar year. FinCEN Form 114 asks for the maximum value, not the year-end value.

1. **Find the highest account balance** at any point during the reporting year. Check your pension statements — the peak may occur mid-year.
2. **Convert to US dollars** using the Treasury year-end exchange rate for December 31. See [how to calculate maximum account value for FBAR](/blog/how-to-calculate-maximum-account-value-fbar).
3. **Aggregate all foreign financial accounts**. If your UK SIPP is $45,000 and your Canadian RRSP is $30,000, your aggregate value is $75,000. Both are reportable.
4. **File FinCEN Form 114** through BSA E-Filing by April 15, with an automatic extension to October 15.

### Present Value vs Cash Surrender Value

Some foreign pension accounts have both a present value and a cash surrender value. For defined contribution pension plans, the maximum account value is the highest balance on your statements during the calendar year.

For annuity contracts or insurance-based foreign pension accounts, use the cash surrender value — the amount you would receive if you terminated the contract. Pension accounts without a determinable value, like pure defined benefit pensions, fall outside the FBAR reporting requirement.

## FBAR vs FATCA for Foreign Retirement Accounts

US persons with foreign pension accounts often face overlapping reporting requirements. Both the FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) and FATCA (Form 8938) may apply to the same foreign retirement accounts.

The FBAR is a Bank Secrecy Act requirement administered by the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network. Form 8938 is an Internal Revenue Service requirement under the Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act. They are separate obligations filed with different agencies.

The FBAR threshold is $10,000 in aggregate value across all foreign financial accounts at any time during the calendar year. The Form 8938 threshold starts at $50,000 on the last day of the year or $75,000 at any time for single filers in the US under [26 USC 6038D](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D). Thresholds are higher for joint filers and taxpayers abroad ($200,000/$300,000 for single, $400,000/$600,000 for joint).

Your foreign pension account may require FBAR reporting even if it falls below the FATCA threshold. FBAR penalties under 31 USC 5321 can reach $16,117 per account for non-willful violations. Form 8938 penalties start at $10,000 for failure to file. Both penalty regimes can apply to the same unreported foreign pension account.

For a detailed comparison, see [FBAR vs. FATCA Form 8938 differences](/blog/fbar-vs-fatca-form-8938-differences).

## Tax Treaty Considerations for Foreign Pension Accounts

US tax treaties contain provisions for foreign pension accounts that affect income tax — but not FBAR filing. The FBAR is a Bank Secrecy Act requirement under 31 USC 5314. Tax treaties are income tax agreements. The two legal frameworks are separate.

A US person who elects treaty benefits for a Canadian RRSP under the Canada-US tax treaty (Article XVIII) still must report the RRSP on FinCEN Form 114. The US-UK tax treaty addresses UK pension contributions and distributions in Article 18, but UK pension accounts remain reportable on the FBAR.

The US-Australia tax treaty does not include a specific provision for superannuation deferral. Australian super growth may be currently taxable for US persons. These treaty provisions affect your income tax return — they do not change FBAR filing obligations for foreign pension accounts.

## Common Mistakes When Reporting Foreign Pensions on FBAR

### Mistake 1: Assuming Treaty Benefits Eliminate FBAR Filing

Tax treaty exemptions affect income tax, not FBAR reporting. A Canadian RRSP with treaty deferral is still reportable on FinCEN Form 114. The Bank Secrecy Act operates independently of income tax law.

### Mistake 2: Not Reporting Defined Contribution Pensions

While defined benefit pensions are generally not reportable, defined contribution pension plans — where you own an account balance — are foreign financial accounts subject to FBAR reporting regardless of what the account is called.

### Mistake 3: Using the Wrong Exchange Rate

The FBAR requires the Treasury year-end exchange rate for December 31. Some filers use the rate on the date of the highest balance or a commercial rate. Only the Treasury rate is acceptable. See [FBAR exchange rates and Treasury rates](/blog/fbar-exchange-rates-treasury-department).

### Mistake 4: Forgetting to Aggregate Foreign Accounts

The $10,000 threshold applies to aggregate value across all foreign financial accounts. If your foreign pension account holds $6,000 and your foreign bank account holds $5,000, your aggregate value is $11,000 and both are reportable.

### Mistake 5: Reporting Year-End Value Instead of Maximum Value

FinCEN Form 114 asks for maximum account value during the calendar year, not year-end balance. If your foreign pension account peaked at $85,000 in June but dropped to $72,000 by December, report $85,000.

### Mistake 6: Ignoring Foreign Pension Accounts After Leaving a Country

A foreign pension account remains reportable even after you leave the country. Your UK SIPP is still a foreign financial account on the FBAR each calendar year as long as you are a US person.

## Foreign Pension Accounts vs US Retirement Accounts

US retirement accounts — IRAs, 401(k) plans, Roth IRAs — are not reported on the FBAR because they are domestic accounts. The Bank Secrecy Act requires reporting of foreign financial accounts to detect money laundering and tax evasion. Foreign pension accounts are not visible to the Internal Revenue Service through domestic reporting, so the FBAR fills this gap.

Foreign pension accounts also lack equivalent US tax treatment. Without a tax treaty, contributions may not be deductible and growth may be currently taxable — creating both FBAR filing obligations and income tax complexity.

## Real-World Scenarios

### Scenario 1: Michael in London, UK SIPP and DB Pension

Michael is a US citizen working in London. His UK SIPP is worth GBP 85,000 — about $107,000 at the Treasury December 31 rate of 1.26. He also participates in his employer's defined benefit pension and holds a UK bank account with $3,000 equivalent.

Michael reports his SIPP and bank account on the FBAR. The aggregate value is about $110,000. He does not report his defined benefit pension — no personal account balance exists. His foreign financial assets also exceed $200,000, so he files Form 8938.

### Scenario 2: Priya in Sydney, Australian Superannuation

Priya is a US green card holder who lived in Australia before returning to the US. She retains her Australian super fund with a balance of AUD 145,000 — about $91,350 at the Treasury December 31 rate.

Priya reports her super fund on the FBAR each calendar year as a US person. The account is still a foreign financial account even though she is back in the US and the fund is accumulating without active management.

### Scenario 3: David in Toronto, RRSP and DB Pension

David is a dual US-Canadian citizen. His RRSP is worth CAD 220,000 — about $163,000 at the December 31 rate. His Canadian government defined benefit pension has no personal account balance.

David reports his RRSP on the FBAR. The defined benefit pension is not reportable. David has elected treaty benefits under Article XVIII, but this defers income tax only — his FBAR filing requirement remains.

### Scenario 4: Katrin in Berlin, German Riester and Bank Account

Katrin is a US citizen who worked in Germany before relocating. Her Riester-Rente has EUR 42,000 and her German bank account EUR 8,000. At the December 31 rate of 1.04, her aggregate foreign financial accounts total about $52,000. Both are reportable on FinCEN Form 114.

## What Penalties Apply for Failing to Report Foreign Pension Accounts?

Failing to report a foreign pension account on the FBAR can result in significant penalties under [31 USC 5321](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network and the Internal Revenue Service enforce FBAR penalties. The amount depends on whether the failure was willful or non-willful.

A **non-willful penalty** can reach $16,117 per account per year under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B) (2025 inflation-adjusted figure). A US person who did not know about the FBAR filing requirement for their foreign pension accounts may face non-willful penalties.

A **willful penalty** is the greater of $100,000 or 50% of the account balance per year under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C). For foreign pension accounts with large balances, willful FBAR penalties can be devastating.

Penalties multiply across unreported years. For a full breakdown, see [FBAR penalties: what happens if you don't file](/blog/fbar-penalties-what-happens-if-you-dont-file).

If you missed prior years of FBAR filing for foreign pension accounts, you may qualify for the [Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures](/blog/fbar-streamlined-filing-compliance-procedures) or [Delinquent FBAR submission procedures](/blog/fbar-delinquent-filing-procedures). These programs can reduce or eliminate FBAR penalties for non-willful failures to report foreign retirement accounts.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I need to report my UK SIPP on the FBAR?**

Yes. A UK SIPP is a defined contribution foreign pension account at a UK financial institution. You own the account balance, meeting the financial interest test under 31 CFR 1010.350(e). If your aggregate foreign financial account balances exceeded $10,000 during the calendar year, report the SIPP on FinCEN Form 114.

**Is a defined benefit pension reportable on FBAR?**

Generally no. A defined benefit pension pays a fixed income with no individual account balance. There is no maximum account value for FBAR purposes. However, a separate defined contribution component — like an AVC sub-account — is reportable.

**Do I report my Canadian RRSP if I have a tax treaty deferral?**

Yes. The Canada-US tax treaty defers income tax on RRSP growth under Article XVIII, but does not remove the FBAR filing obligation. The FBAR is a Bank Secrecy Act requirement under 31 USC 5314, separate from income tax. See [FBAR Canada RRSP and TFSA reporting](/blog/fbar-canada-rrsp-tfsa-reporting).

**Is Australian superannuation a foreign financial account for FBAR?**

Yes. An accumulation-phase super fund is a defined contribution retirement account at an Australian financial institution. You own the balance and have a financial interest under 31 CFR 1010.350(e). See [FBAR Australia superannuation and bank accounts](/blog/fbar-australia-superannuation-bank-accounts).

**Are foreign pensions reportable on Form 8938 as well as FBAR?**

Yes, in most cases. Foreign pension accounts reportable on the FBAR are also reportable on Form 8938 (FATCA) when they exceed the thresholds under 26 USC 6038D. The FBAR and Form 8938 are separate forms with separate thresholds filed with different agencies.

**What exchange rate do I use to value my foreign pension on the FBAR?**

Use the Treasury year-end exchange rate for December 31 of the reporting year from the [Treasury Fiscal Service](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/reports-statements/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/current.html). Even if your foreign pension account peaked mid-year, convert using the December 31 rate. See [FBAR exchange rates and Treasury rates](/blog/fbar-exchange-rates-treasury-department).

**What if my pension balance is below $10,000?**

You must aggregate all foreign financial accounts under 31 CFR 1010.306. If your UK SIPP holds $6,000 and your UK bank account holds $5,000, the aggregate is $11,000 — above the $10,000 threshold. Both must be reported on FinCEN Form 114.

**Can I file a late FBAR for foreign pension accounts I forgot to report?**

Yes. The delinquent FBAR submission procedures or Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures may reduce or eliminate FBAR penalties for non-willful failures. Consult a tax professional for your situation.

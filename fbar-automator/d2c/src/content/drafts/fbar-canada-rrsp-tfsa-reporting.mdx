# FBAR Canada RRSP TFSA Reporting: What US Persons Must Know

About one million US citizens live in Canada. Millions more hold Canadian accounts — RRSP, TFSA, RRIF, or RESP — while living in the US.

All of these accounts are FBAR-reportable if your total foreign balances top $10,000 at any point during the calendar year.

The FBAR is the Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts (FinCEN Form 114). You file it under [31 USC 5314](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5314) and [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). The rules apply even if you report those accounts on your Canadian return to the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA).

The FBAR is a US Treasury Department duty — separate from Canadian law. It is administered by the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN), not the IRS.

Canadian registered accounts add complexity to US filing. The Registered Retirement Savings Plan (RRSP) and RRIF benefit from treaty deferral under the US-Canada Tax Treaty. However, the Tax-Free Savings Account (TFSA) is fully taxable for US persons, despite being tax-free in Canada.

Additionally, the RESP may trigger Form 3520 as a potential foreign trust. Each account type has different rules, and getting them right matters.

## Do I Need to Report Canadian RRSP on FBAR?

Yes. Your Canadian RRSP is a foreign financial account under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). You must report it on the FBAR if your aggregate foreign account balances top $10,000 at any point during the calendar year.

The US-Canada Tax Treaty defers US income tax on RRSP gains — but the treaty does not eliminate the FBAR obligation under [31 USC 5314](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5314). FBAR reporting is required under the Bank Secrecy Act regardless of any tax treaty provision.

These foreign financial accounts require FBAR reporting:

- **RRSP** (Registered Retirement Savings Plan) — tax-deferred Canadian retirement account
- **RRIF** (Registered Retirement Income Fund) — the drawdown phase of an RRSP
- **TFSA** (Tax-Free Savings Account) — Canadian tax-free account, but NOT US tax-free
- **RESP** (Registered Education Savings Plan) — may also require Form 3520
- **LIRA** (Locked-In Retirement Account) — locked-in RRSP funds from a pension plan transfer
- **Standard Canadian chequing, savings, and investment accounts** at Canadian banks

### What Makes a Canadian Account Reportable?

A "financial account" under the Bank Secrecy Act (BSA) includes bank accounts and brokerage accounts where you have a financial interest or signature authority. The FBAR instructions (FinCEN Form 114 instructions) define "financial interest" broadly.

It includes any account you own, control, or from which you receive benefit.

Canadian registered accounts qualify because a Canadian financial institution — RBC, TD Bank, Scotiabank, BMO, or CIBC — holds them. Canadian law may treat these accounts differently for tax purposes, but the BSA definition controls for FBAR filing.

The $10,000 threshold under [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306) is an aggregate value test. If your RRSP holds CAD $8,000 and your TFSA holds CAD $4,000, you must report both — even though each account is individually below $10,000.

Convert to USD using the [Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/reports-statements/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/current.html) for December 31 of the calendar year.

### Accounts That Are NOT Reportable

The FBAR covers foreign financial accounts — not all Canadian programs. These are generally not reportable on their own:

- Canada Pension Plan (CPP) — a government pension program, not a financial account
- Old Age Security (OAS) — a government benefit, not an account you hold
- Provincial pension plans — generally not held at a financial institution in your name

If you are unsure whether a specific account qualifies, treat it as reportable. Consult a cross-border tax professional before leaving it off your FBAR filing.

## Types of Canadian RRSP Accounts and FBAR Treatment

Not all RRSPs are the same. The Canada Revenue Agency recognizes several types of Registered Retirement Savings Plans. Each type is a foreign financial account for FBAR purposes, but understanding the differences helps with accurate reporting.

### Individual RRSP

This is the standard RRSP that most Canadians hold. You open it at a Canadian financial institution, make contributions, and the CRA grants tax-deferred treatment on the growth.

For US persons, the individual RRSP qualifies for treaty deferral under Article XVIII of the US-Canada Tax Treaty. Report it on your FBAR with the maximum account value during the calendar year.

### Group RRSP

A Group RRSP is an employer-sponsored plan where your employer sets up the RRSP through a financial institution on your behalf. Contributions may be made by you, your employer, or both.

For FBAR reporting purposes, a Group RRSP is treated the same as an individual RRSP. You have a financial interest in the account because the funds belong to you. Report the maximum account value in USD on FinCEN Form 114.

The treaty deferral under the US-Canada Tax Treaty applies to Group RRSPs. The plan type does not change the Bank Secrecy Act reporting obligation.

### Spousal RRSP

A Spousal RRSP is an account where one spouse contributes to the other spouse's RRSP. The contributing spouse gets the CRA tax deduction. The account-holding spouse owns the funds.

For FBAR filing, the account-holding spouse reports the Spousal RRSP on their FBAR. The contributing spouse does not report it unless they also have signature authority over the account.

If both spouses are US persons, each files their own FBAR. The spouse who holds the Spousal RRSP includes it in their aggregate value calculation.

### Locked-In RRSP and LIRA

A Locked-In Retirement Account (LIRA) holds pension funds transferred from an employer pension plan. Provincial rules govern when you can access these funds.

A LIRA is FBAR-reportable as a foreign financial account. Treaty deferral applies under Article XVIII of the US-Canada Tax Treaty. Report the LIRA's maximum account value during the calendar year on FinCEN Form 114.

Some provinces use different names — Locked-In RRSP, LRSP, or LIF (Life Income Fund). All are reportable on the FBAR.

## How to Report Canadian TFSA on US Tax Return

The Tax-Free Savings Account is tax-free in Canada — but US persons must report and pay tax on all TFSA income annually. The US does not recognize Canada's tax-free treatment.

Income inside a TFSA is taxable to the US holder in the year earned. Report it on your Form 1040 each year, regardless of withdrawals.

### Why the TFSA Gets No Treaty Deferral

The US-Canada Tax Treaty (Article XVIII) grants deferral to RRSPs and RRIFs. It does not extend to TFSAs.

The IRS has not issued guidance giving treaty-based deferral to Tax-Free Savings Accounts. This is a common mistake among dual US-Canada citizens. The TFSA was created in 2009, after the treaty was negotiated.

Because the TFSA has no treaty protection, a US person pays US tax on interest, dividends, and capital gains earned inside the account each calendar year.

### TFSA Filing Requirements for US Persons

Here is what you must do with a TFSA as a US person:

- **Report the TFSA on your FBAR** each year you meet the $10,000 aggregate threshold
- **Report interest on Schedule B** of your Form 1040 each year
- **Report dividends on Schedule B** of your Form 1040 each year
- **Report capital gains on Schedule D** of your Form 1040 each year
- **Consider Form 3520** if the TFSA is structured as a trust arrangement
- **Report on Form 8938** if your foreign financial accounts exceed FATCA thresholds

Contributing to a TFSA is not tax-efficient for US persons. The tax-free growth that Canadians enjoy does not carry over to US tax law. Consider this before adding more to a Tax-Free Savings Account.

## RRSP vs TFSA: Key FBAR Reporting Differences

Both the RRSP and the TFSA are foreign financial accounts for FBAR purposes. Both must be reported on FinCEN Form 114. However, their US tax treatment differs significantly.

| Feature | RRSP | TFSA |
|---------|------|------|
| FBAR reportable | Yes | Yes |
| US-Canada Tax Treaty deferral | Yes (Article XVIII) | No |
| US tax on annual gains | Deferred until withdrawal | Taxed annually |
| Form 8938 (FATCA) reportable | Yes, if over threshold | Yes, if over threshold |
| Form 3520 risk | No | Possible (trust classification) |
| CRA tax treatment | Tax-deferred growth | Tax-free growth |
| US equivalent concept | Traditional IRA | Roth IRA (but no US recognition) |

### Why This Distinction Matters

The RRSP's treaty deferral means a US person does not owe US income tax on RRSP growth until withdrawal. This aligns the US and Canadian tax treatment during the accumulation phase.

The TFSA has no such protection. A US citizen or US resident with a TFSA pays US income tax on every dollar of interest, dividends, and capital gains earned inside the account — even though the Canada Revenue Agency imposes no Canadian tax.

This creates double-filing complexity. You report TFSA income to both the CRA (as tax-free) and to the IRS (as taxable). The FBAR reporting obligation applies equally to both account types.

## CRA Reporting vs FBAR Reporting

US persons with Canadian accounts often file with both the Canada Revenue Agency and the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network. These are separate obligations with different rules.

### What You Report to the CRA

The CRA tracks your Canadian income, RRSP contributions and withdrawals, TFSA transactions, and other Canadian tax obligations. Canadian residents file a T1 return annually.

If you are a Canadian resident and a US person, you file both a Canadian T1 and a US Form 1040.

### What You Report on the FBAR

The FBAR is filed with FinCEN — not the IRS and not the CRA. You report all foreign bank accounts and foreign financial accounts where you hold a financial interest or signature authority.

The FBAR is filed electronically through the BSA E-Filing System at the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network. The deadline is April 15 with an automatic extension to October 15.

### Key Differences Between CRA and FBAR

| Feature | CRA Reporting | FBAR Reporting |
|---------|--------------|----------------|
| Filed with | Canada Revenue Agency | FinCEN (US Treasury Department) |
| Filing system | CRA My Account / tax software | BSA E-Filing System |
| What you report | Canadian income and deductions | Foreign financial accounts |
| Threshold | Canadian tax filing thresholds | $10,000 aggregate value |
| Currency | Canadian dollars | US dollars |
| Penalties for non-filing | CRA penalties under ITA | Up to $16,117 per account per year (non-willful) |
| Exchange rate used | CRA annual average rate | Treasury Department December 31 rate |

Filing with the CRA does not satisfy your FBAR obligation. Filing an FBAR does not satisfy your CRA obligation. Each requires a separate filing through a separate system.

## How Does the US-Canada Tax Treaty Affect RRSP Reporting?

The US-Canada Tax Treaty (Article XVIII) lets US persons defer US income tax on gains inside an RRSP or RRIF. You do not pay US tax on interest, dividends, or capital gains that accumulate inside the plan. You pay tax only when you withdraw.

This is the treaty deferral.

Before 2014, you had to file Form 8891 each year for this deferral. The IRS dropped Form 8891 via [Rev. Proc. 2014-55](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2014-44_IRB#RP-2014-55). The election is now automatic. You do not need any form to claim deferral on your RRSP or RRIF.

### What the Treaty Deferral Covers

The deferral applies to income and gains inside the RRSP or RRIF. You defer these until distribution, but you still must:

1. Report the RRSP or RRIF on your FBAR each year the aggregate threshold is met
2. File your US income tax return (Form 1040) each year
3. Report RRSP or RRIF distributions as ordinary income in the year you receive them
4. File Form 8938 (FATCA) if your RRSP and other foreign assets exceed the FATCA thresholds under [26 USC 6038D](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D) — Form 8938 is separate from FBAR Form 114

The treaty deferral does NOT eliminate the FBAR obligation. The FBAR is a Bank Secrecy Act requirement — no tax treaty overrides the BSA.

You file FBAR FinCEN Form 114 and claim the treaty deferral separately. For more on how FBAR and FATCA differ, see our guide to [FBAR vs Form 8938 differences](/blog/fbar-vs-fatca-form-8938-differences).

### RRIF Reporting After Conversion

At age 71, Canadians convert their RRSP to an RRIF. The RRIF replaces the RRSP on your FBAR. It is a new account with its own balance and its own line on FinCEN Form 114.

Treaty deferral still applies inside the RRIF. Your annual minimum draws are taxable in the US in the year paid.

Canada's 15% withholding tax on RRIF draws may offset your US tax bill through the foreign tax credit.

## FBAR Canada RRSP TFSA Maximum Value Calculation

The FBAR requires the maximum account value of each foreign financial account during the calendar year — not the year-end balance. Find the highest balance at any point during the year. Then convert to USD.

### How to Determine Maximum Account Value

Here is the process for calculating maximum account value:

1. **Find the highest balance** in each account during the calendar year. For an RRSP with market fluctuations, check quarterly or monthly statements.
2. **Apply the December 31 Treasury rate** for CAD to USD currency conversion. Use the [Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/reports-statements/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/current.html) published by the Treasury Department each quarter-end.
3. **Convert each maximum CAD balance to USD** using that exchange rate.
4. **Sum all converted USD values** across all foreign bank accounts and foreign financial accounts.
5. **If the aggregate value tops $10,000**, file the FBAR and report each account on FinCEN Form 114.

### Example: CAD to USD Conversion Table

Suppose the December 31 Treasury Department exchange rate for CAD is 0.72 (1 CAD = $0.72 USD).

| Account | Maximum CAD Balance | USD Equivalent |
|---------|---------------------|----------------|
| RBC RRSP | CAD 85,000 | USD 61,200 |
| TD TFSA | CAD 22,000 | USD 15,840 |
| Scotiabank chequing | CAD 6,500 | USD 4,680 |
| **Total aggregate value** | | **USD 81,720** |

All three accounts are above $10,000 in aggregate. Report each on a separate line of FinCEN Form 114. Include the maximum account value in USD, the account number, and the financial institution's name and address.

### RRSP Maximum Value and Market Fluctuations

RRSP and RRIF accounts often hold equities, mutual funds, or ETFs. The maximum account value is the highest total market value during the calendar year — not the cost basis.

If your RRSP reached $100,000 USD-equivalent in February but fell to $75,000 by December 31, you report $100,000 as the maximum value.

Keep monthly or quarterly statements from your Canadian custodian (RBC, TD, BMO, Scotiabank, CIBC, Questrade, or Wealthsimple). These show balance history, which makes the maximum value calculation straightforward.

For more on the exchange rate method, see our guide to [FBAR exchange rates and Treasury Department rates](/blog/fbar-exchange-rates-treasury-department). For the full maximum value walkthrough, see [how to calculate maximum account value for FBAR](/blog/how-to-calculate-maximum-account-value-fbar).

### Currency Conversion for Multiple Account Types

If you hold several types of Canadian accounts — an RRSP at one institution, a TFSA at another, and a chequing account at a third — each account's maximum value is converted separately.

Use the same Treasury Department December 31 exchange rate for all Canadian dollar accounts. Do not use the Bank of Canada rate or the CRA annual average rate. The FBAR specifically requires the Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange.

If an account holds investments denominated in a currency other than CAD (for example, US-dollar holdings inside a Canadian RRSP), convert the non-CAD portion at the appropriate Treasury rate for that currency.

## Step-by-Step Guide to Filing FBAR for Canadian Accounts

Filing an FBAR for Canadian accounts follows the same general process as any FBAR filing. Here is a step-by-step walkthrough for US persons with Canadian RRSP, TFSA, or other foreign bank accounts.

### Step 1: Gather Your Canadian Account Statements

Collect year-end statements and any quarterly or monthly statements from every Canadian financial institution where you hold accounts. You need these to determine the maximum account value during the calendar year.

Gather statements for all foreign financial accounts — RRSP, TFSA, RRIF, RESP, LIRA, chequing, savings, and investment accounts.

### Step 2: Calculate the Maximum Value for Each Account

Review each statement and identify the highest balance during the calendar year. For accounts holding investments, the highest market value may occur mid-year rather than at year-end.

### Step 3: Convert to USD Using the Treasury Rate

Apply the December 31 Treasury Department exchange rate to each maximum CAD balance. The [Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/reports-statements/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/current.html) are the only acceptable rates for FBAR currency conversion.

### Step 4: Determine if You Meet the $10,000 Threshold

Sum the maximum USD values of all your foreign bank accounts and foreign financial accounts worldwide — not just Canadian accounts. If the aggregate value exceeds $10,000 at any point during the calendar year, you must file.

### Step 5: File FinCEN Form 114 Through the BSA E-Filing System

File electronically through the BSA E-Filing System at FinCEN.gov. The FBAR cannot be filed on paper. Enter each foreign financial account on a separate line with the account number, maximum account value in USD, the financial institution name, and the institution address.

The FBAR deadline is April 15 with an automatic extension to October 15. No extension request form is needed.

### Step 6: Keep Records for Six Years

The Bank Secrecy Act requires you to retain FBAR records for at least five years from the due date of the report, per [31 CFR 1010.420](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.420). Keep your Canadian account statements, currency conversion calculations, and a copy of your filed FBAR.

## What About the RESP?

The RESP is a Registered Education Savings Plan used by Canadian families to save for post-secondary education. It is FBAR-reportable as a foreign financial account.

Beyond the FBAR, it may also require Form 3520 and Form 3520-A if the IRS treats it as a foreign trust under IRC 6048.

If the RESP functions as a trust — with a subscriber (the contributor) and a beneficiary (the student) — the trust filing rules may apply. Failure to file Form 3520 carries a penalty of the greater of $10,000 or 35% of the gross reportable amount under IRC 6048.

The IRS has not issued clear guidance on RESP classification, so this area carries real risk.

US persons contributing to an RESP should work with a cross-border tax professional. See our article on [FBAR vs Form 3520 for foreign trusts](/blog/fbar-vs-form-3520-foreign-trusts) for background on foreign trust reporting.

## Common FBAR Filing Mistakes With Canadian Accounts

US persons with Canadian foreign financial accounts frequently make mistakes on their FBAR filing. Here are the most common errors and how to avoid them.

### Mistake 1: Assuming Treaty Deferral Eliminates the FBAR

The US-Canada Tax Treaty defers US income tax on RRSP and RRIF income. It does not eliminate the FBAR filing obligation. The FBAR is a Bank Secrecy Act requirement administered by the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network. No tax treaty overrides the BSA.

### Mistake 2: Treating the TFSA as Tax-Free in the US

The Tax-Free Savings Account is tax-free only in Canada. US persons owe US income tax on all TFSA earnings annually. Failing to report TFSA income on your Form 1040 can create additional IRS exposure beyond the FBAR penalty.

### Mistake 3: Using the Wrong Exchange Rate

The FBAR requires the Treasury Department December 31 exchange rate — not the Bank of Canada rate, not the CRA annual average rate, and not the rate from your bank statement. Using the wrong exchange rate can lead to an incorrect aggregate value calculation.

### Mistake 4: Reporting Year-End Balance Instead of Maximum Value

The FBAR asks for the maximum account value during the calendar year, not the December 31 balance. For accounts with market fluctuations — especially RRSPs holding equities or mutual funds — the maximum value may be significantly higher than the year-end balance.

### Mistake 5: Forgetting to Report All Account Types

Some US persons report their RRSP but forget their TFSA, RESP, LIRA, or standard chequing and savings accounts. All foreign bank accounts and foreign financial accounts count toward the $10,000 aggregate threshold.

### Mistake 6: Missing the Filing Deadline

The FBAR deadline is April 15 with an automatic extension to October 15. This deadline is separate from your tax return deadline. Filing late without reasonable cause exposes you to non-willful penalties under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321).

## Real-World Scenarios for US-Canada Dual Filers

### Scenario 1: Dual Citizen in Toronto With RRSP and TFSA

Sarah is a US-Canadian dual citizen living in Toronto. She holds:

- RBC RRSP: maximum balance CAD $120,000 during the year
- TD TFSA: maximum balance CAD $35,000 during the year
- RBC chequing: maximum balance CAD $8,000 during the year

At the December 31 Treasury Department exchange rate of 0.73 CAD/USD:

- RRSP: CAD $120,000 x 0.73 = USD $87,600
- TFSA: CAD $35,000 x 0.73 = USD $25,550
- Chequing: CAD $8,000 x 0.73 = USD $5,840
- Aggregate value: USD $118,990

Sarah must file the FBAR under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350), reporting all three foreign financial accounts. The US-Canada Tax Treaty (Article XVIII) defers US tax on her RRSP income. Her TFSA earnings are taxable on her US return each year.

She also needs Form 8938 (FATCA) because her foreign assets exceed $200,000 on the last day of the year under [26 USC 6038D](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D). She files her FBAR through the BSA E-Filing System and her Form 8938 with her Form 1040.

### Scenario 2: US Resident With a Canadian RRSP From Prior Employment

James worked in Canada for eight years before moving to Boston. He left his Registered Retirement Savings Plan at TD Wealth with a balance of CAD $95,000 (USD $69,350 at the 0.73 exchange rate). He has no other foreign accounts.

James must file the FBAR under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350) because his RRSP exceeds $10,000. He claims treaty deferral automatically — Form 8891 is gone (eliminated in 2014 per IRS [Rev. Proc. 2014-55](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2014-44_IRB#RP-2014-55)).

He can leave the RRSP in Canada and defer US tax until he withdraws. Distributions are taxable as ordinary income in the US. Canada's withholding tax may offset his US liability through the foreign tax credit.

### Scenario 3: New Arrival With Missed FBAR Filings

Maria moved from Ottawa to Chicago three years ago. She kept her RRSP, TFSA, and a RESP for her daughter. She did not know about the FBAR requirement. Her foreign financial accounts exceeded $10,000 in aggregate value each year.

Maria has three years of unfiled FBARs. The IRS Streamlined Domestic Offshore Procedures can resolve non-willful failures. The penalty is 5% of the highest aggregate balance rather than per-violation amounts.

## Penalties for Not Reporting Canadian Accounts on FBAR

Missing an FBAR for a Canadian RRSP, TFSA, or other foreign financial account carries the same FBAR penalties as any other unreported foreign bank account. The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network enforces these penalties under the Bank Secrecy Act.

### Non-Willful FBAR Penalties

Non-willful violations carry up to $16,117 per account per year under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). Non-willful means you did not know about the requirement or made a reasonable mistake.

### Willful FBAR Penalties

Willful violations carry the greater of $100,000 or 50% of the account balance under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C). Willful means you knew about the FBAR filing requirement and chose not to file, or showed reckless disregard.

### Criminal Penalties

Criminal violations under 31 USC 5322 carry up to $250,000 in fines and up to five years imprisonment.

### Penalty Summary Table

| Violation Type | Penalty | Citation |
|----------------|---------|---------|
| Non-willful | Up to $16,117 per violation (2025) | 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B) |
| Willful | Greater of $100,000 or 50% of account balance | 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C) |
| Criminal (willful) | Up to $250,000 fine and/or 5 years prison | 31 USC 5322 |

A violation is typically one account per year. Three unreported foreign financial accounts for two years equals six violations — up to $96,702 in non-willful penalties under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B). Willful violations carry far higher exposure.

The penalties differ substantially — see [willful vs non-willful FBAR penalties](/blog/fbar-willful-vs-non-willful-penalties) for a full breakdown.

If you have unreported Canadian accounts, act before the IRS contacts you. The Streamlined Programs and Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures both offer paths to compliance with reduced penalties. See [FBAR delinquent filing procedures](/blog/fbar-delinquent-filing-procedures) for options.

## Frequently Asked Questions

Here are the most frequently asked questions about FBAR Canada RRSP TFSA reporting. These cover Form 8891, Form 8938, financial interest rules, TFSA taxation, RESP Form 3520 obligations, and how to use FinCEN Form 114 for Canadian registered accounts.

**Do I need to report my Canadian RRSP on the FBAR?**

Yes. An RRSP held at a Canadian financial institution is a foreign financial account under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). You must report it if your aggregate foreign balances exceed $10,000 at any point during the calendar year.

The US-Canada Tax Treaty defers US income tax on RRSP gains, but the treaty does not remove the FBAR reporting obligation under [31 USC 5314](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5314).

**Is a Canadian TFSA taxable in the US?**

Yes. The Tax-Free Savings Account is tax-free in Canada, but US persons pay US income tax on TFSA earnings every year. The US-Canada Tax Treaty's deferral provision (Article XVIII) covers RRSPs and RRIFs — it does not extend to TFSAs.

You report TFSA interest on Schedule B, dividends on Schedule B, and capital gains on Schedule D of your Form 1040. The TFSA is also FBAR-reportable. See our [first-time filer guide](/blog/fbar-first-time-filer-guide) for an overview.

**Do I still need to file Form 8891 for my RRSP?**

No. The IRS eliminated Form 8891 in 2014 through [Rev. Proc. 2014-55](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2014-44_IRB#RP-2014-55). The treaty election for RRSP and RRIF deferral is now automatic.

You do not file any separate election form. You still report the accounts on your FBAR each year. Check Form 8938 obligations if your RRSP balance exceeds FATCA thresholds under 26 USC 6038D.

**Do I need to report my Canadian RESP on the FBAR?**

Yes. You must report the RESP on the FBAR as a foreign financial account. It may also require Form 3520 and Form 3520-A if the IRS treats it as a foreign trust.

Failure to file Form 3520 carries a penalty of the greater of $10,000 or 35% of the gross reportable amount under IRC 6048. Work with a cross-border tax specialist to assess your specific RESP arrangement.

**What exchange rate do I use for Canadian dollars on the FBAR?**

Use the December 31 [Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/reports-statements/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/current.html) published by the Treasury Department. Apply that exchange rate to the maximum account value of each foreign financial account during the calendar year — not the year-end balance.

See our [guide to FBAR exchange rates and Treasury Department rates](/blog/fbar-exchange-rates-treasury-department) for the full methodology.

**What if I have a LIRA?**

A LIRA (Locked-In Retirement Account) is FBAR-reportable. It is a type of locked-in RRSP that holds pension funds transferred from an employer plan.

The same treaty deferral that covers RRSPs applies to LIRAs under Article XVIII of the US-Canada Tax Treaty. Report it on the FBAR with its maximum USD account value for the calendar year.

**Does the CRA report my accounts to the IRS?**

Yes. Under the Canada-US Intergovernmental Agreement (IGA) implementing FATCA, Canadian financial institutions report US person account information to the Canada Revenue Agency. The CRA then exchanges this data with the IRS.

This means the IRS may already know about your Canadian foreign bank accounts. Filing your FBAR ensures you are in compliance before the IRS identifies unreported accounts through information exchange.

**Can I file my FBAR and tax return at the same time?**

The FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) and your US tax return (Form 1040) are filed through separate systems. The FBAR goes through the BSA E-Filing System at FinCEN.gov. Your tax return goes to the IRS.

Both have an April 15 deadline. The FBAR has an automatic extension to October 15. Your tax return extension must be filed on Form 4868. You can prepare and file both simultaneously, but they are separate filings to separate agencies.

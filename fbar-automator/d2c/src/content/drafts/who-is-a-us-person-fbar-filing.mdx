# Who Is a US Person FBAR Rules Apply To? Citizens, Residents, and Entities

Who is a US person FBAR rules apply to? That question determines whether you must file the Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts (FBAR), officially FinCEN Form 114. Under [31 CFR 1010.350(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350), the Bank Secrecy Act (BSA) defines "United States person" to include US citizens, US residents, and entities formed under US law.

The FBAR threshold is $10,000 aggregate across all foreign financial accounts at any point during the calendar year. If you meet that threshold and qualify as a US person under [31 USC 5314](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5314), you must file an FBAR with FinCEN per [IRS guidance](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/report-of-foreign-bank-and-financial-accounts-fbar).

Misclassifying your status can lead to penalties up to $16,117 per violation for non-willful failures under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321), and up to $100,000 or 50% of the account balance for willful violations under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321).

This article covers every category of US person under the BSA, explains the substantial presence test, and addresses special cases such as dual citizens, DACA recipients, and US territory residents.

## Who Is a US Person FBAR Requires Under 31 CFR 1010.350(b)?

A US person for FBAR purposes includes any US citizen, US resident, or domestic entity. Under [31 CFR 1010.350(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350), the regulation identifies four categories: citizens of the United States, residents of the United States, domestic partnerships, and domestic corporations or other entities formed under US state law. If you fall into any of these categories and your foreign financial accounts exceed $10,000, you must file FBAR Form 114.

| Category | Test | Key Authority |
|---|---|---|
| US citizen | Holds US citizenship (any US passport holder) | 31 CFR 1010.350(b)(1) |
| Green card holder | I-551 status (lawful permanent resident) | 31 CFR 1010.350(b)(2) |
| Resident alien | Substantial presence test under IRC 7701(b) | IRC 7701(b); 31 CFR 1010.350(b)(2) |
| Domestic entity | Formed or organized in any US state or territory | 31 CFR 1010.350(b)(3)-(4) |
| Non-resident alien | Does not qualify (unless substantial presence met) | 31 CFR 1010.350(b) |
| Foreign entity | Does not qualify | 31 CFR 1010.350(b) |

### US Citizens

Every US citizen must file the FBAR if their foreign financial accounts exceed $10,000. This rule applies regardless of where you live. A citizen who has never set foot in the United States still qualifies as a US person.

A dual citizen of the US and another country is also a US person. Living abroad or claiming a foreign domicile has no effect on your FBAR obligation. The BSA definition ties to citizenship, not to physical presence or tax residence.

Common situations where citizens may not realize they must file:

- Born abroad to one or two US citizen parents
- Dual nationals who grew up and live in another country
- Citizens who moved abroad decades ago
- Retirees living in Mexico, Panama, or Portugal on a US passport

If any of these applies to you and your foreign financial accounts exceed $10,000, you must file. For a step-by-step walkthrough, see our [FBAR first-time filer guide](/blog/fbar-first-time-filer-guide).

### Green Card Holders (Lawful Permanent Residents)

Green card holders — also called lawful permanent residents — are US persons under [31 CFR 1010.350(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). Your obligation starts the calendar year you receive the I-551 card. Even if you got your green card in December, the entire year's foreign financial accounts are reportable.

Conditional residents (holders of a two-year green card) are also US persons. The BSA does not separate conditional from unconditional permanent residents. You remain a US person until you formally surrender your status through Form I-407.

Letting your green card expire or living abroad does not end the obligation. For a detailed look at these rules, see our article on [FBAR for green card holders](/blog/fbar-green-card-holders).

## Are Resident Aliens Required to File the FBAR?

Yes. A resident alien who meets the substantial presence test under [IRC 7701(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/7701) is a US person for FBAR purposes. FinCEN incorporates the IRC 7701(b) definition by reference through [31 CFR 1010.350(b)(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). This rule applies to FBAR IRS reporting even when you have no green card. Resident alien status depends on the days you spend in the United States, not on your visa type.

### How the Substantial Presence Test Works

You meet the substantial presence test if you satisfy both of these conditions:

1. You are present in the United States for at least **31 days** during the current calendar year
2. Your total weighted presence over three years equals **183 days or more**

The weighted calculation:

- **Current year**: Count all days at **100%**
- **Prior year**: Count days at **1/3**
- **Two years ago**: Count days at **1/6**

For example: 150 days in 2025 (150) + 90 days in 2024 (30) + 60 days in 2023 (10) = 190 weighted days. This person meets substantial presence in 2025. They are a US person for FBAR in 2025.

A second example: 120 days in 2025 (120) + 90 days in 2024 (30) + 180 days in 2023 (30) = 180 weighted days. This falls short of 183, so this person does not meet substantial presence in 2025.

### Closer Connection Exception and Form 8840

You may avoid US resident alien status for income tax by filing [Form 8840](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-8840) (Closer Connection Exception Statement). This is an IRS income tax election. However, it does not remove your FBAR obligation.

The FBAR is a BSA requirement under FinCEN, not an income tax requirement. The closer connection exception changes your income tax status. It does not change your status under [31 CFR 1010.350(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350) for FBAR purposes. If you meet substantial presence, you are a US person for FBAR regardless of Form 8840.

### Tax Treaty Tie-Breaker Positions and FBAR

Some income tax treaties let dual residents claim residency in the treaty country. This is a treaty tie-breaker election. The election reduces your US income tax obligations. It does not remove your FBAR requirement.

The FBAR rests on the BSA under [31 USC 5314](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5314), not on income tax law. Tax treaty provisions govern income taxes. They do not override BSA reporting rules. If you meet substantial presence under IRC 7701(b), you are a US person for FBAR even if a treaty tie-breaker positions you as a non-US resident for income tax purposes.

## Who Is Considered a US Person for Tax Purposes Versus FBAR?

For income tax, a US person typically includes citizens and resident aliens under IRC 7701(a)(30). For FBAR, the definition under [31 CFR 1010.350(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350) is similar but operates independently. A treaty tie-breaker election can affect income tax residency but not FBAR status. Form 8840 can affect income tax status but not FBAR status.

The FBAR instructions and IRS guidance both confirm that the FBAR obligation follows the BSA definition, not the income tax definition. Additionally, FBAR Form PDF (FinCEN Form 114) requires a separate filing with FinCEN — not with the IRS.

Key differences between the two definitions:

- **Income tax**: Treaties and elections can override US resident status
- **FBAR**: BSA rules apply regardless of income tax elections or treaties
- **Filing destination**: FBAR goes to FinCEN; income tax returns go to the IRS
- **Form used**: FBAR uses FinCEN Form 114 (FBAR form), not any IRS income tax form

This distinction matters for anyone with a treaty tie-breaker position, a closer connection exception election, or dual citizenship. Your income tax position and your FBAR position may differ. See our [FBAR vs. FATCA comparison](/blog/fbar-vs-fatca-form-8938-differences) for more on the difference between BSA and income tax obligations.

## Are US Entities Required to File the FBAR?

Yes. Under [31 CFR 1010.350(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350), domestic corporations, partnerships, limited liability companies (LLCs), and trusts formed under US law are US persons. The entity must file if it holds a financial interest in or signature authority over foreign financial accounts exceeding $10,000. Formation under US law is the only test. Business activity can be entirely overseas.

### Corporations and LLCs

A corporation formed in Delaware, an LLC organized in Wyoming, or an S corporation formed in California — all are US persons. This applies even if the corporation conducts all its business in other countries. The FBAR instructions confirm that formation under any US state law makes an entity a US person.

A foreign subsidiary of a US corporation is not a US person because the subsidiary is a separate entity formed under foreign law. However, the US parent may have FBAR duties if it has signature authority over the subsidiary's foreign financial accounts.

### Partnerships and Trusts

A general partnership or limited partnership organized in any US state is a US person. The entity itself must file the FBAR for any foreign financial account it holds. Individual partners may have separate FBAR obligations if they personally hold or have authority over foreign financial accounts.

A trust formed under US law with a US trustee or grantor is also a US person under [31 CFR 1010.350(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). The trust's FBAR duty depends on financial interest and control. For details on entity reporting, see our article on [FBAR for business accounts and signatory authority](/blog/fbar-business-accounts-signatory-authority).

## What Types of Foreign Financial Accounts Require FBAR Reporting?

A US person must report foreign financial accounts when the aggregate maximum value exceeds $10,000 at any point during the calendar year. The Bank Secrecy Act and [31 CFR 1010.350(c)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350) define "foreign financial account" broadly. The account type matters less than where the financial institution is located.

Any account maintained at a financial institution physically located outside the United States qualifies as a foreign financial account. This includes both accounts you own outright and accounts over which you hold signature authority. The [FinCEN FBAR instructions](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/shared/FBAR_Line_Item_Filing_Instructions.pdf) provide the full list of covered account types.

### Bank Accounts and Savings Accounts

The most common type of foreign financial account subject to FBAR reporting is a foreign bank account. This includes checking accounts, savings accounts, and time-deposit accounts held at a foreign bank.

Foreign bank accounts held at any financial institution outside the United States must be reported if the aggregate balance across all your foreign bank accounts — combined with any other foreign financial accounts — exceeds $10,000. The balance of each foreign bank account is measured at its highest point during the calendar year, not the year-end balance.

A US person with a single foreign bank account that hit $12,000 at any point — even briefly — must file. A person with three foreign bank accounts each peaking at $4,000 must also file, because the $10,000 threshold applies to the aggregate of all foreign financial accounts.

### Securities and Brokerage Accounts

Foreign brokerage accounts and securities accounts are foreign financial accounts for FBAR purposes. If a US person holds stocks, bonds, or mutual funds through a foreign financial institution, the account value counts toward the $10,000 threshold.

A US person with a financial interest in a brokerage account at a foreign bank or foreign broker must include that account in the FBAR calculation. This applies even if the securities held in the account are US-listed stocks. The location of the financial institution — not the type of asset — determines whether the account is foreign.

### Mutual Funds and Insurance Policies

Foreign mutual funds are foreign financial accounts under [31 CFR 1010.350(c)(3)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). A US person with a financial interest in a fund that issues shares available to the general public and is registered with a foreign financial regulatory authority must include it on the FBAR.

Foreign life insurance policies with a cash value are also reportable foreign financial accounts. If a foreign financial institution issues a policy that accumulates cash surrender value, that policy is a financial account for FBAR purposes. The cash value — not the death benefit — is the relevant figure for the $10,000 threshold calculation.

### Foreign Pension and Retirement Accounts

Foreign pension accounts and foreign retirement accounts are generally reportable foreign financial accounts. A US person with a financial interest in a pension plan maintained by a foreign employer must report it if the aggregate value exceeds the threshold.

Some exceptions exist for government pension plans in treaty countries, but these exceptions are narrow. A US person with an employer-sponsored foreign pension should not assume an exemption applies without reviewing the specific plan structure. Our article on [FBAR for foreign pension and retirement accounts](/blog/fbar-foreign-pension-retirement-accounts) covers the rules in detail.

## Understanding Financial Interest and Signature Authority

Two separate triggers can create an FBAR obligation: financial interest and signature authority. A US person who meets either test must report the foreign financial account. Understanding both concepts is essential for anyone with access to foreign financial accounts — whether personally held or connected to an employer.

### What Counts as Financial Interest Under the BSA?

A US person has a financial interest in a foreign financial account under [31 CFR 1010.350(e)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350) when they are the owner of record, have legal title to the account, or own more than 50% of the entity that holds the account. Financial interest also extends to situations where a nominee or agent holds the account on your behalf.

The financial interest test captures indirect ownership. If you own 60% of a foreign corporation that holds a foreign bank account, you have a financial interest in that account. The financial interest concept ensures that FBAR obligations cannot be avoided by routing assets through intermediary entities.

A grantor trust is another common source of financial interest. If you transferred assets to a foreign trust and retain the power to revoke it, you have a financial interest in the trust's foreign financial accounts. The analysis focuses on economic substance, not legal title.

### Signature Authority Over Foreign Accounts

Signature authority means the authority — alone or with another — to control the disposition of assets in a foreign financial account. A US person with signature authority over a foreign financial account must file the FBAR even if they have no financial interest in the account.

The most common example is an employee authorized to sign on a company's foreign bank account. Under [31 CFR 1010.350(f)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350), an officer or employee who can direct how funds in the account are moved has signature authority. That person must file — unless an exception applies.

Exceptions to the signature authority filing requirement are limited. The primary exception covers officers and employees of publicly traded companies reporting to their employer under certain conditions. See [IRS Notice 2011-1](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-11-01.pdf) for details on that relief.

### Signature Authority Over Employer's Foreign Financial Account

A US person who has signature authority over an employer's foreign financial account faces FBAR reporting obligations distinct from their personal filings. The individual must file FinCEN Form 114 to report the employer's foreign financial account — even if the employer also files its own FBAR.

This rule catches many employees by surprise. A finance director authorized to wire funds from a company's foreign bank account in Germany holds signature authority over that foreign bank account. That director must file an individual FBAR listing the employer's foreign financial account.

The employer's foreign financial account and the employee's personal foreign financial accounts are reported on the same FinCEN Form 114. The form accommodates multiple accounts — both personal and employer-related. For a detailed look at employer account rules, see our article on [FBAR for business accounts and signatory authority](/blog/fbar-business-accounts-signatory-authority).

### Jointly Owned Foreign Financial Accounts

Jointly owned foreign financial accounts require each co-owner who qualifies as a US person to file a separate FBAR. Both account holders must report the full account balance — not just their share. The $10,000 threshold applies to each person individually based on the maximum value of all their foreign financial accounts combined.

For example, a married couple with jointly owned foreign financial accounts at a foreign bank must each file FinCEN Form 114. Each spouse reports the full balance of the jointly owned accounts on their individual FBAR. There is no "splitting" of jointly owned account balances between co-filers.

An exception exists for spouses. Under the FBAR spousal filing rules, a non-filer spouse can be included on the other spouse's FBAR, but only when specific conditions are met and both spouses sign. When in doubt, each spouse should file a separate FBAR reporting all jointly owned foreign financial accounts at their full value.

## The Role of Foreign Financial Institutions

The FBAR reporting obligation depends on where a financial institution is located, not on its nationality or the currency of the account. A financial institution is "foreign" for FBAR purposes when it is physically located outside the United States. The term financial institution covers banks, securities dealers, insurance companies, and other entities that hold financial assets for customers.

Foreign financial institutions that hold accounts for US persons are not directly responsible for FBAR filing — the US person is. However, many foreign financial institutions now collect US taxpayer identification numbers and report account data to the IRS under FATCA. That foreign institution reporting does not replace the US person's FBAR obligation.

### How Foreign Financial Institutions Are Identified on the FBAR

FinCEN Form 114 requires each filer to identify the foreign financial institution that maintains each reportable account. The form collects the institution's name, address, and country. For foreign bank accounts, filers enter the name and location of the foreign bank. For brokerage accounts, filers enter the name of the foreign broker-dealer or financial institution.

The financial institution's account number — as issued by the foreign financial institution — is also required. If the foreign financial institution does not assign a formal account number, the filer must enter a substitute identifier. Accuracy in identifying the financial institution is important because FinCEN uses this data to detect patterns in offshore financial activity.

### US Branches of Foreign Financial Institutions

A US branch of a foreign bank is a US financial institution for FBAR purposes. An account at the New York branch of a Swiss bank is not a foreign financial account. The physical location of the branch — within the United States — removes it from FBAR coverage.

Conversely, a foreign branch of a US bank is a foreign financial institution for FBAR purposes. An account at the London branch of a US bank is a foreign financial account. US persons holding accounts at foreign branches of US banks must include those accounts in their FBAR filing if the aggregate threshold is met.

## Filing the Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts

The Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts is filed electronically using FinCEN Form 114. The report of foreign bank and financial accounts must be submitted through the BSA E-Filing System — there is no paper filing option. Every US person required to file must use the electronic system directly or authorize a preparer to file on their behalf.

The report of foreign bank and financial accounts covers one calendar year per filing. A separate FinCEN Form 114 is required for each year in which the $10,000 threshold was exceeded and the filer qualifies as a US person. The deadline is April 15, with an automatic extension to October 15 under [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306).

The report of foreign bank and financial accounts requires filers to disclose the maximum value of each foreign financial account during the year. Values must be converted to US dollars using the [Treasury Department's official exchange rates](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/reports-statements/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/current.html) as of December 31 of the reporting year. For details on the conversion process, see our guide on [FBAR exchange rates](/blog/fbar-exchange-rates-treasury-department).

### How to File Through BSA E-Filing

The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) operates the BSA E-Filing System at [bsaefiling.fincen.treas.gov](https://bsaefiling.fincen.treas.gov). The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network requires all FBAR filers to use this system. Individual filers can create a free account and file FinCEN Form 114 directly.

Filing through the BSA E-Filing System requires a current browser and an active registration. The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network accepts filings year-round, not only during tax season. Delinquent FBARs for prior years are also filed through the same system using the appropriate delinquent procedures.

### Delinquent FBAR Filers and the Streamlined Procedures

A US person who failed to file the report of foreign bank and financial accounts for prior years has two main options: the Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures and the IRS Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures. The correct path depends on whether the failure was willful.

The Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures under [IRS and FinCEN guidance](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/delinquent-fbar-submission-procedures) allow non-willful filers to catch up without penalty — provided the IRS has not already contacted the filer about the delinquency. The filer must include a statement explaining the reasons for the late filing.

The Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures are a broader program for US persons who were non-willfully noncompliant. Both the Streamlined Domestic and Streamlined Foreign Offshore procedures require amended tax returns and payment of unpaid tax, if any. For a full breakdown of the catch-up options, see our guide on [FBAR streamlined filing compliance procedures](/blog/fbar-streamlined-filing-compliance-procedures).

## What About DACA Recipients and Residents of US Territories?

DACA recipients and undocumented residents who meet the substantial presence test are US persons for FBAR. The BSA definition under [31 CFR 1010.350(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350) does not require lawful immigration status. It requires residence under IRC 7701(b), which turns on days present in the United States. A DACA recipient who has lived in the United States for years will almost certainly meet the substantial presence test and must file.

Residents of US territories — Puerto Rico, Guam, the US Virgin Islands, American Samoa, and the Northern Mariana Islands — are US citizens under [8 USC 1401](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/8/1401) and therefore US persons for FBAR. These residents have special income tax rules, but those rules do not change their FBAR obligations. The FBAR is a BSA requirement, separate from income tax. Territory residents with foreign financial accounts over $10,000 must file.

## Decision Tree: Do You Need to File the FBAR?

Use this decision tree to determine whether you are a US person for FBAR purposes. Work through the questions in order and stop when you reach a conclusion. Each step maps to the definition in [31 CFR 1010.350(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350) and the IRS FBAR guidelines.

1. **Are you a US citizen?** If yes — you are a US person. File if your foreign financial accounts exceed $10,000 at any point during the year.
2. **Do you hold a US green card (I-551)?** If yes — you are a US person. File if foreign financial accounts exceed $10,000.
3. **Are you present in the United States for at least 31 days this year?** If no — you likely do not meet substantial presence. You are probably not a US person based on presence.
4. **Does your weighted day count equal 183 or more?** (Current year at 100% + prior year at 1/3 + two years ago at 1/6.) If yes — you are a US person under IRC 7701(b). File if foreign financial accounts exceed $10,000.
5. **Does a tax treaty tie-breaker apply?** Even if yes — your FBAR obligation remains. File if foreign financial accounts exceed $10,000.
6. **Is your entity formed under US law?** If yes — the entity is a US person. File if it holds foreign financial accounts over $10,000.
7. **None of the above?** You are likely not a US person for FBAR. If uncertain, consult a tax professional.

If you fall into any of categories 1 through 6 and your foreign financial accounts exceeded $10,000 at any point during the year, the FBAR deadline is April 15, with an automatic extension to October 15, per [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306) and [IRS guidance](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/report-of-foreign-bank-and-financial-accounts-fbar). See our [FBAR filing deadline guide](/blog/fbar-filing-deadline-2026) for details.

## What Penalties Apply If a US Person Does Not File?

Penalties are set by [31 USC 5321](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321) and apply per account, per year. A US person who fails to file faces three levels of enforcement based on whether the failure was willful.

- **Non-willful violation**: Up to $16,117 per account per year under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321) (2025 inflation-adjusted amount)
- **Willful violation**: The greater of $100,000 or 50% of the account balance per year under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321)
- **Criminal penalties**: Up to $250,000 in fines and 5 years imprisonment under [31 USC 5322](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5322)

Three unreported foreign financial accounts for two years equals six violations. At the non-willful rate, that is up to $96,702 in penalties (6 × $16,117 under 31 USC 5321). For a full breakdown, see our [FBAR penalties guide](/blog/fbar-penalties-what-happens-if-you-dont-file).

## Frequently Asked Questions About US Person FBAR Rules

These are the most frequently asked questions about who is a US person for FBAR purposes. The answers below cover the main categories — citizens, resident aliens, green card holders, entities, and the types of foreign financial accounts that trigger reporting. For advice on your specific situation, consult a qualified tax professional.

**Who is a US person for FBAR filing purposes?**

A US person under [31 CFR 1010.350(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350) includes US citizens, lawful permanent residents, and resident aliens who meet the substantial presence test under [IRC 7701(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/7701). It also includes domestic entities — corporations, partnerships, LLCs, and trusts formed under US law. Any US person with foreign financial accounts exceeding $10,000 must file FinCEN Form 114 with FinCEN.

**Does a US citizen living abroad still need to file the FBAR?**

Yes. US citizenship, not physical location, determines FBAR status. Under [31 CFR 1010.350(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350), every US citizen is a US person regardless of where they live. Dual citizens, long-term expats, and citizens who have never lived in the United States must all file if their foreign financial accounts exceed $10,000 at any point under [31 USC 5314](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5314).

**What is the substantial presence test for FBAR?**

The substantial presence test under [IRC 7701(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/7701) counts your US presence over three years. You must be present at least 31 days in the current year. Your total weighted days — current year at 100%, prior year at 1/3, and two years ago at 1/6 — must reach 183 days. If you meet both conditions, you are a US resident alien and therefore a US person for FBAR purposes.

**Does a tax treaty tie-breaker election remove my FBAR obligation?**

No. A treaty tie-breaker election reduces your US income tax obligations but does not remove your FBAR duty. The FBAR is a Bank Secrecy Act requirement under [31 USC 5314](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5314), separate from income tax law. If you meet the substantial presence test, your FBAR obligation exists regardless of any treaty election. See our [FBAR vs. FATCA comparison](/blog/fbar-vs-fatca-form-8938-differences) for more on how BSA and tax rules differ.

**Are corporations and LLCs required to file the FBAR?**

Yes. Under [31 CFR 1010.350(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350), any domestic corporation, partnership, or LLC formed under the laws of any US state is a US person. If the entity holds a financial interest in or has signature authority over foreign financial accounts exceeding $10,000, it must file FBAR Form 114. Formation under US law is the only test — business activity can be entirely overseas.

**Do I still have to file the FBAR if Form 8840 reduces my income tax residency?**

Yes. Form 8840 (Closer Connection Exception) is an IRS income tax election. It affects your income tax residency status, not your FBAR status. The FBAR is governed by [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350), not the Internal Revenue Code. If you meet the substantial presence test, you are a US person for FBAR even if Form 8840 treats you as a non-resident for income tax.

**Who is considered a US person for tax purposes?**

For income tax, IRC 7701(a)(30) defines US persons as citizens and resident aliens. For FBAR, the definition under [31 CFR 1010.350(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350) is similar but operates independently. A treaty tie-breaker can change your income tax residency but not your FBAR status. The FBAR instructions require a separate filing with FinCEN regardless of your income tax position with the IRS.

**What types of foreign financial accounts must be reported?**

Foreign financial accounts subject to FBAR reporting include foreign bank accounts (checking and savings), foreign brokerage and securities accounts, foreign mutual funds, foreign life insurance policies with cash value, foreign pension accounts, and other accounts held at a financial institution outside the United States. The account type matters less than the location of the financial institution. Any account at a foreign financial institution counts toward the $10,000 aggregate threshold.

**Do jointly owned foreign accounts require separate FBAR filings?**

Yes. Each US person with a financial interest in jointly owned foreign financial accounts must file a separate FBAR. Both co-owners report the full balance of the jointly owned accounts — not just their proportional share. The only exception is the married couple spousal filing rule, which requires specific conditions and both spouses' signatures to apply.

**What is the difference between financial interest and signature authority?**

Financial interest means you own or have a beneficial ownership stake in a foreign financial account. Signature authority means you can control the disposition of assets in a foreign financial account — even if you have no ownership stake. Both trigger FBAR reporting. A US employee who can wire funds from an employer's foreign bank account has signature authority over that account and must file, even though the employer owns the account.

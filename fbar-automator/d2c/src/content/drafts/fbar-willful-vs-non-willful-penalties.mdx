# FBAR Willful vs Non-Willful Penalty: What the Difference Costs You

The FBAR willful vs non-willful penalty gap is enormous. A non-willful violation carries a maximum of $16,117 per missed annual report under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). A willful violation carries the greater of $100,000 or 50% of the account balance — per account, per year — under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). That gap can mean $16,117 versus $1,000,000+ for the same unreported account.

The Bank Secrecy Act created this two-tier structure to distinguish honest mistakes from deliberate evasion. Courts have spent years defining the line. Understanding the distinction before you act on late FBARs is critical.

The FBAR covers US persons with any foreign financial account exceeding $10,000 in aggregate value. The duty to file comes from [31 USC 5314](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5314). Missing it triggers penalties under [31 USC 5321](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). Each year you miss is a separate violation with its own statute of limitations.

Every US person — including US citizens, US residents, green card holders, and entities — who holds or has signature authority over foreign financial accounts must file the Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts (FinCEN Form 114) when the aggregate value of those accounts exceeds $10,000 at any point during the calendar year. The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) administers the FBAR program under the Bank Secrecy Act (BSA). The Internal Revenue Service enforces FBAR penalties on behalf of FinCEN.

## Statutory Framework: 31 USC 5321 and 31 USC 5322

The FBAR penalty framework rests on two key statutes within the Bank Secrecy Act. Understanding their structure is essential for evaluating penalty exposure.

[31 USC 5321](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321) governs civil FBAR penalties. Section 5321(a)(5)(A) gives the Treasury Secretary (delegated to the IRS) authority to impose civil penalties for FBAR violations. Section 5321(a)(5)(B) sets the non-willful penalty — up to $16,117 per violation (inflation-adjusted for 2025). Section 5321(a)(5)(C) sets the willful penalty — the greater of $100,000 or 50% of the account balance at the time of the violation.

[31 USC 5322](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5322) governs criminal FBAR penalties. Section 5322(a) imposes fines up to $250,000 and imprisonment up to 5 years for willful violations of BSA reporting requirements. Section 5322(b) increases the maximum to $500,000 and 10 years when the FBAR violation occurs in connection with another federal crime, such as money laundering under 18 USC 1956 or tax evasion under 26 USC 7201.

The civil penalty under 31 USC 5321 and the criminal penalty under 31 USC 5322 are not mutually exclusive. The IRS can assess civil penalties while the Department of Justice simultaneously pursues criminal prosecution for the same willful FBAR violation. This dual-track enforcement makes the FBAR willful penalty among the harshest in the US tax system.

## What Is the Non-Willful FBAR Penalty?

The non-willful FBAR penalty is up to $16,117 per missed annual report under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). This is the 2025 inflation-adjusted figure — the original base was $10,000. A non-willful violation results from negligence, genuine mistake, or misunderstanding — not deliberate concealment. The IRS can reduce or waive this penalty when you show reasonable cause and good faith.

The non-willful penalty applies when a US person fails to file an FBAR but did not do so intentionally or with reckless disregard for the FBAR filing requirement. The penalty assessment depends on the facts and circumstances of each case. The IRS considers the filer's education, compliance history, and the nature of the foreign financial accounts when evaluating whether to assess the full penalty or a reduced amount.

### Non-Willful FBAR Penalties Under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)

Section 5321(a)(5)(B)(i) of 31 USC establishes the maximum non-willful penalty. The statute uses the term "reasonable cause" as the standard for penalty relief. Under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)(ii), if a US person establishes that the FBAR violation was due to reasonable cause and the filer exercised due diligence, the IRS shall not impose a civil penalty.

The non-willful penalty amount is subject to annual inflation adjustment under the Federal Civil Penalties Inflation Adjustment Act. The penalty has increased from the original $10,000 statutory amount as follows:

- **2015**: $10,000 (pre-adjustment base)
- **2016**: $12,459
- **2017–2018**: $12,459
- **2019**: $12,907
- **2020**: $13,481
- **2021**: $12,921
- **2022**: $14,489
- **2023**: $15,611
- **2024**: $16,117
- **2025**: $16,117 (current)

These inflation-adjusted amounts apply to FBAR penalties assessed in the given calendar year, not to the calendar year of the FBAR violation itself. The IRS applies the penalty amount in effect at the time of assessment.

### How the IRS Counts Non-Willful Violations

The Supreme Court settled the non-willful penalty counting dispute in *United States v. Bittner*, 598 U.S. 95 (2023). Alexandru Bittner, a dual Romanian-US citizen, missed FBARs for five years covering 272 foreign accounts. The government sought $2.72 million — one penalty per account per year under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B). The Supreme Court ruled 5-4 for Bittner. Non-willful penalties apply **per annual FBAR report** — not per account.

Therefore, if you miss three consecutive FBARs, the maximum non-willful exposure is 3 reports × $16,117 = **$48,351** (31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)). However, this does not help willful filers. The per-report rule applies to non-willful violations only.

### Bittner v United States: The Supreme Court Per-Report Rule

The *Bittner* decision fundamentally changed the economics of non-willful FBAR penalties. Before *Bittner*, the IRS and several circuit courts assessed non-willful penalties per account, per year. A US person with 50 foreign financial accounts who missed one FBAR faced potential exposure of 50 × $16,117 = $805,850 — even for a purely non-willful violation.

Justice Gorsuch, writing for the majority, held that the "violation" in 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(A) refers to the failure to file the annual FBAR report — not the failure to report each individual foreign financial account. The FBAR is a single report filed once per calendar year. One missed report equals one non-willful violation.

The dissent, led by Justice Barrett, argued that each unreported account constitutes a separate violation of the Bank Secrecy Act reporting requirements. The dissent noted that the FBAR requires account-level detail, and failing to report each account is a distinct regulatory failure.

Despite the dissent, *Bittner* is now settled law for non-willful FBAR penalties. The per-report rule dramatically reduces exposure for US persons with multiple foreign financial accounts who failed to file through non-willful conduct. A US citizen with 100 accounts who missed one year faces a maximum penalty of $16,117 — not $1,611,700.

However, *Bittner* does not help willful filers. The willful penalty under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C) is assessed per account, per year. The Supreme Court expressly limited its holding to non-willful violations under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B).

### What Qualifies as Non-Willful?

Non-willful conduct is a failure to file that results from genuine mistake or lack of knowledge. Common situations include:

- Failing to file because you had no knowledge the FBAR existed
- Misunderstanding who must file — common among new green card holders
- Relying on a tax preparer who never asked about foreign accounts
- Filing your tax return correctly but missing the separate FBAR form
- Language barriers or unfamiliarity with US tax rules

Additionally, the IRS may waive non-willful penalties under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)(ii)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321) when you demonstrate reasonable cause and good faith.

A US person who moved to the United States from a country without equivalent foreign account reporting rules has a strong argument for non-willful treatment. The FBAR filing requirement is unique to US persons — most countries do not require their residents to report foreign financial accounts to a government agency. Immigrants, green card holders, and dual citizens frequently discover the FBAR requirement years after it first applied to them.

## What Is the FBAR Willful Penalty?

The FBAR willful penalty is the **greater of $100,000 or 50% of the account balance** per account, per year under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). This is not a flat cap. For a single $400,000 account, the willful penalty in one year is $200,000 under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C). For two accounts over four years, total penalties can exceed the combined balances.

The willful FBAR penalty is one of the most severe civil penalties in US tax law. Unlike most IRS penalties, the willful FBAR penalty is not capped by the amount of tax owed. A US person who owes zero additional tax — because they reported all income correctly — can still face hundreds of thousands of dollars in willful FBAR penalties for failing to file FinCEN Form 114.

### Willful FBAR Penalties Under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)

Section 5321(a)(5)(C) of 31 USC sets the willful penalty at the greater of two amounts: $100,000 (inflation-adjusted) or 50% of the balance in the unreported foreign financial account at the time of the violation. The $100,000 floor means that even small accounts trigger significant penalties when the IRS establishes willfulness.

For example, if a US citizen willfully fails to report a foreign financial account with a $30,000 balance, the penalty is $100,000 — not $15,000 (50% of $30,000). The 50% calculation only controls when the account balance exceeds $200,000. For a $500,000 account, the penalty is $250,000 (50% of $500,000), which exceeds the $100,000 floor.

The willful penalty applies per account, per year. A US person with three unreported foreign financial accounts over four calendar years faces up to 12 separate penalty assessments under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C). Each assessment carries the greater-of calculation independently.

### How the IRS Counts Willful Violations

Unlike the *Bittner* per-report rule, the IRS assesses willful FBAR penalties **per account, per year**. The Fourth Circuit confirmed this in *United States v. Williams*, 489 F. App'x 655 (4th Cir. 2012). Therefore, the government can stack penalties across every unreported account and every year of non-compliance. A filer with two accounts unreported for four years faces eight separate willful penalty assessments under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C).

The per-account counting method for willful violations means that FBAR penalties can exceed the total value of all foreign financial accounts combined. In *United States v. Zwerner* (S.D. Fla. 2014), the jury assessed willful penalties totaling approximately 133% of the account balance. The court upheld the penalty as constitutional, rejecting an Eighth Amendment excessive fines challenge.

### What Are the Criminal FBAR Penalties?

The most severe FBAR consequences are criminal. Under [31 USC 5322](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5322), willful violations carry:

- Up to **$250,000** in fines, and/or
- Up to **5 years** imprisonment

Violations connected to money laundering under 18 USC 1956 increase the maximum to **$500,000** and **10 years**. IRS Criminal Investigation pursues cases involving large undisclosed accounts and active concealment through nominees or shell companies.

### Criminal Referral Process for Willful FBAR Violations

IRS Criminal Investigation (CI) receives FBAR criminal referrals from civil examiners who identify badges of willfulness during an FBAR examination. The referral triggers a separate criminal investigation. If CI recommends criminal prosecution, the case goes to the Department of Justice Tax Division for review.

Criminal prosecution for willful FBAR violations under 31 USC 5322 requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt — a higher standard than the preponderance of evidence standard for civil penalties. The government must prove that the US person knew about the FBAR filing requirement and deliberately chose not to comply.

Criminal FBAR cases typically involve large account balances, active concealment of foreign financial accounts, use of nominees or shell entities, or concurrent tax evasion. The Department of Justice has publicly stated that FBAR criminal enforcement is a priority. Plea agreements in FBAR criminal cases often include substantial civil penalty assessments under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C) in addition to criminal fines under 31 USC 5322.

## What Does "Willful" Mean Under FBAR Law?

Willfulness is the key legal question in any FBAR case. It separates a $16,117 non-willful fine (31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)) from a $200,000+ willful penalty (31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)). Courts apply the *Safeco Insurance Co. v. Burr*, 551 U.S. 47 (2007) standard: either a "knowing violation of a known legal duty" or "reckless disregard" of that duty satisfies willfulness. You do not need to intend to break the law. Recklessly ignoring a known obligation is enough.

The willfulness standard in FBAR cases is lower than in criminal tax cases. The government does not need to prove that the US person specifically intended to violate the Bank Secrecy Act. Reckless disregard of a known reporting obligation — where the filer was aware of the FBAR requirement or should have been aware — is sufficient to establish willfulness for civil penalty purposes under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C).

### The Bedrosian Standard: Reckless Disregard

The Third Circuit applied this framework to FBAR in *Bedrosian v. United States*, 912 F.3d 144 (3d Cir. 2018). Arthur Bedrosian reported one Swiss UBS account at full value and listed a second at a fraction of its actual balance. The Third Circuit held that willfulness under 31 USC 5321(a)(5) includes **reckless disregard of a known legal obligation**. Specifically, knowing you had foreign accounts, suspecting reporting rules existed, and taking no steps to verify compliance can satisfy reckless disregard. This lower bar has driven hundreds of penalty determinations since 2018.

The *Bedrosian* decision is significant because it lowered the practical threshold for establishing willfulness. Before *Bedrosian*, some courts required the government to show actual knowledge of the FBAR requirement. After *Bedrosian*, the government can establish willfulness by showing that the US person acted with reckless disregard — meaning the filer was aware of facts suggesting a reporting obligation and consciously chose not to investigate further.

### Williams: Willfulness Inferred from Schedule B

In *United States v. Williams*, 489 F. App'x 655 (4th Cir. 2012), the Fourth Circuit found willfulness where the taxpayer checked "No" on Schedule B. Schedule B asks whether you held a financial interest or signature authority over a foreign account. Checking "No" while holding foreign accounts was direct evidence of willfulness — a deliberate misrepresentation.

*Williams* also confirmed that the 50% penalty applies to the **highest account balance for the year** under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C). For example, if your account peaked at $600,000 in April but fell to $300,000 by December, the 50% penalty is $300,000 — based on the high-water mark under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C).

### United States v Boyd: Willfulness and Professional Reliance

In *United States v. Boyd*, No. 2:18-cv-803 (C.D. Cal. 2019), the court examined whether reliance on a tax professional negates a finding of willfulness. Boyd argued that his tax preparer never asked about foreign financial accounts and therefore he could not have willfully failed to file the FBAR. The court rejected this defense, finding that Boyd had independent reason to know about the FBAR requirement based on his Schedule B answers and the account balances involved.

The *Boyd* case illustrates an important limit on the professional reliance defense. Reliance on a tax preparer may support a non-willful classification when the US person had no independent reason to know about the FBAR filing obligation. However, when the filer signed a Schedule B containing the foreign account question, reliance on a preparer who did not raise the issue does not automatically establish non-willful conduct.

### Badges of Willfulness in FBAR Cases

IRS examiners and courts look for specific patterns of conduct — commonly called "badges of willfulness" — when determining whether an FBAR violation was willful. These badges are not individually dispositive, but multiple badges present in the same case strongly support a willfulness finding under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C).

The IRS Internal Revenue Manual and court decisions identify these common badges of willfulness:

- **Schedule B false statement**: Checking "No" to the foreign account question on Form 1040 Schedule B while holding foreign financial accounts
- **Concealment activity**: Using nominee account holders, shell companies, trusts, or intermediaries to obscure ownership of foreign financial accounts
- **Ignored professional advice**: A tax professional, attorney, or financial advisor informed the US person about the FBAR filing requirement, and the filer chose not to comply
- **Repeated non-filing**: Failure to file FBARs across multiple calendar years — especially after receiving IRS correspondence about foreign account reporting
- **Large undisclosed balances**: Account balances large enough that a reasonable US person would recognize a reporting obligation
- **Professional sophistication**: The filer works in finance, law, accounting, or a related field where foreign account reporting rules are commonly known
- **Selective reporting**: Filing FBARs for some foreign financial accounts but not others, demonstrating knowledge of the FBAR requirement
- **Destroyed or hidden records**: Failure to maintain or produce records related to foreign financial accounts
- **Offshore entity use**: Creating foreign entities or structures with no business purpose other than holding financial accounts outside the US

The presence of even two or three badges of willfulness can shift the IRS examiner's assessment from a non-willful penalty under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B) to a willful penalty under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C).

### Willful Blindness and Conscious Avoidance

Courts also recognize **willful blindness** — deliberately avoiding knowledge of obligations you suspect exist. If your CPA asked each year about foreign accounts and you deflected, a court may find willful blindness. In short, this "conscious avoidance" theory applies where taxpayers avoided questions they feared would require disclosure.

The willful blindness doctrine means that a US person cannot avoid a willful FBAR penalty by simply refusing to learn about the FBAR filing requirement. If the facts show that the filer suspected a reporting obligation existed but took deliberate steps to avoid confirming it, courts will treat this as equivalent to actual knowledge for penalty assessment purposes under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C).

## Which of the Following Penalties Applies to Willful Failure to File FBAR?

For willful failure to file an FBAR, the civil penalty is the greater of **$100,000 or 50% of the account balance** per account, per year under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C). Additionally, criminal prosecution under 31 USC 5322 can add up to **$250,000 in fines** and **5 years imprisonment**. Both can apply to the same violation — the IRS handles civil penalties, the Department of Justice handles criminal prosecution. The $100,000 floor means even a small account triggers a $100,000 penalty per year if willful under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C).

## How Do Courts Determine Willfulness in FBAR Cases?

Courts and IRS examiners weigh multiple factors when deciding whether a filer acted willfully. The IRS must prove willfulness by clear and convincing evidence in civil cases. No single factor is decisive — examiners look at the full pattern of conduct and the taxpayer's sophistication.

The burden of proof matters in FBAR penalty litigation. For civil willful penalties, most courts require the government to prove willfulness by a preponderance of the evidence, though some courts have applied the higher "clear and convincing" standard. For criminal FBAR penalties under 31 USC 5322, the government must prove willfulness beyond a reasonable doubt.

### Factors That Indicate Willfulness

- **Schedule B misrepresentation**: Answering "No" on Form 1040 Schedule B while holding foreign accounts — as in *Williams* — is strong evidence of willfulness
- **Ignored professional advice**: Your accountant or attorney told you about the FBAR requirement and you declined to file
- **Large account balances**: The IRS considers it harder to overlook a $400,000 account than a $4,000 one
- **Sophisticated taxpayer**: Attorneys, CPAs, bankers, and financial professionals face a higher awareness standard
- **Active concealment**: Using nominee account holders, shell companies, or offshore structures to hide accounts
- **Selective filing**: Filing FBARs for some accounts but not others shows knowledge of the rule
- **Repeated non-compliance**: Missing FBARs year after year — especially after IRS correspondence

### Factors That Indicate Non-Willfulness

- **No prior FBAR history**: First-time filers, especially new green card holders, who had no reason to know about the rule
- **Full income reporting**: Reporting all foreign interest income on your tax return — no tax benefit to concealment
- **Reliance on a preparer**: Your CPA prepared your returns without asking about foreign accounts
- **Cultural unfamiliarity**: Immigrants from countries without equivalent reporting rules may genuinely be unaware
- **Prompt voluntary disclosure**: Coming forward before any IRS contact demonstrates good faith
- **Small account balances**: Lower balances support the absence of a tax motive
- **No concealment activity**: The foreign financial accounts were held in the filer's own name with no effort to hide them

Consult a tax attorney before filing anything if you are unsure which category fits your situation. The Streamlined Procedures certification is signed under penalty of perjury — filing it when your conduct was willful can create criminal liability.

## FBAR Willful vs Non-Willful Penalty: Side-by-Side Comparison

The FBAR penalty structure covers three tiers based on intent — from negligent non-filing to deliberate concealment. The non-willful civil penalty tops out at $16,117 per year under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B). Willful civil penalties, however, can exceed the account balance itself under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C). Moreover, criminal prosecution adds fines and imprisonment on top of civil penalties under 31 USC 5322.

| Category | Maximum Penalty | Counted Per | Statute |
|----------|----------------|-------------|---------|
| Non-willful civil | $16,117 per violation | Annual report (per *Bittner*) | 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B) |
| Willful civil | $100,000 or 50% of balance (greater) | Account, per year (per *Williams*) | 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C) |
| Criminal | $250,000 fine + 5 years imprisonment | Per prosecution | 31 USC 5322 |
| Criminal (with other crimes) | $500,000 fine + 10 years imprisonment | Per prosecution | 31 USC 5322(b) |

The IRS also uses IRM 4.26.16 and IRM 4.26.17 — the Internal Revenue Manual sections governing FBAR penalty mitigation. Examiners can reduce penalties when filers show reasonable cause, cooperate, and have no prior FBAR violations.

### Non-Willful vs Willful vs Criminal: Detailed Penalty Comparison

The following comparison illustrates how the three FBAR penalty tiers apply to a single unreported foreign financial account worth $300,000 over three calendar years:

| Factor | Non-Willful | Willful Civil | Criminal |
|--------|-------------|---------------|----------|
| Penalty basis | Per report | Per account, per year | Per prosecution |
| Maximum per year | $16,117 | $150,000 (50% of $300K) | $250,000 fine |
| Three-year total | $48,351 | $450,000 | $250,000 + 5 years |
| Reasonable cause defense | Yes | No | N/A |
| Burden of proof | Preponderance | Preponderance | Beyond reasonable doubt |
| Statute of limitations | 6 years | 6 years | 5 years (criminal) |
| Penalty mitigation (IRM) | Available | Limited | Plea negotiation |

This comparison demonstrates why the willful vs non-willful classification is the most consequential determination in any FBAR penalty case. The same unreported foreign financial account produces a $48,351 maximum non-willful penalty versus a $450,000 willful penalty — nearly a 10x difference.

## What Is the Penalty for Understatement Due to Willful or Reckless Conduct?

The penalty for understatement due to willful or reckless FBAR conduct combines multiple layers of exposure. Under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321), the civil FBAR penalty is the greater of $100,000 or 50% of the account balance — per account, per year. Furthermore, the IRS adds a 20% accuracy penalty under 26 USC 6662 on any unpaid tax. Willful tax evasion under 26 USC 7201 adds fines up to $250,000 (26 USC 7201) and 5 years imprisonment. All three can apply to the same willful FBAR violation at once.

When the IRS determines that an FBAR violation involved reckless disregard of the Bank Secrecy Act reporting requirements, the penalty assessment reflects the full scope of willful treatment under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C). Reckless conduct — as defined in *Bedrosian* and *Safeco* — does not require proof that the US person actually knew about the FBAR requirement. It requires only that the filer acted with reckless disregard of a known or obvious reporting obligation.

## Real-World FBAR Penalty Scenarios

### Scenario 1: Non-Willful — Three Missed Reports

Maria became a US permanent resident in 2020. She kept her Brazilian accounts open with an aggregate value of $85,000 and reported all Brazilian interest income on her US tax returns. She had no knowledge of the FBAR form until 2025 — three missed FBARs for 2021, 2022, and 2023.

Under *Bittner*, the non-willful penalty is per annual report:

- Maximum enforcement: 3 reports × $16,117 = **$48,351** (31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B))
- [Streamlined Domestic Offshore Procedures](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/streamlined-filing-compliance-procedures): 5% × $85,000 = **$4,250** flat penalty (31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B))

Voluntary disclosure gives a strong financial incentive to act early. For more, see our guide to [FBAR delinquent filing procedures](/blog/fbar-delinquent-filing-procedures).

### Scenario 2: Willful — Ignored Professional Advice

James is a US citizen living in London. In 2018, his UK accountant told him about the FBAR requirement. James chose not to file. His HSBC UK account held approximately $630,000 at its 2018 peak. The IRS discovers the account through FATCA data exchange in 2024. Consequently, the IRS assesses willful penalties under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C) for 2018 through 2023 — six years at 50% of the annual balance. The six-year total exceeds $1.6 million in FBAR penalties alone. For UK account reporting, see [FBAR and UK bank accounts](/blog/fbar-uk-bank-accounts).

### Scenario 3: Willful Blindness — Schedule B Misrepresentation

Susan is a CPA. She inherited a Swiss investment account from her father in 2015. She knew about the FBAR requirement from her professional training. Nevertheless, she told herself the account did not "count" because her father still managed it — even though she was the named account holder. She checked "No" on Schedule B every year from 2015 through 2022. The account peaked at $180,000.

Under *Williams*, checking "No" on Schedule B is direct evidence of willfulness. The IRS therefore assesses under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C): 8 years × $100,000 floor = **$800,000** (50% × $180,000 = $90,000 falls below the floor under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)). Susan's professional background and Schedule B conduct destroy a non-willful defense. See our guide to [FBAR voluntary disclosure](/blog/fbar-voluntary-disclosure-practice).

### Scenario 4: Non-Willful — Multiple Accounts, Single Report

David is a US citizen and dual Israeli citizen. He holds five bank accounts in Israel with a combined aggregate value of $220,000. He filed his US tax returns and reported all foreign interest income but did not know about the FBAR filing requirement. He missed FBARs for calendar years 2020 through 2024.

Before *Bittner*, the IRS could have assessed penalties per account: 5 accounts × 5 years × $16,117 = $402,925. After *Bittner*, the maximum non-willful penalty is per annual report: 5 reports × $16,117 = **$80,585**. David's full income reporting and genuine lack of FBAR awareness support non-willful treatment and potential reasonable cause relief under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)(ii).

## Reasonable Cause Defense Against FBAR Penalties

The reasonable cause defense is the primary legal protection against non-willful FBAR penalties. Under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)(ii)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321), the IRS shall not impose a non-willful penalty if the US person establishes that the FBAR violation was due to reasonable cause.

Reasonable cause exists when a US person exercised ordinary business care and prudence but was nonetheless unable to comply with the FBAR filing requirement. The IRS evaluates reasonable cause based on the totality of the circumstances, including:

- The filer's education and sophistication regarding US tax and Bank Secrecy Act obligations
- Whether the US person relied in good faith on the advice of a qualified tax professional
- The filer's FBAR compliance history — first-time violations receive more favorable treatment
- Whether the foreign financial accounts were held in the filer's own name or concealed
- Whether the filer reported all foreign income on their US tax return
- The filer's immigration status and familiarity with US reporting requirements

The reasonable cause defense does not apply to willful FBAR penalties under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C). A US person who acted willfully — through knowing violation or reckless disregard — cannot use reasonable cause to reduce or eliminate the willful penalty. This is one of the most important distinctions between the willful vs non-willful FBAR penalty framework.

For a detailed guide on building a reasonable cause case, see our article on the [FBAR reasonable cause defense](/blog/fbar-reasonable-cause-defense).

## What Are the IRS Mitigation Guidelines for FBAR Penalties?

The IRS can reduce FBAR penalties even when a violation is proven. The mitigation framework comes from IRM 4.26.16 and IRM 4.26.17. Examiners weigh these factors before they assess the maximum penalty under 31 USC 5321(a)(5):

1. **Isolated vs recurring violation** — a single missed year supports mitigation; repeated non-compliance does not
2. **Account balance relative to penalty** — the IRS may reduce penalties that appear disproportionate
3. **Full cooperation** — prompt responses to examination requests receive more favorable treatment
4. **Reasonable cause** — a good-faith misunderstanding supported by evidence can support penalty waiver
5. **Prior compliance history** — no prior FBAR violations supports mitigation
6. **Voluntary disclosure** — using Streamlined or Delinquent procedures before IRS contact gives the strongest mitigation available

The IRS does not guarantee mitigation. However, non-willful cases often result in reduced or waived penalties when the filer cooperates and shows good faith.

### IRS Penalty Mitigation Guidelines Under IRM 4.26.16

IRM 4.26.16 provides the Internal Revenue Service framework for FBAR penalty determinations. The manual instructs examiners to consider mitigation before assessing the maximum penalty. Under IRM 4.26.16, the IRS applies a graduated penalty approach based on the severity of the FBAR violation and the filer's conduct.

For non-willful violations, IRM 4.26.16.4 outlines a penalty framework that considers the number of unreported foreign financial accounts, the aggregate value of those accounts, and whether the filer had prior FBAR violations. The IRS examiner may assess a penalty below the statutory maximum when the circumstances warrant a reduced penalty assessment.

For willful violations, IRM 4.26.16.4.5 provides that examiners should assess the maximum penalty under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C) unless specific mitigation factors are present. Mitigation for willful penalties is limited and requires managerial approval. The IRS may consider whether the US person entered a voluntary disclosure program before the IRS initiated contact.

IRM 4.26.17 provides additional guidance on FBAR penalty computation, including how to calculate the penalty based on account balances and how to apply the per-report rule from *Bittner* for non-willful violations.

## How to Respond to an FBAR Penalty Assessment

If you receive an FBAR penalty notice from the IRS, you have the right to respond and contest the penalty assessment. The FBAR penalty assessment process follows a specific administrative sequence under the Bank Secrecy Act enforcement framework.

The IRS typically issues a Letter 3709 or similar correspondence notifying the US person of the proposed FBAR penalty. The letter identifies the calendar year of the violation, the foreign financial accounts at issue, and the proposed penalty amount under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B) or 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C).

After receiving a proposed FBAR penalty assessment, a US person can take the following steps:

1. **Review the proposed penalty**: Determine whether the IRS classified the violation as willful or non-willful under 31 USC 5321(a)(5). Confirm that the penalty amount and account balances are accurate.

2. **Gather supporting documentation**: Collect evidence that supports your position — tax returns showing reported foreign income, correspondence with tax preparers, immigration records, and any other documents demonstrating reasonable cause or non-willful conduct.

3. **Request a conference with the examiner**: You can request a meeting with the IRS examiner to present mitigating evidence before the penalty is formally assessed. This is often the most effective opportunity to argue for reduced penalties.

4. **File a written protest**: If the penalty is formally assessed, you can file a written protest with the IRS Office of Appeals within 30 days of the assessment notice. The Appeals process provides an independent review of the penalty determination.

5. **Consult a tax attorney**: FBAR penalty cases involve complex legal questions about willfulness, reasonable cause, and Bank Secrecy Act enforcement. A tax attorney experienced in FBAR cases can evaluate your options and represent you before the IRS or in court.

6. **Pay and sue for refund**: If administrative remedies are exhausted, you can pay the assessed FBAR penalty and file a refund suit in federal district court. The court will review the willfulness determination and penalty assessment de novo.

The statute of limitations for FBAR penalty assessment is six years from the FBAR due date under 31 USC 5321(b)(1). Once the six-year window closes for a given calendar year, the IRS cannot assess FBAR penalties for that year.

## How Does the FBAR Statute of Limitations Affect Penalties?

The IRS has **six years** from the FBAR filing deadline to assess civil penalties under [31 USC 5321(b)(1)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). The clock starts on the due date set by [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306) — April 15, with an automatic extension to October 15. Each missed FBAR year carries its own six-year window. FATCA data exchange, moreover, gives the IRS foreign account data before the six-year window closes. Waiting does not reduce your risk — it increases it. For full details, see our article on the [FBAR statute of limitations](/blog/fbar-statute-of-limitations).

The six-year statute of limitations applies to both willful and non-willful civil FBAR penalties under 31 USC 5321. For criminal FBAR violations under 31 USC 5322, the statute of limitations is generally five years from the date of the violation under 18 USC 3282. However, some criminal charges related to FBAR violations — such as tax evasion or conspiracy — may carry longer limitation periods.

The practical effect of the statute of limitations is that the IRS can look back six calendar years when assessing FBAR penalties. For a US person who receives an FBAR examination notice in 2026, the IRS can potentially assess penalties for FBAR violations from calendar years 2019 through 2025. Earlier years are time-barred unless the filer signed a statute extension agreement.

## How to Fix Past Non-Filing Without Maximum Penalties

To fix past FBAR non-filing, choose the right IRS compliance path. Three programs are available. Acting before the IRS contacts you keeps all three options open. Once the IRS begins an examination, however, your choices narrow and full penalties apply.

1. **Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures** — For filers who reported all foreign income but missed the FBAR form. File delinquent FBARs through [BSA E-Filing](https://bsaefiling.fincen.treas.gov) with a statement explaining the late filing. No penalties apply when you reported all income and the IRS has not contacted you.

2. **Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures** — For non-willful filers with possible unreported foreign income. File 3 years of amended returns and 6 years of FBARs. Pay back taxes plus interest and a 5% miscellaneous penalty (Domestic) or zero penalty (Foreign). See our guide to [FBAR streamlined filing compliance procedures](/blog/fbar-streamlined-filing-compliance-procedures).

3. **IRS Voluntary Disclosure Practice** — For willful filers or those who cannot certify non-willfulness under penalty of perjury. Submit a preclearance request to IRS Criminal Investigation. You avoid prosecution but pay large civil penalties under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C). A tax attorney is required. See our article on [FBAR voluntary disclosure practice](/blog/fbar-voluntary-disclosure-practice).

Each compliance path carries different penalty exposure. The Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures result in zero penalties for qualifying US persons. The Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures cap the penalty at 5% of the highest aggregate value of foreign financial accounts. The Voluntary Disclosure Practice requires payment of substantial civil penalties but provides protection from criminal prosecution under 31 USC 5322.

## Frequently Asked Questions

These frequently asked questions address the most common issues around the FBAR willful vs non-willful penalty, including the Bittner ruling, Schedule B disclosure, and reasonable cause defense. If your situation involves possible willfulness, consult a tax attorney before filing anything under penalty of perjury.

**What is the difference between willful and non-willful FBAR penalties?**

A non-willful FBAR penalty is up to $16,117 per missed annual report under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). A willful FBAR penalty is the greater of $100,000 or 50% of the account balance, per account, per year under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). The key distinction is intent — negligence versus a knowing or reckless disregard of the filing obligation.

**Does the Bittner decision help willful filers?**

No. *United States v. Bittner*, 598 U.S. 95 (2023), applies only to non-willful penalties — the per-annual-report rule does not change willful penalty exposure. For willful violations, however, the IRS continues to assess penalties per account, per year — as *United States v. Williams* (4th Cir. 2012) confirmed under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C).

**Can I claim reasonable cause to avoid a willful FBAR penalty?**

Reasonable cause is a defense to non-willful penalties — not willful ones. Under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)(ii)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321), the IRS may waive non-willful penalties when you had reasonable cause and acted in good faith. Willful violations have no reasonable cause exception under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C). See our article on the [FBAR reasonable cause defense](/blog/fbar-reasonable-cause-defense).

**What happens if I file a late FBAR before the IRS contacts me?**

Filing late before any IRS contact is the best outcome available. The IRS has discretion under IRM 4.26.17 to treat voluntary compliance as a strong mitigating factor. Non-willful filers who reported all income can use the Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures to file without penalties. Once the IRS contacts you, therefore, these procedures become unavailable.

**Is checking "No" on Schedule B proof of willfulness?**

Courts treat a "No" answer on Schedule B while holding foreign accounts as strong — but not automatic — proof of willfulness. The Fourth Circuit in *United States v. Williams* found willfulness based on the Schedule B misrepresentation. Specifically, a prior "No" answer can destroy a non-willful defense — consult a tax attorney before filing amended returns or delinquent FBARs.

**Can FBAR penalties exceed my account balance?**

Yes. Willful FBAR penalties assessed per account, per year can stack above the total account value. In *United States v. Zwerner* (S.D. Fla. 2014), penalties reached about 133% of the account balance. As a result, penalties across multiple accounts and years can far exceed the combined account values under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C).

**What is the FBAR willful penalty for tax year 2021 or 2022?**

The willful FBAR penalty for violations in 2021, 2022, or any year is the greater of $100,000 or 50% of the account balance per account under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C). The non-willful penalty for those years was $12,921 (2021) and $14,489 (2022) — inflation-adjusted. The current 2025 non-willful penalty is $16,117 per report.

**What is the statute of limitations for FBAR penalty assessment?**

The IRS has six years from the FBAR filing deadline to assess civil FBAR penalties under [31 USC 5321(b)(1)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). The filing deadline is April 15 (with automatic extension to October 15) of the year following the calendar year of the FBAR. For criminal FBAR violations under 31 USC 5322, the statute of limitations is generally five years. Each calendar year of non-filing carries its own separate limitation period.

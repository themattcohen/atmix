# FBAR First-Time Filer Guide: How to Report Your Foreign Accounts

If you're a US person with foreign financial accounts and you've never filed an FBAR, you're not alone. Thousands of Americans — expats, immigrants, dual citizens, and people who inherited accounts abroad — discover the FBAR requirement every year. The good news: filing is straightforward once you understand what's required.

This guide walks you through the entire process, from determining whether you need to file to submitting your report through FinCEN's BSA E-Filing system.

## Do You Need to File an FBAR?

You must file an FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) if you meet **all three conditions**:

1. **You are a "United States person"** — a US citizen, green card holder, or resident alien who meets the [substantial presence test](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/substantial-presence-test) under [26 USC 7701(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/7701). This also includes US-formed entities: corporations, partnerships, trusts, and LLCs. The definition comes from [31 CFR 1010.350(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350).

2. **You had a financial interest in, or signature authority over, at least one foreign financial account** during the calendar year.

3. **The aggregate maximum value of ALL your foreign accounts exceeded $10,000** at any point during the year, per [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306).

That $10,000 threshold is **aggregate** — meaning you add up the highest values of all your foreign accounts. If you had two accounts that peaked at $6,000 and $5,500 at different times during the year, your aggregate maximum is $11,500, and you must report both accounts.

### Who Counts as a "United States Person"?

The FBAR definition is broader than many people expect:

- **US citizens** — regardless of where you live. A US citizen in London with a Barclays checking account must file.
- **Green card holders** — your FBAR obligation begins the moment your green card is issued, even if you still maintain accounts in your home country.
- **Resident aliens** — if you meet the substantial presence test (present in the US for at least 31 days this year and 183 days over three years using the weighted formula), you're a US person for FBAR purposes.
- **H-1B, L-1, and other visa holders** — once you meet the substantial presence test, you have FBAR obligations. Many visa holders don't realize this.

One common misconception: tax treaty elections do **not** affect your FBAR status. If you elected nonresident alien status under a tax treaty for income tax purposes, you are still a US person for FBAR filing under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350).

## What Accounts Must You Report?

### Reportable Foreign Financial Accounts

| Account Type | Reportable? |
|---|---|
| Foreign bank accounts (checking, savings, CDs) | **Yes** |
| Foreign brokerage and securities accounts | **Yes** |
| Foreign mutual fund accounts | **Yes** |
| Foreign insurance policies with cash surrender value | **Yes** |
| Foreign pension/retirement accounts (Canadian RRSP, Australian super, UK SIPP) | **Yes** |
| Accounts where you have signature authority but no ownership | **Yes** |
| Foreign annuity policies with cash value | **Yes** |

Foreign pension plans are a frequent surprise for immigrants. Canadian RRSPs, Mexican AFOREs, UK workplace pensions, and similar accounts are reportable on the FBAR. The exemption for retirement plans under [31 CFR 1010.350(g)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350) applies only to US-qualified plans (IRAs, 401(k)s, 403(b)s).

### What You Don't Need to Report

- **US domestic accounts** — accounts at US banks, even if denominated in foreign currency
- **Foreign real estate** held directly (not through a financial account)
- **US retirement accounts** — IRAs, 401(k)s, 403(b)s under [IRC §§ 408, 401(a), 403(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/408)
- **Cryptocurrency on foreign exchanges** — FinCEN issued [Notice 2020-2](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/shared/Notice-Virtual%20Currency%20Reporting%20on%20the%20FBAR%20123020.pdf) proposing to require this, but the rulemaking is not finalized. Crypto is **not currently required** on the FBAR. (See our article on [FBAR and cryptocurrency](/blog/fbar-cryptocurrency-foreign-exchanges) for the full analysis.)

## How to Calculate Your Maximum Account Value

The FBAR requires the **maximum value** of each account during the calendar year — not the December 31 balance. This distinction catches many first-time filers off guard.

### Step 1: Find the Peak Balance for Each Account

Review your monthly or quarterly account statements for the entire year. Identify the single highest balance reached at any point. Per [FinCEN guidance](https://www.fincen.gov/reporting-maximum-account-value), you can rely on periodic account statements to determine this value.

**Example**: Your UK savings account showed balances of £8,200 in March, £12,500 in July (after a bonus deposit), and £4,100 in December. The maximum value is £12,500, not £4,100.

### Step 2: Convert to US Dollars

Use the **Treasury Department's year-end exchange rate** for December 31 of the reporting year. Find the rates at [fiscal.treasury.gov](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/reports-statements/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/current.html).

For the 2025 tax year (filed in 2026), use December 31, 2025 rates.

If the Treasury doesn't publish a rate for your currency, use another verifiable exchange rate and document the source. For more detail, see our guide on [calculating maximum account value for FBAR](/blog/how-to-calculate-maximum-account-value-fbar).

### Step 3: Add Up All Accounts

Sum the converted maximum values of all your foreign accounts. If the total exceeds $10,000, you must file an FBAR and report **every** foreign account — even those with small balances.

## Filing Deadline

Your FBAR for the 2025 calendar year is due **April 15, 2026**, per [31 CFR 1010.306(c)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306). If you miss it, you automatically receive an extension to **October 15, 2026** — no form or request required. This automatic extension was created by the Surface Transportation and Veterans Health Care Choice Improvement Act of 2015.

Unlike IRS tax returns, no extension form is needed — the extension is automatic under federal law. For more on filing deadlines per IRS and FinCEN guidance, see our article on [FBAR filing deadlines](/blog/fbar-filing-deadline-2026).

## How to File: Step-by-Step

The FBAR is filed electronically through FinCEN's [BSA E-Filing system](https://bsaefiling.fincen.gov/). It is **not** filed with your tax return and does not go to the IRS — it goes to FinCEN (the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network).

### What You'll Need Before You Start

Gather this information for **each** foreign account:

- Financial institution name and address (including country)
- Account number
- Account type (bank, securities, or other)
- Maximum value during the year (converted to USD)
- Your SSN or ITIN

### Filing Steps

**1. Go to the BSA E-Filing system** at [bsaefiling.fincen.gov](https://bsaefiling.fincen.gov/).

**2. Select "File FBAR"** — individuals can use the no-registration option. You do not need to create an account.

**3. Enter your personal information** — name, SSN/ITIN, address, date of birth.

**4. Add each foreign account** — for every account, enter the institution name, address, account number, type, and maximum value in USD.

**5. Click "Validate"** — the system checks for missing required fields and flags errors.

**6. Sign electronically and submit** — no wet signature needed.

**7. Save your confirmation** — within two business days, you'll receive an acknowledgment with a 14-digit BSA Identifier (beginning with "310000"). Keep this permanently. You must retain FBAR records for 5 years per [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350).

Most people with a small number of accounts complete the process in 15–30 minutes once they've gathered their account information.

**Filing with a spouse?** If you and your spouse both have foreign accounts, you can file jointly on a single FBAR. To qualify for joint filing, all accounts must be jointly owned — if either spouse has an account in their name alone, that spouse must file a separate FBAR for that account. The spouse who does not sign the FBAR must complete and retain [FinCEN Form 114a](https://www.fincen.gov/how-do-i-file-fbar) (Record of Authorization to Electronically File FBARs). Keep this form in your records for at least 5 years — FinCEN may request it during an examination.

## What Happens If You Don't File

FBAR penalties are among the steepest in the US tax code. Here's what you face under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321):

### Non-Willful Penalties

If you didn't know about the FBAR requirement or made an honest mistake, the maximum penalty is **$16,536 per report** (2025 inflation-adjusted amount per [31 CFR 1010.821](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.821)).

After the Supreme Court's 2023 decision in [*Bittner v. United States*](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/22pdf/21-1195_h3ci.pdf), non-willful penalties are assessed **per FBAR report**, not per account. So if you failed to file one year's FBAR covering 10 accounts, the maximum non-willful penalty is $16,536 — not $165,360.

### Willful Penalties

If you knew about the requirement and deliberately didn't file, the penalty jumps to the **greater of $165,353 or 50% of the account balance** at the time of the violation, per [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321).

### Criminal Penalties

Willful violations can also result in criminal prosecution under [31 USC 5322](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5322): fines up to $250,000 and up to 5 years imprisonment. Criminal prosecution is rare and typically reserved for cases involving tax evasion or deliberate concealment — not first-time filers who genuinely didn't know.

### Reasonable Cause Exception

The IRS can waive non-willful penalties if you had reasonable cause for not filing, per [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)(ii)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). Factors include reliance on professional advice, the complexity of the law, and your level of education about reporting obligations. For more on penalties, see our article on [FBAR penalties](/blog/fbar-penalties-what-happens-if-you-dont-file).

## What If You Should Have Filed in Prior Years?

If you've had foreign accounts for years but never filed an FBAR, you have options:

### Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures

The [IRS Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/delinquent-fbar-submission-procedures) allow you to file late FBARs without penalties if:

- You properly reported all income from the foreign accounts on your US tax returns
- You paid all tax due on that income
- The IRS has not previously contacted you about the unreported accounts

File the delinquent FBARs through BSA E-Filing and include a statement explaining why you're filing late.

### Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures

If you owe additional tax, the [IRS Streamlined Procedures](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/streamlined-filing-compliance-procedures) offer reduced penalties for non-willful taxpayers. The offshore streamlined program requires a 5% penalty on the highest aggregate account balance over the compliance period — significantly less than the standard non-willful penalty of $16,536 per year.

## Common First-Time Filer Mistakes

**Using the year-end balance instead of maximum value.** If your account peaked at $15,000 in July but was down to $3,000 by December, your reportable maximum is $15,000.

**Forgetting foreign pension accounts.** Canadian RRSPs, UK workplace pensions, and Australian superannuation are all reportable. The "retirement plan" exemption only covers US-qualified plans.

**Thinking the FEIE eliminates FBAR obligations.** The Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (Form 2555) reduces your income tax — it has zero effect on your FBAR filing requirement. The FBAR is a separate FinCEN obligation, not an IRS tax form.

**Not aggregating across accounts.** Two accounts that each peaked below $10,000 can still trigger the filing requirement if their combined peak values exceed $10,000. Remember: each account's maximum value is measured independently at different times during the year, then all maximums are summed.

**Confusing FBAR with FATCA (Form 8938).** The FBAR goes to FinCEN with a $10,000 threshold. Form 8938 goes to the IRS with higher thresholds: $50,000 on the last day of the year or $75,000 at any time during the year for domestic filers ($200,000/$300,000 for expats filing jointly). You may need to file both — the reports serve different purposes and go to different agencies. See the [IRS comparison chart](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/comparison-of-form-8938-and-fbar-requirements) for details.

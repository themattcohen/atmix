# FBAR Business Account Signatory Authority: Who Must File and When

If you are a corporate officer or employee with authority to sign on a foreign bank account, you may have a personal FBAR filing obligation — separate from any filing the company makes. The FBAR business account signatory authority rules under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350) are straightforward once you know the two triggers: financial interest and signatory authority.

The Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts (FBAR), also known as FinCEN Form 114, applies to US persons who hold a financial interest in or have signatory authority over foreign financial accounts that exceeded $10,000 at any point during the year. This obligation comes from [31 USC 5314](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5314), the Bank Secrecy Act (BSA).

Many officers and employees assume their employer's FBAR covers them. It does not. The company and each individual with a qualifying interest file separately. Getting this wrong means potential penalties of $16,117 per non-willful violation under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321).

The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN), a bureau of the US Department of the Treasury, administers the FBAR program under the Bank Secrecy Act. FinCEN requires every US person — whether an individual, a corporation, a partnership, or an LLC — to report foreign financial accounts that exceed the $10,000 threshold. For business accounts, both the entity and the individuals connected to those accounts may have separate FBAR reporting obligations.

## Does Your Foreign Business Account Trigger a Personal FBAR Filing?

Yes — a foreign business account can create a personal FBAR filing obligation. This happens when you have a financial interest in or signatory authority over the account under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). The $10,000 threshold covers all your qualifying foreign accounts combined, at any point during the year. The business files under its own Employer Identification Number. You file separately under your Social Security Number. Both filings are required.

The obligation is per person. Two officers with signatory authority over the same $500,000 foreign account each have an independent filing obligation. The account appears on both returns.

A US person includes any US citizen, US resident, or entity organized under the laws of the United States or any US state. If you are a green card holder or meet the substantial presence test, you are a US person for FBAR purposes regardless of where you live. The Internal Revenue Service (IRS) and FinCEN both treat foreign bank accounts held by US persons as reportable.

## What Is Signature Authority for FBAR Purposes?

Signature authority — also called signatory authority — is a specific legal concept defined in [31 CFR 1010.350(f)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). It means the authority of an individual, acting alone or together with another individual, to control the disposition of money, funds, or other assets held in a foreign financial account by direct communication (whether in writing or otherwise) to the person with whom the financial account is maintained.

This definition has three critical elements. First, you must have the authority to control the disposition of the account's assets. Second, that authority must be exercisable by direct communication with the foreign financial institution. Third, the authority can be sole or joint — if you and another officer must both approve a transfer, each of you still has signatory authority under 31 CFR 1010.350(f).

Signature authority is not the same as being an authorized user. An employee who can view account balances online does not have signature authority. An officer who can independently wire $50,000 from a foreign bank account does have signature authority. The distinction turns on whether you can direct the bank to move money — not whether you can log in.

For FBAR purposes, signature authority creates a personal filing obligation even when you have no financial interest in the account. This is what surprises many corporate officers and employees. You do not need to own a single share of the company to owe an FBAR based on signature authority alone.

## What Is a Financial Interest in a Foreign Account?

A financial interest exists when you own or control the account — either directly or through a business entity you control. Under [31 CFR 1010.350(e)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350), two categories count: direct ownership and indirect ownership. Direct ownership is straightforward — the account is in your name. Indirect ownership, however, depends on your ownership percentage in the entity that holds the foreign account.

The financial interest test under 31 CFR 1010.350(e) is separate from the signature authority test. You can have both a financial interest and signature authority in the same foreign financial account, or you can have one without the other. Each creates an independent FBAR filing obligation.

### Direct Ownership of a Foreign Account

You have a direct financial interest when you are the account holder of record. This includes accounts held in your name even if another person or entity benefits from the funds.

A US person who opens a foreign bank account in their own name has a direct financial interest in that account. The same applies to a US citizen who is listed as a joint holder on a foreign financial account, even if the other joint holder is a non-US person.

### Indirect Ownership Through a Business Entity

You have an indirect financial interest under [31 CFR 1010.350(e)(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350) when you own more than 50% of a corporation, LLC, partnership, or trust that holds the foreign account. Ownership covers both the total value and voting power of the entity.

The 50% test applies through the ownership chain. If you own 60% of a holding company that owns 100% of an operating company with a foreign account, you have an indirect financial interest. Constructive ownership rules apply as they do for other tax purposes.

Under 31 CFR 1010.350(e), the financial interest also extends to accounts held by entities where you are the owner of record or holder of legal title — even if the account is for the benefit of another person. This means directors or officers who are nominal holders of corporate accounts may have a financial interest separate from their signature authority.

**Example**: Maria owns 60% of TechCo LLC, a Delaware company with a €200,000 operating account at Deutsche Bank. At a December 31 exchange rate of $1.09 per euro, the account holds about $218,000. Therefore, Maria has an indirect financial interest and must file an FBAR for that account. TechCo also files its own FBAR separately under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350).

## Financial Interest vs Signature Authority on FBAR

Understanding the distinction between financial interest and signature authority is essential for correct FBAR reporting. Many US persons have one but not the other. Some have both. The FBAR form (FinCEN Form 114) asks filers to identify which applies for each foreign financial account reported.

A financial interest under 31 CFR 1010.350(e) is based on ownership — you own the account directly or own more than 50% of the entity that holds the account. Signature authority under 31 CFR 1010.350(f) is based on control — you can direct the disposition of the account's assets regardless of ownership.

Here is an example that illustrates the difference. An employee who is the CFO of a private corporation has signature authority over the company's foreign bank accounts. She does not own any shares in the corporation. She has signature authority but no financial interest. She must file a personal FBAR based on signature authority alone. If the same CFO also owned 55% of the corporation, she would have both signature authority and a financial interest in the same accounts.

The practical consequence is that both triggers require FBAR filing, but the exemptions differ. There is no exemption from filing based on financial interest. The exemptions under 31 CFR 1010.350(f)(2) apply only to signature authority over accounts at certain regulated employers. This is why ownership-based obligations cannot be exempted, while signature-authority-based obligations sometimes can.

## What Is FBAR Signatory Authority Over a Business Account?

Signatory authority means the authority to control the disposition of assets in a foreign financial account by direct communication with the institution. This definition comes from [31 CFR 1010.350(f)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). You do not need to own the account. You need practical authority to move money or direct the bank to act.

The FBAR business account signatory authority rules focus on control, not access. A CFO who approves and starts wire transfers from a foreign operating account has signatory authority. An accounts payable clerk who enters pre-approved instructions that require a supervisor's sign-off does not.

### Who Typically Has FBAR Signatory Authority?

The following roles commonly create a signatory authority FBAR obligation:

- **Corporate officers** (CFO, Treasurer, VP Finance) listed as authorized signers on foreign bank accounts
- **Directors** who are bank-authorized signatories on corporate foreign financial accounts
- **Employees** named as authorized signatories on foreign operating or payroll accounts
- **Partners** in a US partnership with authority to control foreign partnership accounts
- **Trustees** with authority to control assets in foreign trust accounts (see [FBAR trust reporting requirements](/blog/fbar-trust-reporting-requirements))

In short, the key question is whether you can independently direct the disposition of the account's assets. If yes, you have signatory authority.

### What Does Not Create Signatory Authority?

These situations do not create a signatory authority filing obligation:

- **Read-only account access** — viewing statements or balances online without transaction authority
- **Ministerial processing** — submitting pre-approved wire instructions that require another person's approval before execution
- **Formulaic payroll processing** — running payroll from a foreign account under a fixed schedule without discretion to vary amounts or recipients
- **Backup contact status** — being listed as a secondary contact without authorization to transact
- **IT administrative access** — maintaining online banking systems without authority to initiate financial transactions

If your access requires another person's approval before anything happens, document that arrangement in writing. Keep a copy of the bank's authorization records showing who has signatory authority and who does not. This documentation is your primary defense if FinCEN questions your non-filing.

## Corporate Officers and FBAR Filing Obligations

Corporate officers face some of the most common FBAR business account signatory authority issues. A corporation organized under US law is itself a US person for FBAR purposes. When that corporation holds foreign financial accounts with an aggregate value exceeding $10,000 at any point during the calendar year, the corporation must file its own FBAR.

But the corporation's FBAR filing does not satisfy the personal obligations of its officers. Each officer who has signatory authority over the corporation's foreign bank accounts — or a financial interest through majority ownership — must file a separate personal FBAR.

Officers of a corporation typically have signatory authority if they are listed on the bank's authorization records as persons who can initiate or approve transactions. The title alone does not determine the obligation. A CEO who is not listed as an authorized signer on any foreign financial account does not have signatory authority over those accounts. A mid-level finance director who is listed as an authorized signer does.

The Internal Revenue Service has stated that FBAR obligations apply to each US person independently. An officer cannot rely on the entity's filing to satisfy their personal obligation. FinCEN Form 114 must be filed by both the corporation (reporting its foreign financial accounts) and by each officer who meets the financial interest or signatory authority test.

## Employee Signature Authority and FBAR Reporting

Employees who are not officers can also have FBAR filing obligations based on signatory authority. Any employee who is an authorized signer on a company's foreign financial account must evaluate whether they have a personal FBAR filing obligation under 31 CFR 1010.350(f).

The Bank Secrecy Act does not distinguish between officers and rank-and-file employees for signatory authority purposes. If an employee can independently direct the foreign bank to transfer funds, close the account, or otherwise control the disposition of assets, that employee has signatory authority. The employee's job title is irrelevant — what matters is the authority granted by the foreign financial institution.

Employees who discover they have signatory authority over foreign accounts should determine whether they qualify for one of the exemptions under 31 CFR 1010.350(f)(2). If not, they must file FinCEN Form 114 reporting the foreign financial accounts over which they have signatory authority. The FBAR filing deadline is April 15, with an automatic extension to October 15 under [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306).

Employers should inform employees when they are added as authorized signers on foreign bank accounts. Many FBAR penalties result from employees who were unaware they had been granted signatory authority by their company's foreign banking relationships.

## Which Employees Must File — and Which Are Exempt?

Officers and employees with signatory authority must file a personal FBAR unless they qualify for an exemption. Under [31 CFR 1010.350(f)(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350), the exemption applies when the employee has no financial interest in the account and their employer is one of the following: a federally examined bank (OCC, FDIC, Federal Reserve, or NCUA); an SEC- or CFTC-registered financial institution; a publicly traded US company on a national securities exchange; or a subsidiary at least 50% owned by a publicly traded company.

If your employer is a private company, no exemption applies. You must file.

### The Signatory Authority Exemption in Practice

**Example A — Exempt**: James is the Treasurer of PublicCo, a US company listed on the NYSE. PublicCo holds foreign operating accounts at Barclays in the UK. James has signatory authority but no ownership stake. Because PublicCo is publicly listed, James qualifies for the exemption under [31 CFR 1010.350(f)(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). He does not file a personal FBAR for those accounts.

**Example B — Not Exempt**: James also owns 60% of a private subsidiary of PublicCo that holds a separate HSBC account. That ownership creates a financial interest. The exemption does not cover financial interests. James must file a personal FBAR for the HSBC account.

## The 25-Signer Exemption for Officers and Employees

A separate exemption exists for foreign financial accounts that have 25 or more authorized signers. Under [31 CFR 1010.350(g)(5)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350), if you are an officer or employee with signatory authority (but no financial interest) over a foreign financial account that has 25 or more persons with signatory authority, you are not required to file a personal FBAR for that account.

However, this exemption has conditions. The entity that owns the foreign financial account must itself file an FBAR reporting that account. If the entity fails to file, the exemption does not apply, and each individual with signatory authority is personally responsible for FBAR reporting.

This 25-signer exemption is common in large multinational corporations where dozens of officers and employees across multiple countries are authorized signers on the same corporate foreign bank accounts. Without this exemption, every authorized signer would need to file a personal FBAR — creating significant administrative burden for large organizations.

To rely on the 25-signer exemption, maintain records showing that the account had 25 or more authorized signers during the calendar year and that the entity filed its own FBAR. If audited by FinCEN or the IRS, you will need to produce this documentation.

## When Must a Business Entity File Its Own FBAR?

A business entity that is a US person must file its own FBAR when the aggregate value of its foreign financial accounts exceeds $10,000 at any point during the calendar year. This applies to corporations, partnerships, LLCs, and trusts organized under US law.

The entity files FinCEN Form 114 under its Employer Identification Number (EIN). The entity reports all foreign bank accounts, foreign securities accounts, and other foreign financial accounts in which it has a financial interest. The entity's FBAR is due April 15, with an automatic extension to October 15.

A US corporation with a foreign subsidiary's bank account in its name has a financial interest in that account. A US partnership that opens a foreign bank account for business operations holds a financial interest. In each case, the entity must file its own FBAR.

Entity-level FBAR filing does not relieve individual officers, directors, or employees of their personal FBAR obligations. The entity and each qualifying individual file separately. FinCEN treats these as independent obligations under the Bank Secrecy Act.

Even if a foreign financial account is held in the name of a foreign subsidiary, the US parent may still need to report that account if the parent has a financial interest — for example, if the US parent owns more than 50% of the foreign subsidiary. The ownership chain matters for determining who has a financial interest under 31 CFR 1010.350(e).

## How to Determine Your FBAR Business Account Signatory Authority Obligation?

Use this process to decide whether the FBAR business account signatory authority rules create a personal filing obligation for you. Answer each question in order. Stop as soon as a "no" applies. If you reach step 7, you must file. See our [first-time filer guide](/blog/fbar-first-time-filer-guide) for background on how FBAR works.

1. Are you a US person — a citizen, resident alien, or entity formed under US law? If no, stop — the BSA does not apply.
2. Do you have a foreign financial account (bank, brokerage, or other financial account outside the US)? If no, stop.
3. Did the aggregate balance of all your qualifying foreign accounts exceed $10,000 at any point during the calendar year? If no, stop.
4. Do you have a financial interest in the account — direct ownership or 50%+ ownership of an entity holding the account under [31 CFR 1010.350(e)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350)?
5. Do you have signatory authority — authority to control the disposition of the account's assets under [31 CFR 1010.350(f)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350)?
6. If yes to step 4 or 5, do you qualify for the signatory authority exemption under [31 CFR 1010.350(f)(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350)?
7. If not exempt, file an FBAR by April 15. An automatic extension to October 15 applies per [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306). See our [FBAR 2026 filing deadline guide](/blog/fbar-filing-deadline-2026) — the CFR also permits this extension for FinCEN Form 114.

If you have both financial interest and signatory authority in the same account, you still file once for that account. You check both boxes on FinCEN Form 114 (the FBAR form). Filing once means one Form 114 record for that account. If you have multiple accounts, you report each one. The FBAR instructions on FinCEN's website explain how to complete each field.

When calculating the $10,000 threshold, remember that it is based on the aggregate value of all foreign financial accounts — not just business accounts. If you have a personal foreign bank account worth $6,000 and signatory authority over a corporate foreign account worth $5,000, your aggregate value is $11,000 and both accounts must be reported on your FBAR.

## How Do LLC Members Handle FBAR Signatory Authority Rules?

LLC members face different FBAR rules depending on the LLC's structure. Single-member LLCs and multi-member LLCs receive different treatment, but both can create personal filing obligations alongside the entity's own filing.

### Single-Member LLC Foreign Accounts

A single-member LLC that is disregarded for US tax purposes does not file its own FBAR. Instead, you — the sole member — are the US person for FBAR purposes. You report the LLC's foreign accounts as if you held them directly. The LLC's tax transparency carries over to BSA reporting.

Because the single-member LLC is disregarded, its foreign bank accounts are treated as your personal foreign financial accounts. You report them on your FinCEN Form 114 under your Social Security Number. There is no separate entity filing for the disregarded LLC. The maximum account value for each foreign financial account held by the LLC counts toward your personal $10,000 aggregate threshold.

### Multi-Member LLC Foreign Accounts

A multi-member LLC taxed as a partnership files its own FBAR as an entity. Individual members must also check whether they cross the 50%+ ownership threshold under [31 CFR 1010.350(e)(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). A 60% member has an indirect financial interest and must file personally. A 30% member typically does not cross the financial interest threshold — but that member should still check for signatory authority if they manage day-to-day banking.

A multi-member LLC that elects corporate taxation under the Internal Revenue Service check-the-box rules follows the same FBAR reporting rules as a corporation. The LLC files its own FBAR as an entity. Individual members with more than 50% ownership have a financial interest. Members with signatory authority over the LLC's foreign financial accounts have a signatory authority obligation.

## Partnership Foreign Accounts and FBAR

A US partnership that holds foreign financial accounts must file its own FBAR when the aggregate value of those accounts exceeds $10,000 at any point during the calendar year. The partnership files FinCEN Form 114 under its EIN.

Individual partners face FBAR obligations based on their ownership percentage and their signatory authority. A partner who owns more than 50% of the partnership's profits or capital has a financial interest in the partnership's foreign financial accounts under 31 CFR 1010.350(e)(2). That partner must file a personal FBAR.

A partner who owns 50% or less but has signatory authority over the partnership's foreign bank accounts must also file a personal FBAR — unless an exemption applies under 31 CFR 1010.350(f)(2). In most partnerships, general partners who manage the entity's foreign banking relationships have signatory authority.

Limited partners who are passive investors without signatory authority and without a majority ownership interest typically do not have personal FBAR obligations for the partnership's foreign financial accounts. However, if the limited partner is also a managing member or has been granted signatory authority by the foreign financial institution, the FBAR filing obligation applies.

## Trust Foreign Accounts With Business Purposes

Trusts organized under US law that hold foreign financial accounts for business purposes must file their own FBAR when the $10,000 aggregate value threshold is met during the calendar year. The trustee files FinCEN Form 114 on behalf of the trust.

Trustees with signatory authority over the trust's foreign bank accounts have personal FBAR filing obligations under 31 CFR 1010.350(f). This applies whether the trust holds foreign financial accounts for investment purposes, business operations, or estate planning. The trustee's personal FBAR is separate from the trust's entity-level FBAR.

A US person who is a beneficiary of a trust may also have a financial interest in the trust's foreign financial accounts under 31 CFR 1010.350(e) — particularly if the beneficiary has an ownership interest exceeding 50% of the trust's assets or income. Grantors of revocable trusts are treated as the owners of the trust's foreign accounts for FBAR purposes.

Business trusts — such as statutory trusts used for asset protection or business operations — follow the same FBAR rules as other entities. The trust files its own FBAR, and each individual with a financial interest or signatory authority files separately.

## How to Report Business Accounts on FBAR (Part II vs Part III)

FinCEN Form 114 has three main sections for reporting foreign financial accounts. Understanding which section to use for business accounts is essential for correct FBAR filing.

**Part II** is where you report foreign financial accounts in which you have a financial interest. If you own or control the account — either directly or through more than 50% ownership of the entity that holds it — you report the account in Part II. This is the section for accounts where you are the owner of record, the beneficial owner, or where you have an indirect financial interest under 31 CFR 1010.350(e).

**Part III** is where you report foreign financial accounts over which you have signatory authority but no financial interest. If you are an officer, director, or employee authorized to sign on a company's foreign bank account but you do not own the account or the entity, you report the account in Part III. This section captures the signatory authority obligation under 31 CFR 1010.350(f).

If you have both a financial interest and signatory authority over the same foreign financial account, report the account in Part II. You do not need to report it in both sections. FinCEN Form 114 instructions specify that financial interest takes precedence when both apply.

For each account, you must report the maximum account value during the calendar year, the account number, the name and address of the foreign financial institution, and the type of account (bank, securities, or other). The maximum account value is the highest balance at any point during the calendar year, converted to US dollars using the Treasury Department's end-of-year exchange rate.

## FinCEN Form 114a: Authorization to File

FinCEN Form 114a — Record of Authorization to Electronically File FBARs — is used when one person files the FBAR on behalf of another. For business accounts, this form is commonly used when a corporation authorizes an officer or an external preparer to file the entity's FBAR electronically through the BSA E-Filing System.

Form 114a does not get filed with FinCEN. Instead, the filer and the person authorized to file must keep the signed Form 114a in their records. If FinCEN or the IRS requests documentation, both parties must be able to produce the signed authorization.

For individual FBAR filers who use a professional preparer, Form 114a authorizes the preparer to file FinCEN Form 114 on the individual's behalf. The individual remains personally responsible for the accuracy of the FBAR filing. The authorization does not transfer the filing obligation — it only authorizes electronic submission.

Corporations, partnerships, and LLCs should maintain a signed Form 114a for each calendar year in which an authorized person files the entity's FBAR. This is particularly important when the person filing the FBAR changes from year to year — such as when a new CFO takes over from a predecessor.

## Consolidated FBAR Filing for Entities

A US entity that is the parent of a group of entities may file a consolidated FBAR that covers the foreign financial accounts of the parent and its subsidiaries. Consolidated FBAR filing simplifies reporting when multiple related entities hold foreign bank accounts.

Under FinCEN guidance, a consolidated FBAR can be filed by the parent entity on behalf of subsidiaries that are US persons. The parent reports all foreign financial accounts held by itself and its qualifying subsidiaries on a single FinCEN Form 114 filed under the parent's EIN.

Consolidated FBAR filing does not eliminate individual filing obligations. Even when a parent entity files a consolidated FBAR, each officer, director, and employee with signatory authority or a financial interest in the reported foreign financial accounts must still evaluate their personal FBAR filing obligation under 31 CFR 1010.350.

Not all entity structures qualify for consolidated filing. The subsidiary must be included in the parent's consolidated financial statements, or the parent must own more than 50% of the subsidiary. Foreign subsidiaries that are not US persons are not required to file FBARs, but their accounts may need to be reported on the US parent's FBAR if the parent has a financial interest.

## Comparison: Financial Interest vs. Signatory Authority

The table below shows the key differences between a financial interest and signatory authority under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). Both create a personal FBAR filing obligation. But they have different legal tests, different exemptions, and different documentation needs. Knowing which applies to you helps you decide whether and how to file.

| Factor | Financial Interest | Signatory Authority |
|--------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Legal basis | 31 CFR 1010.350(e) | 31 CFR 1010.350(f) |
| Key test | 50%+ ownership (direct or indirect) | Authority to control disposition of assets |
| Must you own the account? | Yes or 50%+ of entity owning it | No — employer or entity can own it |
| Exemption available? | No | Yes — for regulated employers (banks, public companies, SEC-registered firms) |
| 25-signer exemption? | No | Yes — if account has 25+ authorized signers |
| Does entity also file? | Yes, separately | Yes, separately |
| FBAR form section | Part II | Part III (unless also financial interest) |
| Penalty exposure | Personal, per violation | Personal, per violation |
| Non-willful penalty | Up to $16,117 per violation (31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)) | Up to $16,117 per violation (31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)) |

## Common Mistakes With Business Account FBAR Reporting

Business account FBAR reporting generates more errors than personal account reporting because the rules involve multiple filers, entity structures, and overlapping obligations. These are the most frequent mistakes the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network sees in FBAR filings related to business accounts.

### Assuming the Company's FBAR Covers You

The most common mistake is assuming that because the company filed its FBAR, you as an officer or employee do not need to file. The Bank Secrecy Act requires each US person with a financial interest or signatory authority to file independently. The entity's FBAR filing does not discharge your personal obligation under 31 USC 5314.

### Ignoring Signatory Authority When You Have No Ownership

Many employees believe that because they own no shares in the company, they have no FBAR obligation. Signatory authority under 31 CFR 1010.350(f) does not require ownership. If you can direct the foreign bank to transfer funds, you must evaluate whether you need to file a personal FBAR.

### Miscalculating the $10,000 Threshold

The $10,000 threshold is based on the aggregate maximum account value of all your foreign financial accounts at any point during the calendar year — not the year-end balance. A corporate foreign bank account that held $15,000 in March but $0 in December still triggered the threshold. The maximum account value for each account is the highest balance during the year.

### Reporting in the Wrong FBAR Section

Officers and employees with signatory authority but no financial interest must report accounts in Part III of FinCEN Form 114, not Part II. Reporting in the wrong section can cause processing errors and trigger follow-up inquiries from FinCEN.

### Failing to File for Each Calendar Year Separately

FBAR filing is an annual obligation. Each calendar year requires a separate FinCEN Form 114. If you had signatory authority over a foreign financial account for three consecutive years, you must file three separate FBARs — one for each calendar year. Missing even one year creates a separate violation with its own potential FBAR penalties.

### Not Converting Foreign Currency Correctly

When reporting the maximum account value on the FBAR, you must convert foreign currency to US dollars using the Treasury Department's end-of-year exchange rate from the Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange. Using the wrong exchange rate or failing to convert altogether is a common error in business account FBAR reporting.

## What Penalties Apply for Missing an FBAR on a Business Account?

If you miss a personal FBAR filing for a foreign business account, FinCEN assesses penalties against you individually — not against the entity. Non-willful violations carry up to $16,117 per missed account per year under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). Willful violations are far more serious. The penalties depend on the type of violation:

- **Non-willful violation**: Up to $16,117 per violation (2025 inflation adjustment) under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321)
- **Willful violation**: The greater of $100,000 or 50% of the account balance at the time of the violation under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321)
- **Criminal penalties**: In egregious cases, up to $500,000 in fines and 10 years imprisonment under [31 USC 5322](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5322)

If you missed prior years, the [FBAR delinquent filing procedures](/blog/fbar-delinquent-filing-procedures) may let you catch up without penalty. For larger gaps, [FBAR streamlined filing compliance procedures](/blog/fbar-streamlined-filing-compliance-procedures) or the [voluntary disclosure practice](/blog/fbar-voluntary-disclosure-practice) may apply. The gap between willful and non-willful treatment is significant — from $16,117 (31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)) to $100,000 or more (31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)). See [FBAR willful vs. non-willful penalties](/blog/fbar-willful-vs-non-willful-penalties).

FBAR penalties apply per account, per year. An officer with signatory authority over three foreign bank accounts who fails to file for two calendar years faces potential non-willful penalties of up to $96,702 (six violations at $16,117 each). Willful penalties would be far higher. The IRS and FinCEN have the authority to assess penalties going back six years under the FBAR statute of limitations.

## Real-World Scenarios

### Scenario 1: CFO With Signatory Authority Over Multiple Accounts

Sarah is the CFO of a privately held US manufacturing company with subsidiaries in Germany and Japan. She is an authorized signatory on two foreign accounts: the German subsidiary's Deutsche Bank account (€150,000, or about $163,000) and the Japanese subsidiary's Sumitomo Mitsui account (¥20,000,000, or about $134,000).

The company is private, so the signatory authority exemption for publicly traded companies does not apply. Sarah must file a personal FBAR listing both accounts. The company also files its own FBAR separately, as required by [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350).

Sarah reports both foreign financial accounts in Part III of her FinCEN Form 114 because she has signatory authority but no financial interest. She lists the maximum account value for each account during the calendar year, converting each to US dollars using the Treasury Department's exchange rate.

### Scenario 2: Minority Partner Without Signatory Authority

David owns 25% of a US-registered partnership with a Canadian operating account holding C$80,000 (about $58,000). He has no authority to sign on the account or direct transfers.

David does not meet the 50%+ ownership threshold for a financial interest under [31 CFR 1010.350(e)(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). He also lacks signatory authority. David has no personal FBAR obligation. The partnership files its own FBAR.

### Scenario 3: Accounts Payable Employee With Limited Access

Linda works in accounts payable at a US retailer. She can submit payment instructions to the company's UK subsidiary account. All transactions above $5,000 require a manager's digital approval before execution. She cannot initiate or approve transactions on her own.

Linda cannot independently control the disposition of the account's assets. Her access requires another person's approval before execution. She lacks signatory authority and has no personal FBAR obligation for the UK account.

### Scenario 4: Majority Owner of LLC With Foreign Account

Robert is the sole member of a single-member LLC that holds a foreign bank account in Singapore with a maximum account value of $85,000 during the calendar year. Because the LLC is disregarded for tax purposes, Robert reports the Singapore account on his personal FBAR as if he held it directly. The disregarded LLC does not file a separate FBAR. Robert has a financial interest in the foreign financial account and reports it in Part II of his FinCEN Form 114.

### Scenario 5: Officer at Large Corporation With 25+ Signers

Patricia is a Vice President at a Fortune 500 corporation. She is one of 40 authorized signers on the company's primary foreign operating account at HSBC Hong Kong. She has no ownership interest in the corporation. The corporation files its own FBAR reporting the HSBC account. Because the account has more than 25 authorized signers and the entity has filed its FBAR, Patricia qualifies for the 25-signer exemption under 31 CFR 1010.350(g)(5). She does not need to file a personal FBAR for that account.

## Do Signatory Authority Accounts Go on Form 8938?

No — signatory authority accounts do not go on [FATCA Form 8938](/blog/fbar-vs-fatca-form-8938-differences). Form 8938 covers specified foreign financial assets in which you have a financial interest. It does not apply to accounts where you have signatory authority but no financial interest. This is a key difference between the FBAR form (FinCEN Form 114) and Form 8938. In contrast, FBAR requires reporting based on signatory authority alone.

If you have a financial interest through 50%+ ownership, you may need to report that account on both forms — but different thresholds apply. FBAR requires reporting when the account exceeds $10,000 per [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306). Form 8938 starts at $50,000 for domestic single filers on the last day of the year under [26 USC 6038D](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D).

The Internal Revenue Service administers Form 8938 as part of the FATCA (Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act) regime, while FinCEN administers the FBAR under the Bank Secrecy Act. The two forms have different filing thresholds, different due dates, and different penalties. Filers with both a financial interest and signatory authority in foreign financial accounts should evaluate both filing requirements independently.

## Frequently Asked Questions

These are the questions most commonly asked about FBAR obligations for business accounts and signatory authority. Each answer cites the relevant statute or regulation and links to related articles for further reading. If your situation is not covered here, consult a qualified tax professional or [start your filing with FBAR Direct](/pricing).

**What does FBAR business account signatory authority mean?**

FBAR business account signatory authority means you can control the disposition of assets in a foreign financial account owned by a business entity — by direct contact with the bank. This definition comes from [31 CFR 1010.350(f)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). If you are an authorized signer and can start or approve transactions on your own, you have signatory authority. You may need to file a personal FBAR for those accounts.

**Does signatory authority over a foreign account always require an FBAR filing?**

Not always. Under [31 CFR 1010.350(f)(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350), officers and employees of banks, SEC-registered firms, and publicly traded US companies may be exempt. The exemption applies if you have no financial interest in the account. If you work for a private company, no exemption applies. See [FBAR willful vs. non-willful penalties](/blog/fbar-willful-vs-non-willful-penalties) for what is at stake.

**If my company files its own FBAR, do I still need to file personally?**

Yes. The company and each individual with a financial interest or qualifying signatory authority file separately. The company's filing does not cover your personal obligation under [31 USC 5314](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5314). FinCEN treats entity-level and individual-level filings as separate.

**I own 40% of an LLC with a foreign account. Do I need to file an FBAR?**

At 40%, you do not meet the 50%+ financial interest threshold under [31 CFR 1010.350(e)(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). However, if you are also an authorized signatory on the account, the signatory authority prong creates a filing obligation — unless you qualify for an exemption because the LLC is a regulated entity.

**What is the FBAR threshold for signatory authority accounts?**

The FBAR threshold is $10,000 — the sum of all qualifying foreign accounts at any point during the year — under [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306). If the account held $50,000 on March 1st and $0 on December 31st, you still crossed the threshold. The same $10,000 limit applies to all FBAR filers. See our [FBAR first-time filer guide](/blog/fbar-first-time-filer-guide) for more detail.

**What if I was unaware of my FBAR obligation as an employee?**

FinCEN may treat the violation as non-willful if you have a credible explanation. Non-willful penalties run up to $16,117 per violation under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). The [FBAR reasonable cause defense](/blog/fbar-reasonable-cause-defense) can eliminate penalties if you show reasonable cause and no willful neglect. Filing late is far better than not filing.

**What is FinCEN Form 114a and do I need it?**

FinCEN Form 114a is the Record of Authorization to Electronically File FBARs. You need Form 114a when someone else files your FBAR on your behalf — such as a tax preparer or an authorized officer filing for a corporation. Form 114a is not filed with FinCEN but must be kept in your records. Both the filer and the authorized person should retain a signed copy for each calendar year.

**Can a partnership file a consolidated FBAR for all its partners?**

No. A partnership files its own entity-level FBAR reporting the foreign financial accounts it holds. Each individual partner with a financial interest (more than 50% ownership) or signatory authority over the partnership's foreign bank accounts must file a separate personal FBAR. Consolidated FBAR filing is available for parent-subsidiary relationships, not for partnerships and their individual partners.

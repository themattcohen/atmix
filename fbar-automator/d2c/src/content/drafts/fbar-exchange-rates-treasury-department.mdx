# FBAR Exchange Rate Treasury: How to Convert Foreign Currency to USD

The FBAR exchange rate treasury rule is clear: the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) requires you to use the U.S. Treasury Department's year-end exchange rate to convert foreign financial account balances to USD on the Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts. Under the Bank Secrecy Act (BSA), every U.S. person with aggregate foreign financial accounts exceeding the $10,000 threshold must file FinCEN Form 114. All foreign currency balances must appear in U.S. dollars as the reporting currency on that form.

The U.S. Treasury year end exchange rates appear at [fiscal.treasury.gov](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/reports-statements/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/current.html) under the Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange section. These FBAR exchange rates cover most major currencies worldwide. This article explains how to find the correct currency exchange rate, apply it to your peak balance, and what to do when your foreign currency is not listed.

Getting the exchange rate right protects you from Internal Revenue Service questions and potential FBAR penalties. Under [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306), you report the maximum account value in USD. A rate error — even an honest one — may trigger an IRS notice asking you to justify your reported values on the Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts.

Every U.S. citizen, U.S. resident, and other U.S. person who holds a financial interest in or signature authority over foreign bank accounts must understand how currency conversion works for FBAR reporting. The exchange rate you select directly affects whether your aggregate value crosses the $10,000 threshold and what dollar amount appears on your FinCEN Form 114 filing.

## Which FBAR Exchange Rate Treasury Source Should You Use?

FinCEN requires the U.S. Treasury Fiscal Data year-end exchange rate for the calendar year you are reporting. These FBAR exchange rate treasury figures appear on the Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange page and reflect the December 31 rate for each reporting year. For most major currencies — GBP, EUR, CAD, AUD, JPY, and CHF — the Department of the Treasury publishes a December 31 rate you apply directly to your foreign financial account balance.

The Treasury exchange rate differs from a bank rate or mid-market rate. It is the rate used by federal agencies for financial reporting. Bank currency conversion rates include retail spreads, so they often differ from Treasury figures. Always use the Treasury rate for your FBAR filing, not your bank's quoted rate.

The Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange are published quarterly by the Bureau of the Fiscal Service, a division of the Department of the Treasury. The December 31 rate is the one relevant to FBAR reporting because FinCEN uses the calendar year as the reporting period under the Bank Secrecy Act.

### How to Find the December 31 Treasury Rate

Follow these steps each filing year to locate the correct FBAR exchange rate treasury figure:

1. Go to [fiscal.treasury.gov/reports-statements/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/reports-statements/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/current.html).
2. Download the report dated December 31 for the calendar year you are filing (for example, December 31, 2025 for a 2025 FBAR).
3. Find your foreign currency in the table. Treasury lists rates as foreign currency units per one U.S. dollar.
4. Divide your foreign-currency balance by the listed rate. If the GBP rate is 0.7937, divide your pound balance by 0.7937 to get US dollars.
5. Save the URL and the exact exchange rate. Keep this record for at least six years in case of an IRS examination.

Each step takes only a few minutes. The Treasury website organizes rates by quarter, so scroll to the Q4 report that covers December 31. The fiscal.treasury.gov site also offers downloadable CSV files for bulk currency conversion needs.

### Understanding the Rate Format

Treasury reporting rates appear as foreign currency units per USD. A GBP exchange rate of 0.7937 means £0.7937 equals $1.00. To convert pounds to US dollars, divide your GBP balance by 0.7937.

Many filers reverse this and multiply instead of dividing. The math error is large: £50,000 ÷ 0.7937 = $63,000, but £50,000 × 0.7937 = $39,685. Multiplying would significantly underreport the account value on your FBAR filing. Double-check your arithmetic before filing.

Understanding the rate format is essential for accurate FBAR reporting. When the Treasury rate is less than 1.0 (as with GBP, EUR, and CHF), you divide to get a larger USD number. When the rate is greater than 1.0 (as with JPY, INR, and MXN), you still divide — but the USD result is smaller than the foreign currency figure.

## Where to Find Official Treasury Department Exchange Rates

The official source for FBAR exchange rates is the fiscal.treasury.gov website maintained by the Bureau of the Fiscal Service within the U.S. Treasury. This is the only source the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network recognizes as the primary authority for currency conversion on the Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts.

The Department of the Treasury publishes the Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange for approximately 170 foreign currencies. These rates serve multiple federal purposes, but FBAR filers use them specifically for converting foreign financial account balances to US dollars.

You can access the rates directly at the Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange page. The site provides current quarter rates and an archive of historical rates going back several years.

### Navigating the fiscal.treasury.gov Website

When you visit fiscal.treasury.gov, navigate to the "Reports & Statements" section and select "Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange." The page displays the current quarter's exchange rates by default.

To find the correct rate for your FBAR filing, select the December 31 report for the relevant calendar year. If you are filing a 2025 FBAR, you need the December 31, 2025 rate. The rates are organized alphabetically by country name, making it straightforward to locate your foreign currency.

Each entry shows the country name, the currency name, the foreign currency amount equal to one US dollar, and the effective date. Copy the rate exactly as published — do not round or adjust it. Rounding can introduce errors that compound when applied to large foreign bank account balances.

### Historical Treasury Exchange Rates

If you need to file a late FBAR or amend a prior-year filing, you must use the historical Treasury exchange rate from the applicable calendar year. The fiscal.treasury.gov website maintains an archive of past Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange.

Historical rates are available for multiple prior years. Navigate to the archive section on the Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange page to access December 31 rates from previous calendar years. For very old filings, you may need to contact the Bureau of the Fiscal Service if rates are not available online.

When filing a delinquent FBAR through FinCEN's delinquent filing procedures, the same exchange rate rules apply. You use the December 31 Treasury rate from the calendar year the FBAR covers, not the year you actually file. This distinction matters because exchange rates fluctuate from year to year.

Tax professionals preparing FBAR filings for multiple clients often download the full December 31 rate table as a CSV file. This allows batch currency conversion across many foreign financial accounts without manually looking up each exchange rate.

## How to Convert Foreign Currency for FBAR Reporting

The currency conversion process for FBAR reporting follows a specific formula. Every U.S. person with foreign bank accounts must convert the maximum account value from the local foreign currency to US dollars using the Treasury Department exchange rate.

The conversion formula is:

**Maximum Account Value in Foreign Currency ÷ Treasury Exchange Rate = USD Value for FBAR**

This formula applies to every foreign financial account you report on FinCEN Form 114. The exchange rate is always the December 31 rate from the Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange for the calendar year you are reporting.

Before performing the conversion, gather two pieces of information: the highest balance your foreign bank account reached during the entire calendar year, and the December 31 Treasury exchange rate for that foreign currency. Both values are necessary for an accurate FBAR filing.

### Step-by-Step Currency Conversion Example

Here is a complete example showing how to convert a foreign currency balance to USD for your FBAR:

**Scenario:** Maria is a U.S. citizen living in the United States with a savings account at Deutsche Bank in Germany. She needs to report this foreign bank account on her FBAR.

**Step 1: Find the maximum account value.** Maria reviews her monthly bank statements for the entire calendar year. Her balance peaked at EUR 45,000 in June when she received an inheritance deposit.

**Step 2: Look up the Treasury exchange rate.** Maria visits fiscal.treasury.gov and navigates to the Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange. She finds the December 31 EUR rate: 0.9705 EUR per USD.

**Step 3: Perform the currency conversion.** Maria divides her peak balance by the exchange rate: EUR 45,000 ÷ 0.9705 = **$46,368 USD**.

**Step 4: Enter the USD value on FinCEN Form 114.** Maria reports $46,368 as the maximum account value for this foreign financial account on her FBAR filing.

**Step 5: Document the exchange rate source.** Maria saves a screenshot of the fiscal.treasury.gov page showing the December 31 EUR rate. She keeps this documentation for at least six years.

This five-step process applies to every foreign bank account and foreign financial account you report. The reporting currency on the FBAR is always US dollars, regardless of which foreign currency the account holds.

## How Do You Apply the FBAR Exchange Rate Treasury Rate to Your Peak Balance?

You apply the December 31 Treasury exchange rate to the highest foreign financial account balance during the reporting year, per [31 CFR 1010.306(d)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306). The FBAR asks for the maximum value — the peak balance at any point during the calendar year — not the December 31 balance. You use the year-end exchange rate even though the peak may have occurred months earlier.

This rule surprises many filers. Your foreign bank account may have peaked in March and dropped by December. However, you still report the March peak value converted at the December 31 rate. The rate timing and the balance timing are separate under Bank Secrecy Act regulations.

Understanding this distinction is critical for accurate FBAR reporting. The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network chose the year-end rate as a standardized benchmark. This approach simplifies the filing process because you use one exchange rate per foreign currency per calendar year, rather than tracking daily rates for each account.

### Example: UK Bank Account

Sarah holds a Barclays current account. Her balance peaked at £50,000 in March after receiving a property settlement. By December 31, the balance was £22,000.

- Maximum value: £50,000 (March peak)
- Treasury December 31, 2025 GBP rate: 0.7937 GBP per USD
- USD conversion: £50,000 ÷ 0.7937 = **$63,000**

Sarah reports $63,000 on her FBAR — not $22,000 based on December 31. This foreign bank account balance converted to US dollars exceeds the $10,000 threshold on its own. See [FBAR UK Bank Accounts](/blog/fbar-uk-bank-accounts) for more on reporting British accounts.

### Example: Japanese Yen Account

Carlos holds a Mitsubishi UFJ account. His balance peaked at ¥8,500,000 in August.

- Maximum value: ¥8,500,000 (August peak)
- Treasury December 31, 2025 JPY rate: 157.12 JPY per USD
- USD conversion: ¥8,500,000 ÷ 157.12 = **$54,100**

Carlos reports $54,100. The yen weakened by December, but the December 31 exchange rate still applies regardless. This foreign financial account value in US dollars must appear on his FinCEN Form 114.

### Example: Canadian Dollar Account

David is a U.S. resident with a TD Canada Trust chequing account. His balance peaked at CAD 35,000 in October.

- Maximum value: CAD 35,000 (October peak)
- Treasury December 31, 2025 CAD rate: 1.4419 CAD per USD
- USD conversion: CAD 35,000 ÷ 1.4419 = **$24,273**

David reports $24,273 as the maximum account value. Because the CAD exchange rate is greater than 1.0, the USD equivalent is smaller than the Canadian dollar amount. For Canadian retirement accounts, see [FBAR Canada RRSP and TFSA Reporting](/blog/fbar-canada-rrsp-tfsa-reporting).

## Year-End Rate vs Maximum Value Date Rate

A common question in FBAR filing is whether to use the exchange rate from the date the account reached its maximum value or the December 31 year-end rate. The answer under the Bank Secrecy Act is unambiguous: use the December 31 Treasury exchange rate regardless of when the peak balance occurred.

This means the currency exchange rate on the date of your highest balance is irrelevant for FBAR purposes. Even if the exchange rate was dramatically different in March when your account peaked versus December 31, you apply only the year-end rate from the Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange.

The IRS and FinCEN enforce this rule consistently. There is no provision in [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306) allowing filers to select an alternative date for the exchange rate. Using the wrong date's rate — even if it comes from the Treasury Department — is a reporting error.

### Why FinCEN Uses the December 31 Rate

The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network standardized on the December 31 rate for practical reasons. Requiring filers to match each account's peak date with a date-specific exchange rate would create significant compliance burdens. A U.S. person with five foreign bank accounts peaking on different dates would need five different exchange rates.

The calendar year-end rate provides a single, verifiable benchmark. Every filer converting GBP for a given reporting year uses the same Treasury exchange rate. This uniformity makes it easier for the Internal Revenue Service to verify FBAR filings during examinations.

The year-end approach also aligns with how Treasury publishes the Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange. The December 31 rate is always available in the Q4 report, providing a consistent reference point for all FBAR reporting.

## What If Treasury Doesn't Publish a Rate for Your Currency?

When the Department of the Treasury does not publish a year-end exchange rate for your foreign currency, use a reasonable rate from another recognized source. FinCEN does not require a specific backup source. However, the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network expects you to document your method clearly for any IRS examination.

Accepted alternative sources include:

- **OANDA Historical Rates** (oanda.com) — widely used by tax professionals, downloadable for any specific date
- **XE.com Historical Rates** (xe.com) — free lookup for historical December 31 exchange rates
- **Federal Reserve H.10 Statistical Release** — covers more currencies than the Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange, published weekly
- **Wall Street Journal published rates** — acceptable for currencies not on the Treasury Department's list

Save a PDF of the rate page you used. Record the URL, the exchange rate, and the access date. This documentation demonstrates a good-faith effort to find a reliable rate for your FBAR filing.

The key requirement is reasonableness. The Internal Revenue Service will not penalize a U.S. person who uses a reputable third-party exchange rate when the Treasury Department does not publish one. However, you cannot use a rate that is clearly favorable to underreport the account value in US dollars.

## Common Currency Conversion Mistakes on FBAR

Currency conversion errors are among the most frequent mistakes on FBAR filings. Each of the following mistakes can produce an inaccurate account value on your FinCEN Form 114, potentially triggering IRS inquiries or FBAR penalties.

### Multiplying Instead of Dividing

The most common currency conversion error is multiplying the foreign currency balance by the Treasury exchange rate instead of dividing. Because Treasury rates are expressed as foreign currency units per one US dollar, dividing is the correct operation.

For example, if you hold EUR 50,000 and the Treasury exchange rate is 0.9705 EUR per USD, the correct conversion is EUR 50,000 ÷ 0.9705 = $51,520. Multiplying EUR 50,000 × 0.9705 = $48,525 — an underreporting of nearly $3,000. For currencies like GBP and CHF where the rate is less than 1.0, the error is even more pronounced.

Always verify your arithmetic: for currencies with an exchange rate below 1.0, the USD value should be larger than the foreign currency amount. For currencies with a rate above 1.0, the USD value should be smaller.

### Using a Bank or Google Rate

Some filers use Google search results, their bank's posted exchange rate, or an ATM rate instead of the official Treasury exchange rate. Bank rates and online search rates include retail spreads, fees, and timing differences that make them unsuitable for FBAR reporting.

The Treasury Department exchange rate is the only rate FinCEN recognizes for the Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts. Using a different rate creates a discrepancy if the IRS cross-checks your FBAR filing against Treasury rate calculations. Document that you used the fiscal.treasury.gov rate to avoid questions during an examination.

### Applying the Wrong Year's Rate

Another frequent mistake is using last year's exchange rate or a current-day rate instead of the December 31 rate from the correct calendar year. Exchange rates can shift significantly between years. The EUR rate, for example, may move from 0.9705 to 0.9400 between December 2024 and December 2025.

Always verify the date on the Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange page matches the calendar year you are filing for. A 2025 FBAR uses the December 31, 2025 Treasury exchange rate — not a 2024 or 2026 rate.

### Forgetting to Convert at All

A small number of filers enter the raw foreign currency amount on FinCEN Form 114 without performing any currency conversion. The FBAR requires all values in US dollars as the reporting currency. Entering £50,000 instead of the USD equivalent of $63,000 is an error that will be flagged.

## Can Using the Wrong Rate Lead to Penalties?

Yes — a rate error that produces an inaccurate FBAR value is a filing deficiency under the IRS and FinCEN rules. Non-willful FBAR violations carry FBAR penalties up to $16,117 per violation under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). Willful violations carry the greater of $100,000 or 50% of the account balance under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). See [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306) for the currency conversion rule.

An honest rate error using a reasonable source is unlikely to trigger willful FBAR penalties. Nevertheless, using a current-day rate instead of the December 31 exchange rate — or using your bank's retail rate without documentation — creates unnecessary risk. Document your source every year, regardless of which exchange rate you use.

The distinction between willful and non-willful FBAR penalties matters for exchange rate errors. A U.S. person who makes a good-faith effort to use the correct Treasury exchange rate but makes a mathematical error faces a far lower penalty risk than someone who deliberately uses a favorable rate to underreport foreign financial account values.

## Aggregating Multiple Accounts in Different Currencies

When you hold foreign bank accounts in more than one country, you must convert each account's maximum value to US dollars separately, then add all USD values together. This aggregate value determines whether you meet the $10,000 threshold for FBAR filing under the Bank Secrecy Act.

For example, if you hold a GBP account worth $6,000 in USD and a EUR account worth $5,000 in USD, your aggregate value is $11,000. Both accounts must be reported on your FBAR because the combined total exceeds the $10,000 threshold — even though neither individual account crosses $10,000 on its own.

Each foreign financial account uses the December 31 Treasury exchange rate for its specific foreign currency. You cannot use a single exchange rate for accounts in different currencies. A U.S. person with accounts in pounds, euros, and yen performs three separate currency conversions using three different Treasury rates.

### How to Determine Whether You Meet the $10,000 Threshold

The $10,000 FBAR filing threshold is based on the aggregate value of all your foreign financial accounts at any point during the calendar year. Follow these steps:

1. For each foreign bank account or foreign financial account, determine the maximum value in the local foreign currency during the calendar year.
2. Convert each maximum value to US dollars using the December 31 Treasury exchange rate for that currency.
3. Add all USD maximum values together. If the total exceeds $10,000 at any point during the year, you must file FinCEN Form 114.

The aggregate value calculation includes all types of foreign financial accounts: bank accounts, securities accounts, mutual funds, and certain insurance policies with cash value. A U.S. citizen or U.S. resident who holds a financial interest in these accounts — or who has signature authority over them — must include them in the aggregation.

The $10,000 threshold is not per account. It applies to the combined maximum values across all foreign bank accounts and other foreign financial accounts in which you have a financial interest.

## How Do You Handle Multi-Currency Accounts?

Multi-currency foreign financial accounts require you to convert each currency separately using the December 31 Treasury exchange rate for that currency, then total all USD equivalents to determine the account's maximum value. This applies to multi-currency accounts at fintech banks like Wise or Revolut and to international brokerage accounts holding balances in more than one foreign currency simultaneously.

For example, an account holding EUR 20,000 and GBP 15,000 at its peak requires two currency conversions:

- EUR 20,000 ÷ [EUR Treasury rate] = USD equivalent of the EUR portion
- GBP 15,000 ÷ [GBP Treasury rate] = USD equivalent of the GBP portion
- Total USD maximum value = both amounts added together

Report the single combined total on your FBAR. You do not list each foreign currency on a separate line within the same account on FinCEN Form 114.

Determining the peak balance for a multi-currency account can be complex. The maximum account value is the highest combined USD equivalent on any single day during the calendar year. You may need to convert balances for multiple dates to identify which day produced the highest total in US dollars.

### Accounts in India: NRE and NRO

Indian NRE and NRO accounts held in rupees use the INR December 31 Treasury exchange rate. The Department of the Treasury typically publishes the INR rate, but verify annually since coverage can change. For details on Indian foreign bank account reporting, see [FBAR India NRE and NRO Accounts](/blog/fbar-india-nre-nro-accounts).

Both NRE and NRO accounts are foreign financial accounts for FBAR purposes. A U.S. person with a financial interest in either type must include the account value in their aggregate total when determining whether they meet the $10,000 threshold.

## Exchange Rate Rules for Joint Foreign Accounts

Joint foreign bank accounts have specific rules under FBAR reporting. When two or more U.S. persons hold a financial interest in the same foreign financial account, each person must file a separate FBAR reporting the full maximum account value — not half or a proportional share.

For example, if a married couple jointly holds a foreign bank account with a maximum value of GBP 80,000, each spouse converts the full GBP 80,000 using the December 31 Treasury exchange rate. Each spouse reports the full USD equivalent on their individual FinCEN Form 114.

This rule applies regardless of ownership percentages. Even if one spouse contributed 90% of the funds, both report the full maximum account value in US dollars on their respective FBAR filings.

For joint accounts held with a non-U.S. person, only the U.S. person files the FBAR. The U.S. citizen or U.S. resident reports the full account value converted at the Treasury exchange rate. The non-U.S. account holder has no FBAR filing obligation under the Bank Secrecy Act.

The currency conversion process for joint accounts is identical to individual accounts: use the December 31 Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange from fiscal.treasury.gov, divide the foreign currency maximum value by the exchange rate, and report the result in US dollars.

## FBAR Exchange Rates vs IRS Tax Return Rates

The FBAR exchange rate treasury rules differ from the exchange rate rules used on your federal income tax return (Form 1040). Understanding these differences prevents confusion when you file both forms in the same tax season.

On the FBAR (FinCEN Form 114), you always use the December 31 Treasury exchange rate from the Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange, applied to the maximum account value during the calendar year. The reporting currency is US dollars, and the rate source is fiscal.treasury.gov.

On Form 1040 and related IRS schedules, the Internal Revenue Service generally accepts the exchange rate on the date of each transaction. If you received foreign income on March 15, you convert that income to US dollars using the exchange rate on March 15 — not the year-end rate. The IRS publishes yearly average exchange rates at [irs.gov](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/yearly-average-currency-exchange-rates) for convenience, but transaction-date rates are also acceptable.

### Key Differences Between FBAR and Form 1040 Currency Reporting

| Feature | FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) | Form 1040 (IRS Tax Return) |
|---------|------------------------|---------------------------|
| Exchange rate date | December 31 only | Transaction date or yearly average |
| Rate source | Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange | IRS rates or any reputable source |
| What is converted | Maximum account value | Income, gains, and deductions |
| Filing authority | Financial Crimes Enforcement Network | Internal Revenue Service |
| Legal basis | Bank Secrecy Act, [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306) | [26 USC 6038D](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D) and IRS regulations |
| Dollar threshold | $10,000 aggregate | Varies by form |

The distinction matters for U.S. persons who file both an FBAR and a tax return reporting foreign income. The same foreign bank account may produce different USD figures on each form because the exchange rates and the amounts being converted differ.

Form 8938 (FATCA) reporting under [26 USC 6038D](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D) uses the same December 31 Treasury exchange rate as the FBAR. However, Form 8938 has different filing thresholds starting at $50,000 for domestic filers. See [FBAR vs FATCA Form 8938](/blog/fbar-vs-fatca-form-8938-differences) for a full comparison.

## How to Calculate Your Maximum Account Value Step by Step

Calculating the correct USD maximum value for a foreign financial account involves three clear steps. Review your full-year statements before starting — the peak balance can occur on any day of the calendar year, not only at month-end or year-end.

1. **Find the highest balance in foreign currency.** Review monthly statements or the full transaction history. Look for the single highest daily balance during the calendar year. This is the maximum account value you will convert.
2. **Convert using the December 31 Treasury exchange rate.** Go to [fiscal.treasury.gov](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/reports-statements/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/current.html) and find your foreign currency. Divide the peak foreign-currency balance by the Treasury exchange rate to get the USD equivalent.
3. **Document your source.** Record the fiscal.treasury.gov URL, the exchange rate used, and the date you accessed it. For alternative-source currencies, save a PDF showing the rate and date.

These three steps apply to every foreign bank account and foreign financial account you hold. A U.S. person with multiple foreign financial accounts repeats this process for each account before aggregating the USD values.

For more detail on the maximum value calculation, see [How to Calculate Maximum Account Value for FBAR](/blog/how-to-calculate-maximum-account-value-fbar).

## Frequently Asked Questions

These questions address the most common points of confusion about FBAR exchange rates. Each answer is a stand-alone summary you can use to verify your own currency conversion approach for FBAR reporting.

**Do I use the exchange rate on the date my balance peaked, or the FBAR exchange rate treasury December 31 rate?**

You use the Treasury December 31 exchange rate for the reporting calendar year, regardless of when the peak balance occurred. Under [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306), the rule is the year-end rate applied to the maximum annual value. Therefore, if your Swiss franc account peaked in July, convert that July balance using the December 31 CHF rate for that same year.

**What if my currency is not listed on the Treasury website?**

Use an exchange rate from a recognized source such as OANDA, XE.com, or the Federal Reserve H.10 release. Document the source, the rate, and the date you accessed it. Keep this for at least six years in case of IRS review. This record shows you made a good-faith effort to find a reliable exchange rate rather than guessing.

**Can I use my bank's exchange rate instead of the Treasury rate?**

No. The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network requires the Treasury Department's year-end exchange rate for FBAR purposes. Your bank's currency conversion rate includes retail spreads and may differ from the Treasury figure. Using a bank rate may create a discrepancy if the Internal Revenue Service compares your reported values against Treasury-rate calculations during an examination.

**What if I held the foreign bank account for only part of the year?**

The December 31 exchange rate still applies even if you closed the account before year-end. You report the maximum value the foreign financial account held while open, converted at the December 31 Treasury rate for that calendar year. This applies to accounts closed mid-year. See [FBAR First-Time Filer Guide](/blog/fbar-first-time-filer-guide) for more on partial-year accounts.

**Does the FBAR exchange rate treasury rule differ from Form 8938 rules?**

Both the FBAR and Form 8938 (FATCA) under [26 USC 6038D](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D) use the Treasury year-end exchange rate. However, they have different dollar thresholds. The FBAR threshold is $10,000 in aggregate across all foreign financial accounts under [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306). Form 8938 thresholds start at $50,000 for domestic filers. See [FBAR vs FATCA Form 8938](/blog/fbar-vs-fatca-form-8938-differences) for a full comparison.

**Do foreign retirement accounts use the same exchange rate rules?**

Yes. Foreign pension and retirement accounts reportable on the FBAR follow the same December 31 Treasury exchange rate rule. This includes Canadian RRSPs, Australian retirement funds, and Israeli pension funds. The maximum account value is the highest balance during the calendar year, converted at the December 31 rate. Additionally, the $10,000 aggregate threshold applies across all such foreign financial accounts.

**What exchange rate do I use for a foreign life insurance policy with cash value?**

A foreign life insurance policy with a cash surrender value is a foreign financial account for FBAR reporting purposes. Use the December 31 Treasury exchange rate to convert the cash value from the local foreign currency to US dollars. The maximum account value is the highest cash surrender value during the calendar year, reported in US dollars as the reporting currency on FinCEN Form 114.

**How do I report an account that changed currencies during the year?**

If a foreign bank account converted from one foreign currency to another during the calendar year (for example, from EUR to GBP), determine the maximum value for each currency period separately. Convert each peak using the December 31 Treasury exchange rate for that foreign currency, and report whichever USD equivalent is higher as the maximum account value on your FBAR filing.

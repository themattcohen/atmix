# FBAR Canada RRSP TFSA Reporting: What US Persons Must Know

About one million US citizens live in Canada. Millions more hold Canadian accounts — RRSP, TFSA, RRIF, or RESP — while living in the US. All of these accounts are FBAR-reportable if your total foreign balances top $10,000 at any point during the year.

The FBAR is the Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts (FinCEN Form 114). You file it under [31 USC 5314](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5314) and [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). The rules apply even if you report those accounts on your Canadian return. The FBAR is a US Treasury duty — separate from Canadian law.

Canadian registered accounts add complexity to US filing. The RRSP and RRIF benefit from treaty deferral under the US-Canada Tax Treaty. However, the TFSA is fully taxable for US persons, despite being tax-free in Canada. Additionally, the RESP may trigger Form 3520 as a potential foreign trust. Each account type has different rules, and getting them right matters.

## Do I Need to Report Canadian RRSP on FBAR?

Yes. Your Canadian RRSP is a foreign financial account under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). You must report it on the FBAR if your aggregate foreign account balances top $10,000 at any point during the year. The US-Canada Tax Treaty defers US income tax on RRSP gains — but the treaty does not eliminate the FBAR obligation under [31 USC 5314](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5314).

These accounts require FBAR reporting:

- **RRSP** (Registered Retirement Savings Plan) — tax-deferred Canadian retirement account
- **RRIF** (Registered Retirement Income Fund) — the drawdown phase of an RRSP
- **TFSA** (Tax-Free Savings Account) — Canadian tax-free account, but NOT US tax-free
- **RESP** (Registered Education Savings Plan) — may also require Form 3520
- **LIRA** (Locked-In Retirement Account) — locked-in RRSP funds from a pension plan transfer
- **Standard Canadian chequing, savings, and investment accounts** at Canadian banks

### What Makes a Canadian Account Reportable?

A "financial account" under the Bank Secrecy Act (BSA) includes bank accounts and brokerage accounts where you have a financial interest or signature authority. The FBAR instructions (FinCEN Form 114 instructions) define "financial interest" broadly — it includes any account you own, control, or from which you receive benefit. Canadian registered accounts qualify because a Canadian financial institution — RBC, TD Bank, Scotiabank, BMO, or CIBC — holds them. Canadian law may treat these accounts differently for tax purposes, but the BSA definition controls for FBAR.

The $10,000 threshold under [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306) is aggregate. Therefore, if your RRSP holds CAD $8,000 and your TFSA holds CAD $4,000, you must report both — even though each account is individually below $10,000. Convert to USD using the [Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/reports-statements/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/current.html) for December 31 of the filing year.

### Accounts That Are NOT Reportable

The FBAR covers financial accounts — not all Canadian programs. These are generally not reportable on their own:

- Canada Pension Plan (CPP) — a government pension program, not a financial account
- Old Age Security (OAS) — a government benefit, not an account you hold
- Provincial pension plans — generally not held at a financial institution in your name

If you are unsure whether a specific account qualifies, treat it as reportable. Consult a cross-border tax professional before leaving it off.

## How to Report Canadian TFSA on US Tax Return

The TFSA is tax-free in Canada — but US persons must report and pay tax on all TFSA income annually. The US does not recognize Canada's tax-free treatment. Income inside a TFSA is taxable to the US holder in the year earned. Report it on your Form 1040 each year, regardless of withdrawals.

The US-Canada Tax Treaty (Article XVIII) grants deferral to RRSPs and RRIFs. It does not extend to TFSAs. The IRS has not issued guidance giving treaty-based deferral to TFSAs. This is a common mistake among dual US-Canada citizens.

Here is what you must do with a TFSA as a US person:

- **Report the TFSA on your FBAR** each year you meet the $10,000 aggregate threshold
- **Report interest on Schedule B** of your Form 1040 each year
- **Report dividends on Schedule B** of your Form 1040 each year
- **Report capital gains on Schedule D** of your Form 1040 each year
- **Consider Form 3520** if the TFSA is structured as a trust arrangement

Contributing to a TFSA is not tax-efficient for US persons. The tax-free growth that Canadians enjoy does not carry over to US tax law. Consequently, consider this before adding more to a TFSA.

## How Does the US-Canada Tax Treaty Affect RRSP Reporting?

The US-Canada Tax Treaty (Article XVIII) lets US persons defer US income tax on gains inside an RRSP or RRIF. You do not pay US tax on interest, dividends, or capital gains that accumulate inside the plan. You pay tax only when you withdraw. This is the treaty deferral.

Before 2014, you had to file Form 8891 each year for this deferral. The IRS dropped Form 8891 via [Rev. Proc. 2014-55](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2014-44_IRB#RP-2014-55). The election is now automatic. You do not need any form to claim deferral on your RRSP or RRIF.

### What the Treaty Deferral Covers

The deferral applies to income and gains inside the RRSP or RRIF. You defer these until distribution, but you still must:

1. Report the RRSP or RRIF on your FBAR each year the aggregate threshold is met
2. File your US income tax return (Form 1040) each year
3. Report RRSP or RRIF distributions as ordinary income in the year you receive them
4. File Form 8938 (FATCA) if your RRSP and other foreign assets exceed the FATCA thresholds under [26 USC 6038D](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D) — Form 8938 is separate from FBAR Form 114

The treaty deferral does NOT eliminate the FBAR obligation. The FBAR is a Bank Secrecy Act requirement — no tax treaty overrides the BSA. Therefore, you file FBAR Form 114 and claim the treaty deferral separately. For more on how FBAR and FATCA differ, see our guide to [FBAR vs Form 8938 differences](/blog/fbar-vs-fatca-form-8938-differences).

### RRIF Reporting After Conversion

At age 71, Canadians convert their RRSP to an RRIF. The RRIF replaces the RRSP on your FBAR. It is a new account with its own balance and its own line on FinCEN Form 114. Treaty deferral still applies inside the RRIF. Your annual minimum draws are taxable in the US in the year paid. Canada's 15% withholding tax on RRIF draws may offset your US tax bill.

## FBAR Canada RRSP TFSA Maximum Value Calculation

The FBAR requires the maximum value of each account during the calendar year — not the year-end balance. Find the highest balance at any point during the year. Then convert to USD.

Here is the process:

1. **Find the highest balance** in each account during the year. For an RRSP with market fluctuations, check quarterly or monthly statements.
2. **Apply the December 31 Treasury rate** for CAD to USD. Use the [Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/reports-statements/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/current.html) published each quarter-end.
3. **Convert each maximum CAD balance to USD** using that rate.
4. **Sum all converted USD values** across all foreign accounts.
5. **If the aggregate tops $10,000**, file the FBAR and report each account.

### Example: CAD to USD Conversion Table

Suppose the December 31 Treasury rate for CAD is 0.72 (1 CAD = $0.72 USD).

| Account | Maximum CAD Balance | USD Equivalent |
|---------|---------------------|----------------|
| RBC RRSP | CAD 85,000 | USD 61,200 |
| TD TFSA | CAD 22,000 | USD 15,840 |
| Scotiabank chequing | CAD 6,500 | USD 4,680 |
| **Total aggregate** | | **USD 81,720** |

All three accounts are above $10,000 in aggregate. Report each on a separate line of FinCEN Form 114. Include the maximum USD value, account number, and the institution's name and address.

### RRSP Maximum Value and Market Fluctuations

RRSP and RRIF accounts often hold equities, mutual funds, or ETFs. The maximum value is the highest total market value during the year — not the cost basis. If your RRSP reached $100,000 USD-equivalent in February but fell to $75,000 by December 31, you report $100,000 as the maximum value.

Keep monthly or quarterly statements from your Canadian custodian (RBC, TD, BMO, Scotiabank, CIBC, Questrade, or Wealthsimple). These show balance history, which makes the maximum value calculation straightforward. ## What About the RESP?

The RESP is a Registered Education Savings Plan used by Canadian families to save for post-secondary education. It is FBAR-reportable as a financial account. Beyond the FBAR, it may also require Form 3520 and Form 3520-A if the IRS treats it as a foreign trust under IRC 6048.

If the RESP functions as a trust — with a subscriber (the contributor) and a beneficiary (the student) — the trust filing rules may apply. Failure to file Form 3520 carries a penalty of the greater of $10,000 or 35% of the gross reportable amount under IRC 6048. The IRS has not issued clear guidance on RESP classification, so this area carries real risk.

US persons contributing to an RESP should work with a cross-border tax professional. See our article on [FBAR vs Form 3520 for foreign trusts](/blog/fbar-vs-form-3520-foreign-trusts) for background on foreign trust reporting.

## Real-World Scenarios for US-Canada Dual Filers

### Scenario 1: Dual Citizen in Toronto With RRSP and TFSA

Sarah is a US-Canadian dual citizen living in Toronto. She holds:

- RBC RRSP: maximum balance CAD $120,000 during the year
- TD TFSA: maximum balance CAD $35,000 during the year
- RBC chequing: maximum balance CAD $8,000 during the year

At the December 31 Treasury rate of 0.73 CAD/USD:

- RRSP: CAD $120,000 × 0.73 = USD $87,600
- TFSA: CAD $35,000 × 0.73 = USD $25,550
- Chequing: CAD $8,000 × 0.73 = USD $5,840
- Aggregate: USD $118,990

Sarah must file the FBAR under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350), reporting all three accounts. The US-Canada Tax Treaty (Article XVIII) defers US tax on her RRSP income. Her TFSA earnings are taxable on her US return each year. She also needs Form 8938 (FATCA) because her foreign assets exceed $200,000 on the last day of the year under [26 USC 6038D](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D).

### Scenario 2: US Resident With a Canadian RRSP From Prior Employment

James worked in Canada for eight years before moving to Boston. He left his RRSP at TD Wealth with a balance of CAD $95,000 (USD $69,350 at the 0.73 rate). He has no other foreign accounts.

James must file the FBAR under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350) because his RRSP exceeds $10,000. He claims treaty deferral automatically — Form 8891 is gone (eliminated in 2014 per IRS [Rev. Proc. 2014-55](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2014-44_IRB#RP-2014-55)). He can leave the RRSP in Canada and defer US tax until he withdraws. Distributions are taxable as ordinary income in the US. Canada's withholding tax may offset his US liability.

Maria moved from Ottawa to Chicago three years ago. She kept her RRSP, TFSA, and a RESP for her daughter. She did not know about the FBAR requirement. Her accounts exceeded $10,000 in aggregate each year.

Maria has three years of unfiled FBARs. The IRS Streamlined Domestic Offshore Procedures can resolve non-willful failures. The penalty is 5% of the highest aggregate balance rather than per-violation amounts. ## What Penalties Apply for Unreported Canadian Accounts?

Missing an FBAR for a Canadian RRSP, TFSA, or other account carries the same penalties as any other unreported foreign account. Non-willful violations carry up to $16,117 per account per year under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). Willful violations carry the greater of $100,000 or 50% of the account balance under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C).

| Violation Type | Penalty | Citation |
|----------------|---------|---------|
| Non-willful | Up to $16,117 per violation (2025) | 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B) |
| Willful | Greater of $100,000 or 50% of account balance | 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C) |
| Criminal (willful) | Up to $250,000 fine and/or 5 years prison | 31 USC 5322 |

A violation is typically one account per year. Three unreported accounts for two years equals six violations — up to $96,702 in non-willful penalties under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B). Willful violations carry far higher exposure.

Non-willful means you did not know about the requirement or made a reasonable mistake. Willful means you knew and chose not to file, or showed reckless disregard. Furthermore, the penalties differ substantially — see [willful vs non-willful FBAR penalties](/blog/fbar-willful-vs-non-willful-penalties) for a full breakdown.

If you have unreported Canadian accounts, act before the IRS contacts you. Additionally, the Streamlined Programs and Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures both offer paths to compliance with reduced penalties. ## Frequently Asked Questions

Here are the most frequently asked questions about FBAR Canada RRSP TFSA reporting. These cover Form 8891, Form 8938, financial interest rules, TFSA taxation, RESP Form 3520 obligations, and how to use FBAR Form 114 for Canadian registered accounts. Each FAQ answer states the key rule and relevant statute.

**Do I need to report my Canadian RRSP on the FBAR?**

Yes. An RRSP held at a Canadian financial institution is a foreign financial account under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). You must report it if your aggregate foreign balances exceed $10,000 at any point during the year. The US-Canada Tax Treaty defers US income tax on RRSP gains, but the treaty does not remove the FBAR reporting obligation under [31 USC 5314](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5314).

**Is a Canadian TFSA taxable in the US?**

Yes. The TFSA is tax-free in Canada, but US persons pay US income tax on TFSA earnings every year. The US-Canada Tax Treaty's deferral provision (Article XVIII) covers RRSPs and RRIFs — it does not extend to TFSAs. You report TFSA interest on Schedule B, dividends on Schedule B, and capital gains on Schedule D of your Form 1040. The TFSA is also FBAR-reportable. See our [first-time filer guide](/blog/fbar-first-time-filer-guide) for an overview.

**Do I still need to file Form 8891 for my RRSP?**

No. The IRS eliminated Form 8891 in 2014 through [Rev. Proc. 2014-55](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2014-44_IRB#RP-2014-55). The treaty election for RRSP and RRIF deferral is now automatic. You do not file any separate election form. You still report the accounts on your FBAR each year. Check Form 8938 obligations if your RRSP balance exceeds FATCA thresholds under 26 USC 6038D.

**Do I need to report my Canadian RESP on the FBAR?**

Yes. You must report the RESP on the FBAR as a financial account. It may also require Form 3520 and Form 3520-A if the IRS treats it as a foreign trust. Failure to file Form 3520 carries a penalty of the greater of $10,000 or 35% of the gross reportable amount under IRC 6048. Work with a cross-border tax specialist to assess your specific RESP arrangement.

**What exchange rate do I use for Canadian dollars on the FBAR?**

Use the December 31 [Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/reports-statements/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/current.html). Apply that rate to the maximum balance of each account during the year — not the year-end balance. See our [guide to FBAR exchange rates and Treasury Department rates](/blog/fbar-exchange-rates-treasury-department) for the full methodology.

**What if I have a LIRA?**

A LIRA (Locked-In Retirement Account) is FBAR-reportable. It is a type of locked-in RRSP that holds pension funds transferred from an employer plan. The same treaty deferral that covers RRSPs applies to LIRAs under Article XVIII of the US-Canada Tax Treaty. Report it on the FBAR with its maximum USD balance for the year.

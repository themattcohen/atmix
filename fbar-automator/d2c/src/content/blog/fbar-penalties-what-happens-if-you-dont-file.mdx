## FBAR Penalties Are Among the Harshest in US Tax Enforcement

The penalties for failing to file an FBAR are disproportionately severe compared to most other tax and reporting violations. A single unreported foreign bank account with $50,000 can generate a non-willful penalty of $12,909 — and that's per violation, per year. Willful violations can reach $100,000 or 50% of the account balance, whichever is greater.

These aren't theoretical numbers. The IRS and Department of Justice have aggressively enforced FBAR penalties since the 2008 UBS scandal that exposed thousands of US taxpayers with undisclosed Swiss bank accounts. Understanding the penalty structure is the first step toward making sure you never face it.

## The Three Tiers of FBAR Penalties

FBAR penalties exist on a spectrum, and the tier you fall into depends primarily on one word: **willfulness**.

### Tier 1: Non-Willful Penalties

**Statute**: 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)(i)

**Maximum penalty**: **$12,909 per violation** (2026 inflation-adjusted amount; the base amount of $10,000 is adjusted annually per the Federal Civil Penalties Inflation Adjustment Act of 2015)

**What counts as "non-willful"**: You failed to file because you didn't know about the requirement, misunderstood it, forgot, or made a genuine mistake. There was no intentional effort to hide accounts from the government.

**How violations are counted**: Each unreported account in each year is a separate violation. Three accounts unreported for two years = six violations = up to **$77,454** in potential penalties.

**Important nuance from *United States v. Bittner* (2023)**: The Supreme Court ruled 5-4 that for non-willful violations, the penalty is assessed per report (per year), not per account. This means if you failed to file an FBAR for one year covering five accounts, that's one violation — up to $12,909 — not five violations. This is a significant taxpayer-friendly ruling.

However, this per-report interpretation applies specifically to non-willful violations. If you missed FBARs for three years, that's three violations, up to **$38,727** total, regardless of how many accounts were involved.

### Tier 2: Willful Penalties

**Statute**: 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)

**Maximum penalty**: The greater of:
- **$100,000** per violation, or
- **50% of the account balance** at the time of the violation

**What counts as "willful"**: You knew or should have known about the FBAR requirement and consciously chose not to file. This includes:
- Actual knowledge of the requirement plus deliberate non-filing
- "Reckless disregard" of the requirement — you were told about it (by a CPA, by the Schedule B instructions, by media coverage) and ignored it
- Active concealment of foreign accounts
- Lying to your tax preparer about foreign accounts

**The willfulness standard from *United States v. Williams***: The court held that willfulness for FBAR purposes includes conduct that is voluntary, intentional, and a known violation of a known legal duty. Importantly, the court also held that "willful blindness" — deliberately avoiding learning about the FBAR requirement when you had reason to know — can constitute willfulness.

**How violations are counted**: Unlike non-willful violations after *Bittner*, willful violations are assessed **per account, per year**. Five accounts unreported for three years = fifteen violations.

**Example**: David has a Swiss bank account with $400,000. His CPA asked him annually if he had foreign accounts, and he said no. The IRS discovers the account during an examination.

- Willful penalty per year: Greater of $100,000 or 50% of $400,000 = **$200,000 per year**
- Over three years: **$600,000** — exceeding the account balance itself
- Plus potential back taxes, accuracy-related penalties, and interest on unreported income

### Tier 3: Criminal Penalties

**Statute**: 31 USC 5322

**Maximum penalty**:
- Up to **$250,000** in fines, and/or
- Up to **5 years** imprisonment

For violations committed in connection with other crimes (such as tax evasion under 26 USC 7201), the penalties increase to **$500,000** and **10 years**.

**When criminal prosecution occurs**: The IRS Criminal Investigation (CI) division investigates cases involving:
- Large undisclosed foreign accounts (typically $1,000,000+)
- Active concealment (nominee accounts, shell companies, encrypted communications)
- Obstruction (destroying records, lying to agents)
- Pattern of evasion across multiple years
- Connection to other criminal activity (money laundering, fraud)

Criminal FBAR cases often result in plea agreements. Between 2010 and 2024, the average prison sentence for FBAR-related criminal convictions was approximately 2-3 years, with restitution and civil penalties added on top.

## What Makes a Violation "Willful"?

This is the most consequential question in FBAR enforcement because it's the difference between a $12,909 penalty and a $200,000+ penalty.

### Factors That Point Toward Willfulness

- **Checking "No" on Schedule B**: Your Form 1040 Schedule B asks: "At any time during [the year], did you have a financial interest in or a signature authority over a financial account in a foreign country?" If you checked "No" while having foreign accounts, the IRS will argue this demonstrates awareness of the question and a deliberate lie.
- **Professional advice ignored**: Your CPA or financial advisor told you about the FBAR requirement, and you didn't file.
- **Large account balances**: While account size alone doesn't determine willfulness, the IRS argues it's harder to "forget" about a $500,000 account than a $5,000 one.
- **Sophisticated taxpayer**: Professionals in finance, law, or accounting are held to a higher awareness standard.
- **Active steps to conceal**: Using nominee names, structuring deposits, maintaining accounts in secrecy jurisdictions.
- **Filing FBARs for some accounts but not others**: Selective reporting suggests knowledge of the requirement.

### Factors That Point Against Willfulness

- **First-time filer**: No prior history of FBAR filing, especially for recent immigrants or green card holders.
- **No foreign income**: If the accounts earned minimal interest and there was no tax benefit to concealment, willfulness is harder to prove.
- **Reliance on professional advice**: If your CPA didn't ask about foreign accounts, and you relied on them for compliance, this supports a non-willful finding.
- **Prompt disclosure**: Coming forward voluntarily before the IRS contacts you demonstrates good faith.
- **Cultural factors**: Immigrants from countries with no equivalent reporting requirement may genuinely be unaware.

### The *Safeco* Standard

Courts apply the *Safeco Insurance Co. v. Burr* standard: willfulness requires a "knowing violation of a known legal duty" or "reckless disregard" of that duty. Reckless disregard means running a risk of violating the law substantially greater than the risk associated with ordinary carelessness.

In practice, this means the IRS doesn't need to prove you specifically intended to break the law. They only need to show you were aware of — or recklessly indifferent to — the FBAR filing requirement.

## Real-World FBAR Penalty Cases

### Case 1: *United States v. Zwerner* (2014)

**Facts**: Carl Zwerner, a retired businessman, held a Swiss bank account at UBS that was not reported on his FBARs. The account held approximately $1.69 million. Zwerner argued his failure was non-willful.

**Outcome**: A jury found the violation was willful. The court assessed a penalty of **$2.24 million** — approximately 133% of the account balance. The case was eventually settled for a reduced amount, but it demonstrated that FBAR penalties can exceed the total account value.

**Lesson**: Once willfulness is established, the 50% per-year penalty can stack to exceed the account balance.

### Case 2: *United States v. Bittner* (2023, Supreme Court)

**Facts**: Alexandru Bittner, a dual Romanian-US citizen, failed to file FBARs for five years covering 272 foreign accounts. The government assessed penalties per account per year (totaling $2.72 million at $10,000 per violation), while Bittner argued the penalty should be per report per year ($50,000 total).

**Outcome**: The Supreme Court ruled 5-4 in Bittner's favor. For **non-willful** violations, the maximum penalty is $12,909 per annual FBAR report, regardless of the number of accounts. This reduced Bittner's exposure from $2.72 million to $50,000.

**Lesson**: The *Bittner* decision is a major win for non-willful violators. If your failure was truly non-willful, you face a maximum of $12,909 per year, not per account. But this protection **does not apply** to willful violations.

### Case 3: Streamlined Filing vs. Full Penalties

**Scenario**: Elena, a green card holder from Brazil, had three accounts in Brazil with an aggregate peak value of $180,000 over a six-year period. She reported all interest income on her tax returns but didn't know about the FBAR.

**If she files voluntarily through Streamlined**: 5% penalty on $180,000 = **$9,000** total. All years resolved.

**If the IRS discovers it first**: Up to $12,909 per year (post-*Bittner*) x 6 years = **$77,454** for non-willful violations. If found willful: 50% of $180,000 = $90,000 per year x 6 years = **$540,000**.

**Lesson**: Voluntary disclosure through Streamlined procedures can save hundreds of thousands of dollars compared to enforcement penalties. The $9,000 Streamlined penalty versus $77,454+ in enforcement penalties speaks for itself.

## The Statute of Limitations

Under 31 USC 5321(b)(1), the IRS has **6 years** from the date of the violation to assess FBAR penalties. The clock starts on the filing deadline:

- 2019 FBAR (due April 15, 2020): Statute expires April 15, 2026
- 2020 FBAR (due April 15, 2021): Statute expires April 15, 2027
- 2025 FBAR (due April 15, 2026): Statute expires April 15, 2032

There is no statute of limitations extension for fraud (unlike income tax, where fraud eliminates the statute entirely). However, the IRS can extend the FBAR statute of limitations through signed agreements with the taxpayer — which they often request during examinations.

Six years is a long time. The IRS can open an FBAR examination years after you thought you were in the clear. And with international information exchange agreements (FATCA, CRS) automatically delivering account data to the IRS from foreign governments, the likelihood of discovery increases every year.

## How to Fix Past Non-Filing

If you've been reading this article with a sinking feeling because you have unreported foreign accounts, take a breath. There are well-established paths to come into compliance, and the sooner you act, the better your outcome.

### Path 1: Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures

**Who qualifies**: Filers who properly reported all foreign income on their tax returns but simply didn't file the FBAR.

**Process**:
1. File delinquent FBARs through BSA E-Filing for all missed years
2. Include a statement with each FBAR explaining why it's late
3. The IRS will not automatically impose penalties if the income was properly reported

**Cost**: Potentially zero penalty. The IRS has stated that it will not impose penalties for delinquent FBARs where there is no underreported income and the taxpayer has not been previously contacted about an examination.

**Best for**: People who reported a $500 interest payment from their foreign savings account on Schedule B but didn't know about the separate FBAR filing. Or people with accounts that earned no income at all (like a foreign checking account used for bill payments).

### Path 2: Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures

**Who qualifies**: Non-willful violators who may have both unreported FBARs and unreported foreign income.

**Process**:
1. File 3 years of amended or delinquent income tax returns (current year + 2 prior years)
2. File 6 years of delinquent FBARs
3. Pay all tax and interest due on unreported income
4. Pay the 5% miscellaneous offshore penalty (Domestic) or no penalty (Foreign)
5. Submit a certification of non-willful conduct under penalty of perjury

**Penalty calculation**: The 5% penalty is based on the highest aggregate value of your foreign accounts during the 6-year FBAR look-back period.

**Example**: Peak aggregate value was $250,000. Streamlined Domestic penalty = $12,500. This covers all accounts, all years, and provides certainty — no risk of higher penalties later.

**The Foreign Streamlined option**: If you meet the non-residency requirement (lived outside the US for at least 330 days in at least one of the three tax return years), you qualify for Streamlined Foreign Offshore Procedures — which carry **zero penalty**. You pay the back taxes and interest only.

### Path 3: IRS Voluntary Disclosure Practice

**Who qualifies**: Filers whose non-compliance was willful or who cannot certify non-willfulness under penalty of perjury.

**Process**: Submit a preclearance request to IRS Criminal Investigation, followed by full disclosure. This involves working with a tax attorney — it is not a self-service program.

**Outcome**: Avoidance of criminal prosecution in exchange for civil penalties, which are typically 75% of the total tax liability plus various civil penalties. Expensive, but far better than criminal prosecution.

## IRS Enforcement Trends

The IRS has increased its focus on international tax compliance significantly since the passage of FATCA (Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act) in 2010. Key enforcement mechanisms include:

- **FATCA reporting**: Over 300,000 foreign financial institutions in 110+ jurisdictions report US account holder information to the IRS annually. If you have an account at a FATCA-compliant bank, the IRS already knows about it.
- **Common Reporting Standard (CRS)**: While the US is not a CRS participant, the data flows are increasingly bidirectional through bilateral agreements and tax treaties.
- **John Doe summonses**: The IRS has issued summonses to banks and cryptocurrency exchanges to obtain account holder records.
- **Data analytics**: The IRS uses sophisticated matching programs to compare FBAR filings against FATCA data, foreign tax credit claims, and Schedule B disclosures.

The bottom line: the era when you could maintain an unreported foreign account with a reasonable expectation of anonymity is over. Information exchange is automatic, comprehensive, and accelerating. Filing your FBAR is not optional — it's a mathematical certainty that the IRS will eventually have the data to identify non-filers.

## What Does "Maximum Account Value" Actually Mean?

If you hold financial accounts outside the United States, you've probably heard the term "maximum account value" thrown around. But what does it actually mean for FBAR purposes?

Under 31 CFR 1010.350(g)(1), the maximum account value is the largest amount of currency or nonmonetary assets in the account at any time during the calendar year. Not the December 31 balance. Not the average balance. The absolute peak value at any point during the year.

This distinction matters because it determines whether you cross the $10,000 aggregate threshold that triggers your filing obligation under 31 USC 5314. Get this number wrong, and you either file when you don't need to — or worse, skip a filing you were required to make.

Let me walk you through exactly how to calculate this correctly.

## Step 1: Identify Every Foreign Financial Account You Hold

Before you can calculate anything, you need a complete inventory. Under 31 CFR 1010.350(c), a "financial account" includes:

- **Bank accounts** — savings, checking, time deposits (CDs)
- **Securities accounts** — brokerage accounts held at foreign institutions
- **Insurance policies with cash value** — certain foreign life insurance or annuity contracts
- **Mutual funds and pooled investment vehicles** — foreign-domiciled funds
- **Pension accounts** — some foreign pension arrangements (this varies by country)
- **Cryptocurrency exchange accounts** — accounts on foreign-domiciled exchanges like Binance

A common mistake is forgetting about accounts you rarely use. That dormant savings account in your home country with £2,500 sitting in it? It counts. The old checking account in Germany with €800? It counts too.

### Example: Sarah's Account Inventory

Sarah, a dual US-UK citizen living in New York, has:

1. A Barclays savings account in London
2. A Stocks & Shares ISA with Hargreaves Lansdown
3. A joint checking account with her mother at HSBC UK
4. A Chase checking account in New York (this one is domestic — does not count)

Sarah has three foreign accounts to evaluate.

## Step 2: Determine the Maximum Value for Each Account

For each foreign account, you need to find the highest balance at any point during the calendar year. Here's where people run into trouble.

### How to Find the Peak Balance

Most banks provide monthly or quarterly statements. Look at every statement for the year and identify the highest balance. If your bank provides daily transaction history, even better — scan for the peak.

**For bank accounts**: Review each monthly statement. The highest closing balance across all 12 months is usually your maximum value, but intra-month peaks could be higher. If you received a $50,000 wire transfer on March 3 and moved $45,000 out on March 5, your maximum value for that account might be around the March 3-5 period, even though the month-end statement shows a much lower balance.

**For securities/brokerage accounts**: Use the highest portfolio value during the year. If your foreign brokerage account held stocks worth $120,000 in January but dropped to $85,000 by December, your maximum value is $120,000.

**For joint accounts**: Per FinCEN guidance, each US person who has a financial interest in or signature authority over the account reports the **full value** of the account — not half, not their proportional share. The entire balance.

### Example: Finding Sarah's Maximum Values

Looking at Sarah's 2025 account activity:

1. **Barclays savings**: Monthly balances ranged from £32,000 to £45,000. The peak was £45,000 in July.
2. **Hargreaves Lansdown ISA**: Portfolio peaked at £28,500 in November.
3. **HSBC joint checking with mother**: The account hit £8,200 in September (Sarah reports the full £8,200, not half).

## Step 3: Convert Foreign Currency to US Dollars

Here's where precision matters. You must convert each account's maximum value to US dollars using the Treasury Department's Financial Management Service rate for the last day of the calendar year, per FinCEN instructions.

These rates are published at [treasury.gov](https://fiscaldata.treasury.gov/datasets/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/treasury-reporting-rates-of-exchange). You use the **December 31 rate** of the reporting year, regardless of when the maximum value actually occurred during the year.

This is counterintuitive. Even though your account peaked in July, you convert using the December 31 exchange rate. This is the rate FinCEN requires.

### Example: Converting Sarah's Accounts

For calendar year 2025, let's say the Treasury Department's December 31, 2025 rate for British pounds is 1 GBP = 1.27 USD.

1. **Barclays savings**: £45,000 x 1.27 = **$57,150**
2. **Hargreaves Lansdown ISA**: £28,500 x 1.27 = **$36,195**
3. **HSBC joint checking**: £8,200 x 1.27 = **$10,414**

Each account's maximum value is now stated in US dollars.

## Step 4: Determine If You Meet the $10,000 Aggregate Threshold

The FBAR filing requirement under 31 USC 5314 triggers when the **aggregate** maximum value of all your foreign accounts exceeds $10,000 at any point during the calendar year.

"Aggregate" means you add them all up. But here's the subtlety: you don't just add the maximums together and compare to $10,000. Technically, the aggregate value is the total across all accounts at any single point in time. In practice, FinCEN instructs filers to report each account's individual maximum value, and the filing threshold is met if the combined total of all accounts' maximum values exceeds $10,000.

If you need to file, you report **every** foreign account — even the ones with small balances.

### Example: Sarah's Aggregate Calculation

Sarah's aggregate maximum values:

- Barclays: $57,150
- Hargreaves Lansdown: $36,195
- HSBC joint: $10,414
- **Total: $103,759**

Sarah clearly exceeds the $10,000 threshold. She must file an FBAR and report all three accounts.

But what if Sarah only had the HSBC joint checking account at $10,414? That single account exceeds $10,000, so she'd still need to file.

And what if she had two accounts — one at $6,000 and one at $5,000? The aggregate is $11,000, which exceeds $10,000, so she files and reports both accounts.

## Multi-Country, Multi-Currency Calculations

Things get more complex when you hold accounts in multiple countries with different currencies. The process is the same — find the maximum value, convert using the December 31 Treasury rate — but you're working with multiple exchange rates.

### Example: Raj's Three-Country Calculation

Raj is a US person who holds accounts in India, the UK, and Canada:

1. **State Bank of India savings account**: Peak balance ₹2,500,000 in August
2. **Barclays UK current account**: Peak balance £12,000 in March
3. **TD Canada Trust TFSA**: Peak balance C$35,000 in October
4. **ICICI Bank India fixed deposit**: Peak balance ₹1,800,000 (term deposit, value is the deposit amount plus accrued interest)

Using December 31, 2025 Treasury Department rates:
- Indian Rupee: 1 USD = 83.50 INR (so 1 INR = 0.01198 USD)
- British Pound: 1 GBP = 1.27 USD
- Canadian Dollar: 1 CAD = 0.74 USD

**Conversions**:
1. SBI savings: ₹2,500,000 / 83.50 = **$29,940**
2. Barclays UK: £12,000 x 1.27 = **$15,240**
3. TD Canada TFSA: C$35,000 x 0.74 = **$25,900**
4. ICICI fixed deposit: ₹1,800,000 / 83.50 = **$21,557**

**Aggregate total: $92,637**

Raj must file an FBAR reporting all four accounts. Note that the Canadian TFSA is reportable on the FBAR — even though it's tax-free in Canada, it's still a foreign financial account for US reporting purposes.

## Common Mistakes That Lead to Incorrect Calculations

After processing thousands of FBAR filings, I see the same errors repeatedly:

### Mistake 1: Using Year-End Balance Instead of Maximum Value

This is the most common error. Your December 31 balance might be $7,000, but if the account held $15,000 in June, your maximum value is $15,000. The year-end balance is irrelevant for determining your filing obligation.

### Mistake 2: Using the Wrong Exchange Rate

Some filers use the exchange rate from the date the account peaked. Others use a rate from Google or their bank. FinCEN requires the Treasury Department's rate for December 31 of the reporting year. Using any other rate is technically incorrect, and significant deviations could raise questions during an examination.

### Mistake 3: Reporting Half the Value of Joint Accounts

If you hold a joint account with your spouse, parent, or business partner, you report the **full** value of the account — not your proportional share. Both account holders who are US persons must file their own FBAR reporting the full value.

### Mistake 4: Forgetting About Accounts That Were Closed Mid-Year

If you closed an account in March but it held €25,000 at its peak before closure, you still report that account on your FBAR. The obligation is based on whether the account existed at any point during the calendar year.

### Mistake 5: Ignoring Accounts With Small Balances

Once you exceed the $10,000 aggregate threshold, you must report **every** foreign account, even one with a $50 balance. People often skip the small ones, but FinCEN expects a complete picture.

### Mistake 6: Not Knowing Which Accounts Are "Foreign"

An account is "foreign" based on where the financial institution is located, not where you access it from. If you have an HSBC account that you opened in the UK and HSBC UK maintains it, that's a foreign account — even if you manage it online from your couch in Texas. But an HSBC account opened at an HSBC branch in New York is a domestic account.

## What to Do With Accounts Denominated in Multiple Currencies

Some accounts — particularly brokerage accounts — may hold assets in multiple currencies. In this case, convert the total portfolio value (across all currency denominations) to US dollars using the relevant December 31 Treasury rates. Most brokerages will provide a consolidated statement in a base currency, which simplifies the calculation.

If your foreign brokerage account holds $30,000 in US-denominated stocks, €15,000 in European equities, and £5,000 in UK gilts, you'd convert the euro and pound portions and add them to the dollar portion:

- US stocks: $30,000 (no conversion needed)
- European equities: €15,000 x 1.10 (example rate) = $16,500
- UK gilts: £5,000 x 1.27 = $6,350
- **Account maximum value: $52,850**

## Accounts With No Readily Available Maximum Value

Certain account types make it harder to determine the maximum value:

- **Foreign real estate held in a foreign entity account**: Generally, real estate itself is not reportable, but if the real estate is held through a foreign entity and you have an ownership interest, the entity's financial accounts may be.
- **Foreign life insurance with cash surrender value**: Use the cash surrender value at its peak during the year, as reported on policy statements.
- **Precious metals accounts**: Use the highest market value during the year based on daily spot prices.

When you can't determine an exact maximum, use a reasonable estimate. FinCEN acknowledges that in some cases, a good-faith estimate based on available information is acceptable. Document your methodology.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Maximum value = peak balance at any point during the calendar year**, not the year-end balance.
2. **Convert using the Treasury Department's December 31 exchange rate** — not Google, not your bank's rate, not the rate on the date the peak occurred.
3. **Joint accounts are reported at full value** by each US person with an interest.
4. **Once you cross the $10,000 aggregate threshold**, report every foreign account, no matter how small.
5. **Closed accounts still count** if they existed during the calendar year.
6. **Document your methodology** — keep statements and records showing how you arrived at each maximum value.

# FBAR Business Account Signatory Authority: Who Must File and When

If you are a corporate officer or employee with authority to sign on a foreign bank account, you may have a personal FBAR filing obligation — separate from any filing the company makes. The FBAR business account signatory authority rules under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350) are straightforward once you know the two triggers: financial interest and signatory authority.

The Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts (FBAR), also known as FinCEN Form 114, applies to US persons who hold a financial interest in or have signatory authority over foreign financial accounts that exceeded $10,000 at any point during the year. This obligation comes from [31 USC 5314](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5314), the Bank Secrecy Act (BSA).

Many officers and employees assume their employer's FBAR covers them. It does not. The company and each individual with a qualifying interest file separately. Getting this wrong means potential penalties of $16,117 per non-willful violation under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321).

## Does Your Foreign Business Account Trigger a Personal FBAR Filing?

Yes — a foreign business account can create a personal FBAR filing obligation. This happens when you have a financial interest in or signatory authority over the account under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). The $10,000 threshold covers all your qualifying foreign accounts combined, at any point during the year. The business files under its own Employer Identification Number. You file separately under your Social Security Number. Both filings are required.

The obligation is per person. Two officers with signatory authority over the same $500,000 foreign account each have an independent filing obligation. The account appears on both returns.

## What Is a Financial Interest in a Foreign Account?

A financial interest exists when you own or control the account — either directly or through a business entity you control. Under [31 CFR 1010.350(e)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350), two categories count: direct ownership and indirect ownership. Direct ownership is straightforward — the account is in your name. Indirect ownership, however, depends on your ownership percentage in the entity that holds the foreign account.

### Direct Ownership of a Foreign Account

You have a direct financial interest when you are the account holder of record. This includes accounts held in your name even if another person or entity benefits from the funds.

### Indirect Ownership Through a Business Entity

You have an indirect financial interest under [31 CFR 1010.350(e)(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350) when you own more than 50% of a corporation, LLC, partnership, or trust that holds the foreign account. Ownership covers both the total value and voting power of the entity.

The 50% test applies through the ownership chain. If you own 60% of a holding company that owns 100% of an operating company with a foreign account, you have an indirect financial interest. Constructive ownership rules apply as they do for other tax purposes.

**Example**: Maria owns 60% of TechCo LLC, a Delaware company with a €200,000 operating account at Deutsche Bank. At a December 31 exchange rate of $1.09 per euro, the account holds about $218,000. Therefore, Maria has an indirect financial interest and must file an FBAR for that account. TechCo also files its own FBAR separately under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350).

## What Is FBAR Signatory Authority Over a Business Account?

Signatory authority means the authority to control the disposition of assets in a foreign financial account by direct communication with the institution. This definition comes from [31 CFR 1010.350(f)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). You do not need to own the account. You need practical authority to move money or direct the bank to act.

The FBAR business account signatory authority rules focus on control, not access. A CFO who approves and starts wire transfers from a foreign operating account has signatory authority. An accounts payable clerk who enters pre-approved instructions that require a supervisor's sign-off does not.

### Who Typically Has FBAR Signatory Authority?

The following roles commonly create a signatory authority FBAR obligation:

- **Corporate officers** (CFO, Treasurer, VP Finance) listed as authorized signers on foreign bank accounts
- **Employees** named as authorized signatories on foreign operating or payroll accounts
- **Partners** in a US partnership with authority to control foreign partnership accounts
- **Trustees** with authority to control assets in foreign trust accounts (see [FBAR trust reporting requirements](/blog/fbar-trust-reporting-requirements))

In short, the key question is whether you can independently direct the disposition of the account's assets. If yes, you have signatory authority.

### What Does Not Create Signatory Authority?

These situations do not create a signatory authority filing obligation:

- **Read-only account access** — viewing statements or balances online without transaction authority
- **Ministerial processing** — submitting pre-approved wire instructions that require another person's approval before execution
- **Formulaic payroll processing** — running payroll from a foreign account under a fixed schedule without discretion to vary amounts or recipients
- **Backup contact status** — being listed as a secondary contact without authorization to transact

If your access requires another person's approval before anything happens, document that arrangement in writing.

## Which Employees Must File — and Which Are Exempt?

Officers and employees with signatory authority must file a personal FBAR unless they qualify for an exemption. Under [31 CFR 1010.350(f)(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350), the exemption applies when the employee has no financial interest in the account and their employer is one of the following: a federally examined bank (OCC, FDIC, Federal Reserve, or NCUA); an SEC- or CFTC-registered financial institution; a publicly traded US company on a national securities exchange; or a subsidiary at least 50% owned by a publicly traded company.

If your employer is a private company, no exemption applies. You must file.

### The Signatory Authority Exemption in Practice

**Example A — Exempt**: James is the Treasurer of PublicCo, a US company listed on the NYSE. PublicCo holds foreign operating accounts at Barclays in the UK. James has signatory authority but no ownership stake. Because PublicCo is publicly listed, James qualifies for the exemption under [31 CFR 1010.350(f)(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). He does not file a personal FBAR for those accounts.

**Example B — Not Exempt**: James also owns 60% of a private subsidiary of PublicCo that holds a separate HSBC account. That ownership creates a financial interest. The exemption does not cover financial interests. James must file a personal FBAR for the HSBC account.

## How to Determine Your FBAR Business Account Signatory Authority Obligation?

Use this process to decide whether the FBAR business account signatory authority rules create a personal filing obligation for you. Answer each question in order. Stop as soon as a "no" applies. If you reach step 7, you must file. See our [first-time filer guide](/blog/fbar-first-time-filer-guide) for background on how FBAR works.

1. Are you a US person — a citizen, resident alien, or entity formed under US law? If no, stop — the BSA does not apply.
2. Do you have a foreign financial account (bank, brokerage, or other financial account outside the US)? If no, stop.
3. Did the aggregate balance of all your qualifying foreign accounts exceed $10,000 at any point during the calendar year? If no, stop.
4. Do you have a financial interest in the account — direct ownership or 50%+ ownership of an entity holding the account under [31 CFR 1010.350(e)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350)?
5. Do you have signatory authority — authority to control the disposition of the account's assets under [31 CFR 1010.350(f)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350)?
6. If yes to step 4 or 5, do you qualify for the signatory authority exemption under [31 CFR 1010.350(f)(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350)?
7. If not exempt, file an FBAR by April 15. An automatic extension to October 15 applies per [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306). See our [FBAR 2026 filing deadline guide](/blog/fbar-filing-deadline-2026) — the CFR also permits this extension for FinCEN Form 114.

If you have both financial interest and signatory authority in the same account, you still file once for that account. You check both boxes on FinCEN Form 114 (the FBAR form). Filing once means one Form 114 record for that account. If you have multiple accounts, you report each one. The FBAR instructions on FinCEN's website explain how to complete each field.

## How Do LLC Members Handle FBAR Signatory Authority Rules?

LLC members face different FBAR rules depending on the LLC's structure. Single-member LLCs and multi-member LLCs receive different treatment, but both can create personal filing obligations alongside the entity's own filing.

### Single-Member LLC

A single-member LLC that is disregarded for US tax purposes does not file its own FBAR. Instead, you — the sole member — are the US person for FBAR purposes. You report the LLC's foreign accounts as if you held them directly. The LLC's tax transparency carries over to BSA reporting.

### Multi-Member LLC

A multi-member LLC taxed as a partnership files its own FBAR as an entity. Individual members must also check whether they cross the 50%+ ownership threshold under [31 CFR 1010.350(e)(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). A 60% member has an indirect financial interest and must file personally. A 30% member typically does not cross the financial interest threshold — but that member should still check for signatory authority if they manage day-to-day banking.

## Comparison: Financial Interest vs. Signatory Authority

The table below shows the key differences between a financial interest and signatory authority under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). Both create a personal FBAR filing obligation. But they have different legal tests, different exemptions, and different documentation needs. Knowing which applies to you helps you decide whether and how to file.

| Factor | Financial Interest | Signatory Authority |
|--------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Legal basis | 31 CFR 1010.350(e) | 31 CFR 1010.350(f) |
| Key test | 50%+ ownership (direct or indirect) | Authority to control disposition of assets |
| Must you own the account? | Yes or 50%+ of entity owning it | No — employer or entity can own it |
| Exemption available? | No | Yes — for regulated employers (banks, public companies, SEC-registered firms) |
| Does entity also file? | Yes, separately | Yes, separately |
| Penalty exposure | Personal, per violation | Personal, per violation |
| Non-willful penalty | Up to $16,117 per violation (31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)) | Up to $16,117 per violation (31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)) |

## What Penalties Apply for Missing an FBAR on a Business Account?

If you miss a personal FBAR filing for a foreign business account, FinCEN assesses penalties against you individually — not against the entity. Non-willful violations carry up to $16,117 per missed account per year under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). Willful violations are far more serious. The penalties depend on the type of violation:

- **Non-willful violation**: Up to $16,117 per violation (2025 inflation adjustment) under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321)
- **Willful violation**: The greater of $100,000 or 50% of the account balance at the time of the violation under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321)
- **Criminal penalties**: In egregious cases, up to $500,000 in fines and 10 years imprisonment under [31 USC 5322](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5322)

Your employer's FBAR does not protect you. In fact, FinCEN treats the individual and the entity as separate filers with separate obligations. Read our full guide on [FBAR penalties and what happens if you don't file](/blog/fbar-penalties-what-happens-if-you-dont-file).

If you missed prior years, the [FBAR delinquent filing procedures](/blog/fbar-delinquent-filing-procedures) may let you catch up without penalty. For larger gaps, [FBAR streamlined filing compliance procedures](/blog/fbar-streamlined-filing-compliance-procedures) or the [voluntary disclosure practice](/blog/fbar-voluntary-disclosure-practice) may apply. The gap between willful and non-willful treatment is significant — from $16,117 (31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)) to $100,000 or more (31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)). See [FBAR willful vs. non-willful penalties](/blog/fbar-willful-vs-non-willful-penalties).

## Real-World Scenarios

### Scenario 1: CFO With Signatory Authority Over Multiple Accounts

Sarah is the CFO of a privately held US manufacturing company with subsidiaries in Germany and Japan. She is an authorized signatory on two foreign accounts: the German subsidiary's Deutsche Bank account (€150,000, or about $163,000) and the Japanese subsidiary's Sumitomo Mitsui account (¥20,000,000, or about $134,000).

The company is private, so the signatory authority exemption for publicly traded companies does not apply. Sarah must file a personal FBAR listing both accounts. The company also files its own FBAR separately, as required by [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350).

### Scenario 2: Minority Partner Without Signatory Authority

David owns 25% of a US-registered partnership with a Canadian operating account holding C$80,000 (about $58,000). He has no authority to sign on the account or direct transfers.

David does not meet the 50%+ ownership threshold for a financial interest under [31 CFR 1010.350(e)(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). He also lacks signatory authority. David has no personal FBAR obligation. The partnership files its own FBAR.

### Scenario 3: Accounts Payable Employee With Limited Access

Linda works in accounts payable at a US retailer. She can submit payment instructions to the company's UK subsidiary account. All transactions above $5,000 require a manager's digital approval before execution. She cannot initiate or approve transactions on her own.

Linda cannot independently control the disposition of the account's assets. Her access requires another person's approval before execution. She lacks signatory authority and has no personal FBAR obligation for the UK account.

## Do Signatory Authority Accounts Go on Form 8938?

No — signatory authority accounts do not go on [FATCA Form 8938](/blog/fbar-vs-fatca-form-8938-differences). Form 8938 covers specified foreign financial assets in which you have a financial interest. It does not apply to accounts where you have signatory authority but no financial interest. This is a key difference between the FBAR form (FinCEN Form 114) and Form 8938. In contrast, FBAR requires reporting based on signatory authority alone.

If you have a financial interest through 50%+ ownership, you may need to report that account on both forms — but different thresholds apply. FBAR requires reporting when the account exceeds $10,000 per [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306). Form 8938 starts at $50,000 for domestic single filers on the last day of the year under [26 USC 6038D](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D).

These are the questions most commonly asked about FBAR obligations for business accounts and signatory authority. Each answer cites the relevant statute or regulation and links to related articles for further reading. **What does FBAR business account signatory authority mean?**

FBAR business account signatory authority means you can control the disposition of assets in a foreign financial account owned by a business entity — by direct contact with the bank. This definition comes from [31 CFR 1010.350(f)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). If you are an authorized signer and can start or approve transactions on your own, you have signatory authority. You may need to file a personal FBAR for those accounts.

**Does signatory authority over a foreign account always require an FBAR filing?**

Not always. Under [31 CFR 1010.350(f)(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350), officers and employees of banks, SEC-registered firms, and publicly traded US companies may be exempt. The exemption applies if you have no financial interest in the account. If you work for a private company, no exemption applies. See [FBAR willful vs. non-willful penalties](/blog/fbar-willful-vs-non-willful-penalties) for what is at stake.

**If my company files its own FBAR, do I still need to file personally?**

Yes. The company and each individual with a financial interest or qualifying signatory authority file separately. The company's filing does not cover your personal obligation under [31 USC 5314](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5314). FinCEN treats entity-level and individual-level filings as separate.

**I own 40% of an LLC with a foreign account. Do I need to file an FBAR?**

At 40%, you do not meet the 50%+ financial interest threshold under [31 CFR 1010.350(e)(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). However, if you are also an authorized signatory on the account, the signatory authority prong creates a filing obligation — unless you qualify for an exemption because the LLC is a regulated entity.

**What is the FBAR threshold for signatory authority accounts?**

The FBAR threshold is $10,000 — the sum of all qualifying foreign accounts at any point during the year — under [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306). If the account held $50,000 on March 1st and $0 on December 31st, you still crossed the threshold. The same $10,000 limit applies to all FBAR filers. See our [FBAR first-time filer guide](/blog/fbar-first-time-filer-guide) for more detail.

**What if I was unaware of my FBAR obligation as an employee?**

FinCEN may treat the violation as non-willful if you have a credible explanation. Non-willful penalties run up to $16,117 per violation under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). The [FBAR reasonable cause defense](/blog/fbar-reasonable-cause-defense) can eliminate penalties if you show reasonable cause and no willful neglect. Filing late is far better than not filing.

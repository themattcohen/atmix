# FBAR Willful vs Non-Willful Penalty: What the Difference Costs You

The FBAR willful vs non-willful penalty gap is enormous. A non-willful violation carries a maximum of $16,117 per missed annual report under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). A willful violation carries the greater of $100,000 or 50% of the account balance — per account, per year — under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). That gap can mean $16,117 versus $1,000,000+ for the same unreported account.

The Bank Secrecy Act created this two-tier structure to distinguish honest mistakes from deliberate evasion. Courts have spent years defining the line. Understanding the distinction before you act on late FBARs is critical.

The FBAR covers US persons with any foreign financial account exceeding $10,000 in aggregate value. The duty to file comes from [31 USC 5314](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5314). Missing it triggers penalties under [31 USC 5321](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). Each year you miss is a separate violation with its own statute of limitations.

## What Is the Non-Willful FBAR Penalty?

The non-willful FBAR penalty is up to $16,117 per missed annual report under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). This is the 2025 inflation-adjusted figure — the original base was $10,000. A non-willful violation results from negligence, genuine mistake, or misunderstanding — not deliberate concealment. The IRS can reduce or waive this penalty when you show reasonable cause and good faith.

### How the IRS Counts Non-Willful Violations

The Supreme Court settled the non-willful penalty counting dispute in *United States v. Bittner*, 598 U.S. 95 (2023). Alexandru Bittner, a dual Romanian-US citizen, missed FBARs for five years covering 272 foreign accounts. The government sought $2.72 million — one penalty per account per year under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B). The Supreme Court ruled 5-4 for Bittner. Non-willful penalties apply **per annual FBAR report** — not per account.

Therefore, if you miss three consecutive FBARs, the maximum non-willful exposure is 3 reports × $16,117 = **$48,351** (31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)). However, this does not help willful filers. The per-report rule applies to non-willful violations only.

### What Qualifies as Non-Willful?

Non-willful conduct is a failure to file that results from genuine mistake or lack of knowledge. Common situations include:

- Failing to file because you had no knowledge the FBAR existed
- Misunderstanding who must file — common among new green card holders
- Relying on a tax preparer who never asked about foreign accounts
- Filing your tax return correctly but missing the separate FBAR form
- Language barriers or unfamiliarity with US tax rules

Additionally, the IRS may waive non-willful penalties under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)(ii)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321) when you demonstrate reasonable cause and good faith.

## What Is the FBAR Willful Penalty?

The FBAR willful penalty is the **greater of $100,000 or 50% of the account balance** per account, per year under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). This is not a flat cap. For a single $400,000 account, the willful penalty in one year is $200,000 under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C). For two accounts over four years, total penalties can exceed the combined balances.

### How the IRS Counts Willful Violations

Unlike the *Bittner* per-report rule, the IRS assesses willful FBAR penalties **per account, per year**. The Fourth Circuit confirmed this in *United States v. Williams*, 489 F. App'x 655 (4th Cir. 2012). Therefore, the government can stack penalties across every unreported account and every year of non-compliance. A filer with two accounts unreported for four years faces eight separate willful penalty assessments under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C).

### What Are the Criminal FBAR Penalties?

The most severe FBAR consequences are criminal. Under [31 USC 5322](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5322), willful violations carry:

- Up to **$250,000** in fines, and/or
- Up to **5 years** imprisonment

Violations connected to money laundering under 18 USC 1956 increase the maximum to **$500,000** and **10 years**. IRS Criminal Investigation pursues cases involving large undisclosed accounts and active concealment through nominees or shell companies.

## What Does "Willful" Mean Under FBAR Law?

Willfulness is the key legal question in any FBAR case. It separates a $16,117 non-willful fine (31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)) from a $200,000+ willful penalty (31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)). Courts apply the *Safeco Insurance Co. v. Burr*, 551 U.S. 47 (2007) standard: either a "knowing violation of a known legal duty" or "reckless disregard" of that duty satisfies willfulness. You do not need to intend to break the law. Recklessly ignoring a known obligation is enough.

### The Bedrosian Standard: Reckless Disregard

The Third Circuit applied this framework to FBAR in *Bedrosian v. United States*, 912 F.3d 144 (3d Cir. 2018). Arthur Bedrosian reported one Swiss UBS account at full value and listed a second at a fraction of its actual balance. The Third Circuit held that willfulness under 31 USC 5321(a)(5) includes **reckless disregard of a known legal obligation**. Specifically, knowing you had foreign accounts, suspecting reporting rules existed, and taking no steps to verify compliance can satisfy reckless disregard. This lower bar has driven hundreds of penalty determinations since 2018.

### Williams: Willfulness Inferred from Schedule B

In *United States v. Williams*, 489 F. App'x 655 (4th Cir. 2012), the Fourth Circuit found willfulness where the taxpayer checked "No" on Schedule B. Schedule B asks whether you held a financial interest or signature authority over a foreign account. Checking "No" while holding foreign accounts was direct evidence of willfulness — a deliberate misrepresentation.

*Williams* also confirmed that the 50% penalty applies to the **highest account balance for the year** under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C). For example, if your account peaked at $600,000 in April but fell to $300,000 by December, the 50% penalty is $300,000 — based on the high-water mark under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C).

### Willful Blindness and Conscious Avoidance

Courts also recognize **willful blindness** — deliberately avoiding knowledge of obligations you suspect exist. If your CPA asked each year about foreign accounts and you deflected, a court may find willful blindness. In short, this "conscious avoidance" theory applies where taxpayers avoided questions they feared would require disclosure.

## Which of the Following Penalties Applies to Willful Failure to File FBAR?

For willful failure to file an FBAR, the civil penalty is the greater of **$100,000 or 50% of the account balance** per account, per year under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C). Additionally, criminal prosecution under 31 USC 5322 can add up to **$250,000 in fines** and **5 years imprisonment**. Both can apply to the same violation — the IRS handles civil penalties, the Department of Justice handles criminal prosecution. The $100,000 floor means even a small account triggers a $100,000 penalty per year if willful under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C).

## How Do Courts Determine Willfulness in FBAR Cases?

Courts and IRS examiners weigh multiple factors when deciding whether a filer acted willfully. The IRS must prove willfulness by clear and convincing evidence in civil cases. No single factor is decisive — examiners look at the full pattern of conduct and the taxpayer's sophistication.

### Factors That Indicate Willfulness

- **Schedule B misrepresentation**: Answering "No" on Form 1040 Schedule B while holding foreign accounts — as in *Williams* — is strong evidence of willfulness
- **Ignored professional advice**: Your accountant or attorney told you about the FBAR requirement and you declined to file
- **Large account balances**: The IRS considers it harder to overlook a $400,000 account than a $4,000 one
- **Sophisticated taxpayer**: Attorneys, CPAs, bankers, and financial professionals face a higher awareness standard
- **Active concealment**: Using nominee account holders, shell companies, or offshore structures to hide accounts
- **Selective filing**: Filing FBARs for some accounts but not others shows knowledge of the rule
- **Repeated non-compliance**: Missing FBARs year after year — especially after IRS correspondence

### Factors That Indicate Non-Willfulness

- **No prior FBAR history**: First-time filers, especially new green card holders, who had no reason to know about the rule
- **Full income reporting**: Reporting all foreign interest income on your tax return — no tax benefit to concealment
- **Reliance on a preparer**: Your CPA prepared your returns without asking about foreign accounts
- **Cultural unfamiliarity**: Immigrants from countries without equivalent reporting rules may genuinely be unaware
- **Prompt voluntary disclosure**: Coming forward before any IRS contact demonstrates good faith
- **Small account balances**: Lower balances support the absence of a tax motive

Consult a tax attorney before filing anything if you are unsure which category fits your situation. The Streamlined Procedures certification is signed under penalty of perjury — filing it when your conduct was willful can create criminal liability.

## FBAR Willful vs Non-Willful Penalty: Side-by-Side Comparison

The FBAR penalty structure covers three tiers based on intent — from negligent non-filing to deliberate concealment. The non-willful civil penalty tops out at $16,117 per year under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B). Willful civil penalties, however, can exceed the account balance itself under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C). Moreover, criminal prosecution adds fines and imprisonment on top of civil penalties under 31 USC 5322.

| Category | Maximum Penalty | Counted Per | Statute |
|----------|----------------|-------------|---------|
| Non-willful civil | $16,117 per violation | Annual report (per *Bittner*) | 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B) |
| Willful civil | $100,000 or 50% of balance (greater) | Account, per year (per *Williams*) | 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C) |
| Criminal | $250,000 fine + 5 years imprisonment | Per prosecution | 31 USC 5322 |
| Criminal (with other crimes) | $500,000 fine + 10 years imprisonment | Per prosecution | 31 USC 5322(b) |

The IRS also uses IRM 4.26.16 and IRM 4.26.17 — the Internal Revenue Manual sections governing FBAR penalty mitigation. Examiners can reduce penalties when filers show reasonable cause, cooperate, and have no prior FBAR violations.

## What Is the Penalty for Understatement Due to Willful or Reckless Conduct?

The penalty for understatement due to willful or reckless FBAR conduct combines multiple layers of exposure. Under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321), the civil FBAR penalty is the greater of $100,000 or 50% of the account balance — per account, per year. Furthermore, the IRS adds a 20% accuracy penalty under 26 USC 6662 on any unpaid tax. Willful tax evasion under 26 USC 7201 adds fines up to $250,000 (26 USC 7201) and 5 years imprisonment. All three can apply to the same willful FBAR violation at once.

## Real-World FBAR Penalty Scenarios

### Scenario 1: Non-Willful — Three Missed Reports

Maria became a US permanent resident in 2020. She kept her Brazilian accounts open with an aggregate value of $85,000 and reported all Brazilian interest income on her US tax returns. She had no knowledge of the FBAR form until 2025 — three missed FBARs for 2021, 2022, and 2023.

Under *Bittner*, the non-willful penalty is per annual report:

- Maximum enforcement: 3 reports × $16,117 = **$48,351** (31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B))
- [Streamlined Domestic Offshore Procedures](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/streamlined-filing-compliance-procedures): 5% × $85,000 = **$4,250** flat penalty (31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B))

Voluntary disclosure gives a strong financial incentive to act early. For more, see our guide to [FBAR delinquent filing procedures](/blog/fbar-delinquent-filing-procedures).

### Scenario 2: Willful — Ignored Professional Advice

James is a US citizen living in London. In 2018, his UK accountant told him about the FBAR requirement. James chose not to file. His HSBC UK account held approximately $630,000 at its 2018 peak. The IRS discovers the account through FATCA data exchange in 2024. Consequently, the IRS assesses willful penalties under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C) for 2018 through 2023 — six years at 50% of the annual balance. The six-year total exceeds $1.6 million in FBAR penalties alone. For UK account reporting, see [FBAR and UK bank accounts](/blog/fbar-uk-bank-accounts).

### Scenario 3: Willful Blindness — Schedule B Misrepresentation

Susan is a CPA. She inherited a Swiss investment account from her father in 2015. She knew about the FBAR requirement from her professional training. Nevertheless, she told herself the account did not "count" because her father still managed it — even though she was the named account holder. She checked "No" on Schedule B every year from 2015 through 2022. The account peaked at $180,000.

Under *Williams*, checking "No" on Schedule B is direct evidence of willfulness. The IRS therefore assesses under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C): 8 years × $100,000 floor = **$800,000** (50% × $180,000 = $90,000 falls below the floor under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)). Susan's professional background and Schedule B conduct destroy a non-willful defense. See our guide to [FBAR voluntary disclosure](/blog/fbar-voluntary-disclosure-practice).

## What Are the IRS Mitigation Guidelines for FBAR Penalties?

The IRS can reduce FBAR penalties even when a violation is proven. The mitigation framework comes from IRM 4.26.16 and IRM 4.26.17. Examiners weigh these factors before they assess the maximum penalty under 31 USC 5321(a)(5):

1. **Isolated vs recurring violation** — a single missed year supports mitigation; repeated non-compliance does not
2. **Account balance relative to penalty** — the IRS may reduce penalties that appear disproportionate
3. **Full cooperation** — prompt responses to examination requests receive more favorable treatment
4. **Reasonable cause** — a good-faith misunderstanding supported by evidence can support penalty waiver
5. **Prior compliance history** — no prior FBAR violations supports mitigation
6. **Voluntary disclosure** — using Streamlined or Delinquent procedures before IRS contact gives the strongest mitigation available

The IRS does not guarantee mitigation. However, non-willful cases often result in reduced or waived penalties when the filer cooperates and shows good faith.

## How Does the FBAR Statute of Limitations Affect Penalties?

The IRS has **six years** from the FBAR filing deadline to assess civil penalties under [31 USC 5321(b)(1)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). The clock starts on the due date set by [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306) — April 15, with an automatic extension to October 15. Each missed FBAR year carries its own six-year window. FATCA data exchange, moreover, gives the IRS foreign account data before the six-year window closes. Waiting does not reduce your risk — it increases it. For full details, see our article on the [FBAR statute of limitations](/blog/fbar-statute-of-limitations).

## How to Fix Past Non-Filing Without Maximum Penalties

To fix past FBAR non-filing, choose the right IRS compliance path. Three programs are available. Acting before the IRS contacts you keeps all three options open. Once the IRS begins an examination, however, your choices narrow and full penalties apply.

1. **Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures** — For filers who reported all foreign income but missed the FBAR form. File delinquent FBARs through [BSA E-Filing](https://bsaefiling.fincen.treas.gov) with a statement explaining the late filing. No penalties apply when you reported all income and the IRS has not contacted you.

2. **Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures** — For non-willful filers with possible unreported foreign income. File 3 years of amended returns and 6 years of FBARs. Pay back taxes plus interest and a 5% miscellaneous penalty (Domestic) or zero penalty (Foreign). See our guide to [FBAR streamlined filing compliance procedures](/blog/fbar-streamlined-filing-compliance-procedures).

3. **IRS Voluntary Disclosure Practice** — For willful filers or those who cannot certify non-willfulness under penalty of perjury. Submit a preclearance request to IRS Criminal Investigation. You avoid prosecution but pay large civil penalties under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C). A tax attorney is required. See our article on [FBAR voluntary disclosure practice](/blog/fbar-voluntary-disclosure-practice).

## Frequently Asked Questions

These frequently asked questions address the most common issues around the FBAR willful vs non-willful penalty, including the Bittner ruling, Schedule B disclosure, and reasonable cause defense. If your situation involves possible willfulness, consult a tax attorney before filing anything under penalty of perjury.

**What is the difference between willful and non-willful FBAR penalties?**

A non-willful FBAR penalty is up to $16,117 per missed annual report under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). A willful FBAR penalty is the greater of $100,000 or 50% of the account balance, per account, per year under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). The key distinction is intent — negligence versus a knowing or reckless disregard of the filing obligation.

**Does the Bittner decision help willful filers?**

No. *United States v. Bittner*, 598 U.S. 95 (2023), applies only to non-willful penalties — the per-annual-report rule does not change willful penalty exposure. For willful violations, however, the IRS continues to assess penalties per account, per year — as *United States v. Williams* (4th Cir. 2012) confirmed under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C).

**Can I claim reasonable cause to avoid a willful FBAR penalty?**

Reasonable cause is a defense to non-willful penalties — not willful ones. Under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)(ii)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321), the IRS may waive non-willful penalties when you had reasonable cause and acted in good faith. Willful violations have no reasonable cause exception under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C). See our article on the [FBAR reasonable cause defense](/blog/fbar-reasonable-cause-defense).

**What happens if I file a late FBAR before the IRS contacts me?**

Filing late before any IRS contact is the best outcome available. The IRS has discretion under IRM 4.26.17 to treat voluntary compliance as a strong mitigating factor. Non-willful filers who reported all income can use the Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures to file without penalties. Once the IRS contacts you, therefore, these procedures become unavailable.

**Is checking "No" on Schedule B proof of willfulness?**

Courts treat a "No" answer on Schedule B while holding foreign accounts as strong — but not automatic — proof of willfulness. The Fourth Circuit in *United States v. Williams* found willfulness based on the Schedule B misrepresentation. Specifically, a prior "No" answer can destroy a non-willful defense — consult a tax attorney before filing amended returns or delinquent FBARs.

**Can FBAR penalties exceed my account balance?**

Yes. Willful FBAR penalties assessed per account, per year can stack above the total account value. In *United States v. Zwerner* (S.D. Fla. 2014), penalties reached about 133% of the account balance. As a result, penalties across multiple accounts and years can far exceed the combined account values under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C).

**What is the FBAR willful penalty for tax year 2021 or 2022?**

The willful FBAR penalty for violations in 2021, 2022, or any year is the greater of $100,000 or 50% of the account balance per account under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C). The non-willful penalty for those years was $12,921 (2021) and $14,489 (2022) — inflation-adjusted. The current 2025 non-willful penalty is $16,117 per report.

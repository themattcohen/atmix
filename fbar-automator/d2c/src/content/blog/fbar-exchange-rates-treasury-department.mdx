# FBAR Exchange Rate Treasury: How to Convert Foreign Currency to USD

The FBAR exchange rate treasury rule is clear: FinCEN requires you to use the U.S. Treasury Department's year-end exchange rate to convert foreign financial account balances to USD on the Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts. Under the Bank Secrecy Act (BSA), every U.S. person with aggregate foreign accounts exceeding $10,000 must file FinCEN Form 114. All foreign currency balances must appear in U.S. dollars on that form.

The U.S. Treasury year end exchange rates appear at [fiscal.treasury.gov](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/reports-statements/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/current.html) under the Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange section. These FBAR exchange rates cover most major currencies worldwide. This article explains how to find the correct rate, apply it to your peak balance, and what to do when your currency is not listed.

Getting the exchange rate right protects you from IRS questions. Under [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306), you report the maximum account value in USD. Therefore, a rate error — even an honest one — may trigger an IRS notice asking you to justify your reported values.

## Which FBAR Exchange Rate Treasury Source Should You Use?

FinCEN requires the U.S. Treasury Fiscal Data year-end exchange rate for the calendar year you are reporting. These FBAR exchange rate treasury figures appear on the Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange page and reflect the December 31 rate for each reporting year. For most major currencies — GBP, EUR, CAD, AUD, JPY, and CHF — Treasury publishes a December 31 rate you apply directly to your foreign financial account balance.

The Treasury rate differs from a bank rate or mid-market rate. It is the rate used by federal agencies for financial reporting. Bank conversion rates include retail spreads, so they often differ from Treasury figures. Always use the Treasury rate, not your bank's quoted rate.

### How to Find the December 31 Treasury Rate

Follow these steps each filing year:

1. Go to [fiscal.treasury.gov/reports-statements/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/reports-statements/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/current.html).
2. Download the report dated December 31 for the calendar year you are filing (for example, December 31, 2025 for a 2025 FBAR).
3. Find your currency in the table. Treasury lists rates as foreign currency units per one U.S. dollar.
4. Divide your foreign-currency balance by the listed rate. If the GBP rate is 0.7937, divide your pound balance by 0.7937 to get USD.
5. Save the URL and the exact rate. Keep this record for at least six years in case of an IRS examination.

### Understanding the Rate Format

Treasury rates appear as foreign currency units per USD. A GBP rate of 0.7937 means £0.7937 equals $1.00. To convert pounds to dollars, divide your GBP balance by 0.7937.

Many filers reverse this and multiply instead of dividing. The math error is large: £50,000 ÷ 0.7937 = $63,000, but £50,000 × 0.7937 = $39,685. Multiplying would significantly underreport the account value. Double-check your arithmetic before filing.

## How Do You Apply the FBAR Exchange Rate Treasury Rate to Your Peak Balance?

You apply the December 31 Treasury exchange rate to the highest foreign financial account balance during the reporting year, per [31 CFR 1010.306(d)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306). The FBAR asks for the maximum value — the peak balance at any point during the year — not the December 31 balance. You use the year-end exchange rate even though the peak may have occurred months earlier.

This rule surprises many filers. Your account may have peaked in March and dropped by December. However, you still report the March peak value converted at the December 31 rate. The rate timing and the balance timing are separate under BSA regulations.

### Example: UK Bank Account

Sarah holds a Barclays current account. Her balance peaked at £50,000 in March after receiving a property settlement. By December 31, the balance was £22,000.

- Maximum value: £50,000 (March peak)
- Treasury December 31, 2025 GBP rate: 0.7937 GBP per USD
- USD conversion: £50,000 ÷ 0.7937 = **$63,000**

Sarah reports $63,000 on her FBAR — not $22,000 based on December 31. See [FBAR UK Bank Accounts](/blog/fbar-uk-bank-accounts) for more on reporting British accounts.

### Example: Japanese Yen Account

Carlos holds a Mitsubishi UFJ account. His balance peaked at ¥8,500,000 in August.

- Maximum value: ¥8,500,000 (August peak)
- Treasury December 31, 2025 JPY rate: 157.12 JPY per USD
- USD conversion: ¥8,500,000 ÷ 157.12 = **$54,100**

Carlos reports $54,100. The yen weakened by December, but the December 31 rate still applies regardless.

## What If Treasury Doesn't Publish a Rate for Your Currency?

When Treasury does not publish a year-end rate for your currency, use a reasonable rate from another recognized source. FinCEN does not require a specific backup source. However, it expects you to document your method clearly for any IRS examination.

Accepted alternative sources include:

- **OANDA Historical Rates** (oanda.com) — widely used by tax professionals, downloadable for any specific date
- **XE.com Historical Rates** (xe.com) — free lookup for historical December 31 rates
- **Federal Reserve H.10 Statistical Release** — covers more currencies than Treasury, published weekly
- **Wall Street Journal published rates** — acceptable for currencies not on Treasury's list

Save a PDF of the rate page you used. Record the URL, the rate, and the access date. This documentation demonstrates a good-faith effort to find a reliable rate.

## Can Using the Wrong Rate Lead to Penalties?

Yes — a rate error that produces an inaccurate FBAR value is a filing deficiency under the IRS and FinCEN rules. Non-willful FBAR violations carry penalties up to $16,117 per violation under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). Willful violations carry the greater of $100,000 or 50% of the account balance under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). See [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306) for the currency conversion rule.

An honest rate error using a reasonable source is unlikely to trigger willful penalties. Nevertheless, using a current-day rate instead of the December 31 rate — or using your bank's retail rate without documentation — creates unnecessary risk. Document your source every year, regardless of which rate you use.

## How Do You Handle Multi-Currency Accounts?

Multi-currency foreign financial accounts require you to convert each currency separately using the December 31 Treasury rate for that currency, then total all USD equivalents to determine the account's maximum value. This applies to multi-currency accounts at fintech banks like Wise or Revolut and to international brokerage accounts holding balances in more than one currency simultaneously.

For example, an account holding €20,000 and £15,000 at its peak requires two conversions:

- €20,000 ÷ [EUR Treasury rate] = USD equivalent of the EUR portion
- £15,000 ÷ [GBP Treasury rate] = USD equivalent of the GBP portion
- Total USD maximum value = both amounts added together

Report the single combined total on your FBAR. You do not list each currency on a separate line within the same account.

### Accounts in India: NRE and NRO

Indian NRE and NRO accounts held in rupees use the INR December 31 Treasury rate. Treasury typically publishes the INR rate, but verify annually since coverage can change. For details on Indian account reporting, see [FBAR India NRE and NRO Accounts](/blog/fbar-india-nre-nro-accounts).

## How to Calculate Your Maximum Account Value Step by Step

Calculating the correct USD maximum value for a foreign financial account involves three clear steps. Review your full-year statements before starting — the peak balance can occur on any day of the year, not only at month-end or year-end.

1. **Find the highest balance in foreign currency.** Review monthly statements or the full transaction history. Look for the single highest daily balance during the calendar year.
2. **Convert using the December 31 Treasury rate.** Go to [fiscal.treasury.gov](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/reports-statements/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/current.html) and find your currency. Divide the peak foreign-currency balance by the Treasury rate to get the USD equivalent.
3. **Document your source.** Record the fiscal.treasury.gov URL, the rate used, and the date you accessed it. For alternative-source currencies, save a PDF showing the rate and date.

For more detail on the maximum value calculation, see [How to Calculate Maximum Account Value for FBAR](/blog/how-to-calculate-maximum-account-value-fbar).

## Frequently Asked Questions

These questions address the most common points of confusion about FBAR exchange rates. Each answer is a stand-alone summary you can use to verify your own conversion approach.

**Do I use the exchange rate on the date my balance peaked, or the FBAR exchange rate treasury December 31 rate?**

You use the Treasury December 31 exchange rate for the reporting year, regardless of when the peak balance occurred. Under [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306), the rule is the year-end rate applied to the maximum annual value. Therefore, if your Swiss franc account peaked in July, convert that July balance using the December 31 CHF rate for that same year.

**What if my currency is not listed on the Treasury website?**

Use a rate from a recognized source such as OANDA, XE.com, or the Federal Reserve H.10 release. Document the source, the rate, and the date you accessed it. Keep this for at least six years in case of IRS review. This record shows you made a good-faith effort to find a reliable rate rather than guessing.

**Can I use my bank's exchange rate instead of the Treasury rate?**

No. FinCEN requires the Treasury Department's year-end rate for FBAR purposes. Your bank's conversion rate includes retail spreads and may differ from the Treasury figure. Using a bank rate may create a discrepancy if the IRS compares your reported values against Treasury-rate calculations during an examination.

**What if I held the account for only part of the year?**

The December 31 rate still applies even if you closed the account before year-end. You report the maximum value the account held while open, converted at the December 31 Treasury rate for that calendar year. This applies to accounts closed mid-year. See [FBAR First-Time Filer Guide](/blog/fbar-first-time-filer-guide) for more on partial-year accounts.

**Does the FBAR exchange rate treasury rule differ from Form 8938 rules?**

Both the FBAR and Form 8938 (FATCA) under [26 USC 6038D](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D) use the Treasury year-end rate. However, they have different dollar thresholds. The FBAR threshold is $10,000 in aggregate across all foreign financial accounts under [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306). Form 8938 thresholds start at $50,000 for domestic filers. See [FBAR vs FATCA Form 8938](/blog/fbar-vs-fatca-form-8938-differences) for a full comparison.

**Do foreign retirement accounts use the same exchange rate rules?**

Yes. Foreign pension and retirement accounts reportable on the FBAR follow the same December 31 Treasury rate rule. This includes Canadian RRSPs, Australian retirement funds, and Israeli pension funds. The maximum value is the highest balance during the year, converted at the December 31 rate. Additionally, the $10,000 aggregate threshold applies across all such accounts.

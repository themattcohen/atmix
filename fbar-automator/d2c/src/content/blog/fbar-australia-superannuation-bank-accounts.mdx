# FBAR Australia Superannuation and Bank Account Reporting Guide

If you are a US person in Australia, the FBAR australia superannuation question comes up fast. Australia has an estimated 200,000 or more American citizens, dual citizens, and green card holders. Most hold Australian bank accounts. Most also have a superannuation (retirement fund) balance. Both are reportable under [31 USC 5314](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5314). The rules for super involve more steps than most filers expect.

The FBAR — officially the Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts (FinCEN Form 114) — is a Bank Secrecy Act (BSA) filing. You must file it when your foreign financial account balances exceed $10,000 in aggregate on any single day of the year. [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306) establishes this threshold. You convert Australian dollars (AUD) to USD using the Treasury year-end rate from [fiscal.treasury.gov](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/reports-statements/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/current.html).

This article covers which Australian accounts trigger FBAR reporting, how superannuation fits the foreign financial account definition, how to convert AUD balances, and what dual citizens need to know.

## Are Australian Bank Accounts Reportable on the FBAR?

Yes. Australian bank accounts are foreign financial accounts under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). You must report them when your aggregate foreign balances exceed $10,000 on any single day. This covers checking accounts, savings accounts, term deposits, and offset accounts linked to Australian mortgages.

Australia's four major banks — Commonwealth Bank of Australia (CBA), ANZ, NAB, and Westpac — are all financial institutions subject to FBAR reporting. If you are a US citizen, green card holder, or US resident for tax purposes, the FBAR rules apply to any accounts you hold at these banks.

### Which Account Types Are Covered?

The following Australian account types are reportable:

- **Transaction and savings accounts** — everyday banking accounts at any Australian bank or credit union
- **Term deposits** — fixed-term deposit accounts, similar to US CDs
- **Offset accounts** — accounts linked to Australian home loans
- **Investment accounts** — brokerage and share trading accounts at Australian institutions
- **SMSF accounts** — bank accounts held by a Self-Managed Super Fund where you are a trustee or member

An account is reportable whether you hold a beneficial interest or only signature authority. If you sign on a business account held by an Australian company, you may need to report it even without ownership. See [FBAR business accounts and signatory authority](/blog/fbar-business-accounts-signatory-authority) for details.

### FATCA and the Australia–US Information Agreement

Australia signed a Model 1 FATCA Intergovernmental Agreement (IGA) with the United States. Under this agreement, Australian banks report account data to the Australian Taxation Office (ATO). The ATO then shares it with the IRS. Therefore, the IRS may already have data on your Australian accounts. However, the FBAR is a separate obligation filed with FinCEN. For a comparison of the two, see [FBAR vs. FATCA Form 8938](/blog/fbar-vs-fatca-form-8938-differences).

## FBAR Australia Superannuation: Is Super Reportable?

Yes. Australian superannuation is reportable on the FBAR. A US person who is a member of an Australian super fund has a financial interest in a foreign financial account under [31 CFR 1010.350(e)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). The member's accumulated balance is the reportable amount — not employer contributions or the fund's total assets.

Many US persons think super is excluded from FBAR because it works like a pension. However, the IRS has not issued a specific exclusion for Australian superannuation. Until such guidance exists, super is a foreign financial account and the FBAR applies. See [FBAR reporting for foreign pension and retirement accounts](/blog/fbar-foreign-pension-retirement-accounts) for a broader comparison.

### Industry Super Funds

Industry super funds — such as AustralianSuper, REST, Hostplus, and HESTA — are accumulation funds. Your balance grows based on contributions and investment returns. You report your member balance at its highest point during the calendar year. You do not report the fund's total assets — only your individual account balance.

To find your reportable balance:

1. Log into your super fund's member portal
2. Find the member statement showing the maximum balance for the reporting year
3. Convert that AUD balance to USD using the Treasury year-end exchange rate
4. Report that USD amount on FinCEN Form 114

If you have accounts in multiple super funds — because you changed employers and have not yet consolidated — each fund is a separate account. List each one on the FBAR.

### Self-Managed Super Funds (SMSFs)

A Self-Managed Super Fund (SMSF) is a private super fund you control as trustee. If you are a US person and an SMSF trustee, you have both a financial interest in and signature authority over the fund's accounts. The SMSF's bank accounts, brokerage accounts, and other financial accounts are all reportable.

SMSFs carry additional US tax risk. Beyond FBAR, SMSFs that hold shares in foreign corporations or managed funds may trigger PFIC (Passive Foreign Investment Company) rules under IRC 1291. See [FBAR and foreign mutual funds — PFIC rules](/blog/fbar-foreign-mutual-funds-pfic) for more. SMSFs may also trigger Form 3520 rules for foreign trusts depending on the fund structure.

### What Is NOT Reportable: The Australian Age Pension

The Australian Age Pension is a government benefit. It is a social security payment the Australian government makes to eligible retirees. It is not a financial account. You do not report the Age Pension on the FBAR. A government-administered benefit program is not a "foreign financial account." This mirrors how US Social Security works — the right to future payments from a government program is not reportable.

| Account Type | Reportable on FBAR? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| CBA / ANZ / NAB / Westpac bank accounts | Yes | All account types |
| Term deposits | Yes | Include in aggregate |
| Industry super fund (AustralianSuper, REST, etc.) | Yes | Report member balance |
| SMSF bank account | Yes | Trustee has financial interest |
| SMSF share/investment account | Yes | Same trustee rule |
| Australian Age Pension | No | Government benefit, not an account |
| Defined benefit super (e.g., military, public service) | Consult a tax pro | Complex — may be excluded |

## Is Australian Superannuation Taxed in the US?

Australian superannuation may be taxed in the US. This is a separate question from FBAR reporting. The FBAR is a disclosure form — it does not create a tax. However, Australian super fund earnings and distributions may be subject to US income tax. Many super funds hold investments classified as PFICs under IRC 1291. PFIC taxation is punitive and complex. Additionally, the US-Australia tax treaty provides limited relief for pension income. Consult a cross-border tax professional to understand your US tax obligations on superannuation before you make any withdrawal.

Superannuation withdrawals — including the tax-free component under Australian law — may still be taxable in the US. Australian super is not treated the same as a US IRA or 401(k). The US-Australia tax treaty does not fully protect super from US tax. This is a common surprise for US persons who assumed Australian tax rules apply.

## How Do You Convert AUD to USD for the FBAR?

Convert AUD account balances to USD using the Treasury's year-end exchange rate. The Treasury publishes official rates at [fiscal.treasury.gov](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/reports-statements/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/current.html). For the 2025 FBAR (filed in 2026), use the rate as of December 31, 2025.

The conversion formula is:

> **USD amount = AUD balance × Treasury AUD/USD rate**

For example, if the Treasury year-end rate for 2025 is 0.6200 (AUD 1 = USD 0.62), an AUD 100,000 super balance converts to USD 62,000. You report $62,000 on the FBAR.

For a detailed walkthrough, see [FBAR exchange rates — using Treasury Department rates](/blog/fbar-exchange-rates-treasury-department). For help finding the maximum value across a full year, see [how to calculate maximum account value for FBAR](/blog/how-to-calculate-maximum-account-value-fbar).

### Using the Maximum Balance, Not Year-End Balance

You report the **maximum balance** during the year — not the December 31 balance. For most bank accounts, these are close. However, super funds can change a lot with employer contributions and market moves. Check your annual super fund statement for the highest balance recorded during the year.

If your statement shows only the year-end balance, use that. If it shows quarterly or monthly balances, use the highest one. When in doubt, use the highest balance you can document.

## Real-World Scenarios

### Scenario 1: Sydney Expat with CBA Account and AustralianSuper

Maria is a US citizen in Sydney. She holds a CBA account with a maximum balance of AUD 35,000 during 2025. She also holds an AustralianSuper account with a maximum balance of AUD 180,000. Using a Treasury year-end AUD rate of 0.6200:

- CBA account: AUD 35,000 × 0.6200 = USD 21,700
- AustralianSuper: AUD 180,000 × 0.6200 = USD 111,600
- Aggregate: USD 133,300

Maria must file the FBAR under [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306). She reports both accounts on FinCEN Form 114. She also checks her FATCA Form 8938 obligation. As an expat, her FATCA threshold is $200,000 at year-end or $300,000 at any point during the year under [26 USC 6038D](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D).

### Scenario 2: Dual Citizen with an SMSF

David holds dual US–Australian citizenship. He is a trustee and sole member of an SMSF. The SMSF holds an NAB bank account (AUD 25,000) and Australian shares in a broker account (AUD 320,000). Using a Treasury rate of 0.6200:

- NAB bank account: AUD 25,000 × 0.6200 = USD 15,500
- Broker account: AUD 320,000 × 0.6200 = USD 198,400

David must report both accounts on the FBAR. He also evaluates whether the SMSF investments trigger PFIC rules, which would require additional US tax reporting.

### Scenario 3: Recent Arrival — Mid-Year Move to Australia

Jennifer moved from the US to Melbourne in June 2025. She opened an ANZ account in July 2025 with AUD 8,000. She contributed AUD 15,000 to REST super through her employer by December 31, 2025. Her ANZ maximum during 2025 was AUD 8,000 (USD 4,960 at 0.6200). Her REST maximum was AUD 15,000 (USD 9,300). Her aggregate is USD 14,260 — above $10,000. She must file the FBAR for 2025 under [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306), even though she only held these accounts for part of the year.

## What Are the Penalties for Not Filing?

Failure to file the FBAR carries penalties under [31 USC 5321](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). Non-willful violations carry penalties up to $16,117 per violation (2025 inflation-adjusted figure) per [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). Willful violations carry penalties up to $100,000 or 50% of the account balance per violation, whichever is greater, per [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321).

Each unreported account in each year is a separate violation. For example, if you failed to report your CBA account and your AustralianSuper account for three years, that is six separate violations. Non-willful penalties alone could total $96,702 or more.

The IRS has access to Australian account data through the FATCA information-sharing program with the ATO. This increases the risk of detection. For more on penalties, see [FBAR penalties — what happens if you don't file](/blog/fbar-penalties-what-happens-if-you-dont-file).

### Catching Up If You Are Behind

If you have not reported your Australian accounts in prior years, two programs let you catch up with reduced penalties:

- **Streamlined Foreign Offshore Procedures** — for US persons living outside the United States, this program allows late filings with a 5% penalty on the highest aggregate unreported balance
- **Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures** — for filers with no unreported income who simply missed the form

See [FBAR streamlined filing compliance procedures](/blog/fbar-streamlined-filing-compliance-procedures) and [FBAR delinquent filing procedures](/blog/fbar-delinquent-filing-procedures) for eligibility rules.

## Does the US-Australia Tax Treaty Affect FBAR Filing?

No. The US-Australia tax treaty does not remove the FBAR obligation. Tax treaties cover income tax — they do not override the Bank Secrecy Act (BSA) reporting rules that require FBAR filing. Even if treaty provisions reduce your US income tax on Australian super income, you must still file the FBAR for any Australian accounts above the $10,000 threshold.

Dual US–Australian citizens follow the same FBAR rules as any other US person. Holding Australian citizenship does not reduce the filing obligation. The US taxes its citizens on worldwide income and requires worldwide account reporting under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350), unlike most other countries.

For Australian citizens who hold a US green card, the same rules apply. Green card holders are US persons for FBAR purposes. The obligation ends only when the green card is formally abandoned and US tax residency is terminated. See [FBAR for green card holders](/blog/fbar-green-card-holders) for more.

## Frequently Asked Questions

These frequently asked questions address FBAR australia superannuation reporting for US persons. The answers cover the FBAR filing threshold, superannuation account types, AUD-to-USD conversion, and what happens when you leave Australia. Each answer is based on [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350) and [31 USC 5314](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5314). Consult a qualified tax professional for advice specific to your situation.

**Does my Australian superannuation count toward the $10,000 FBAR threshold?**

Yes. Your super fund balance counts toward the $10,000 aggregate threshold. You add your super balance — converted to USD using the Treasury year-end rate — to any Australian bank account balances. If the total exceeds $10,000 on any day during the year, you must file. For calculation details, see [how to calculate maximum account value for FBAR](/blog/how-to-calculate-maximum-account-value-fbar).

### Do I have to declare superannuation on my tax return in Australia?

Yes. As a US person, you must report Australian super fund earnings and distributions on your US federal tax return. The FBAR is a separate disclosure you file with FinCEN — it does not replace income tax reporting. Additionally, you must file Form 8938 if your total foreign financial assets exceed the threshold under [26 USC 6038D](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D). Form 8938 goes to the IRS with your federal return.

### What happens to my superannuation if I leave Australia?

Your FBAR obligation does not end when you leave Australia. You must still file the FBAR for the year you depart if your balances exceeded $10,000 at any point that year. The maximum balance during the year sets the reportable amount. Withdrawing your super before December 31 does not remove the filing requirement — the balance before withdrawal is still the maximum value for the year. Once all Australian accounts are closed, the FBAR obligation ends.

**Are Australian superannuation withdrawals taxed in the US?**

Possibly. The US-Australia tax treaty does not fully protect super withdrawals from US tax. Australian super is not treated the same as a US IRA or 401(k). Even the tax-free component under Australian law may be taxable in the US. Additionally, investments held inside an SMSF or super fund may qualify as PFICs under IRC 1291, which carries its own tax rules. Consult a cross-border tax professional before you take a superannuation withdrawal.

**Do I also need Form 8938 for my Australian super fund?**

Possibly. Form 8938 Australian superannuation reporting is separate from the FBAR. You file Form 8938 with your federal tax return. It has higher thresholds — $200,000 at year-end (or $300,000 at any time) for single taxpayers living abroad under [26 USC 6038D](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D). If you meet both thresholds, you must file both the FBAR (with FinCEN) and Form 8938 (with the IRS under [26 USC 6038D](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D)). See [FBAR vs. FATCA Form 8938](/blog/fbar-vs-fatca-form-8938-differences) for a comparison.

**What is the FBAR filing deadline for Australian accounts?**

The FBAR is due April 15, with an automatic extension to October 15. No separate extension request is needed — the extension is automatic under [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306). Missing both deadlines can trigger penalties. For deadline details, see [FBAR filing deadline 2026](/blog/fbar-filing-deadline-2026).

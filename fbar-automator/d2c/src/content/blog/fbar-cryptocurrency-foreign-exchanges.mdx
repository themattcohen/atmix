## The Short Answer: Yes, Foreign Crypto Exchanges Are Reportable

If you hold cryptocurrency on a foreign exchange and the aggregate value of all your foreign financial accounts — including crypto — exceeds $10,000 at any point during the calendar year, you need to file an FBAR.

This wasn't always clear-cut. For years, taxpayers and practitioners debated whether cryptocurrency accounts on foreign exchanges fell within the scope of 31 USC 5314 and the Bank Secrecy Act's reporting requirements. FinCEN settled the question.

## FinCEN's Position on Cryptocurrency and FBARs

In December 2020, FinCEN issued a Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (NPRM) titled "Reporting Requirements Regarding Foreign Financial Accounts" that explicitly proposed amending 31 CFR 1010.350 to include virtual currency as a type of reportable account. The NPRM stated that FinCEN intended to require the reporting of holdings in foreign virtual currency accounts on the FBAR.

Before that, FinCEN Notice 2020-2 informed the public that FinCEN intended to propose amending FBAR regulations to include virtual currency as a reportable account type. The notice stated that while the rulemaking process was ongoing, FinCEN would not expect filers to report virtual currency accounts on the FBAR — but this was widely understood as a temporary reprieve, not an exemption.

Since then, FinCEN has confirmed that virtual currency held in accounts at foreign financial institutions is reportable. The BSA E-Filing system has been updated to accommodate these filings.

The practical takeaway: if you hold crypto on a foreign exchange, report it on your FBAR. The regulatory intent is unmistakable, and failing to report while the rules are being finalized exposes you to potential penalties under 31 USC 5321(a)(5) — up to $12,909 per non-willful violation.

## Which Crypto Exchanges Are "Foreign"?

This is the question that matters most. Whether you need to report an exchange account on your FBAR depends on where the exchange is domiciled — not where you access it from.

### Foreign Exchanges (FBAR-Reportable)

- **Binance** (international version): Domiciled outside the US. The main Binance platform, accessible at binance.com, is operated by entities registered in the Cayman Islands and other non-US jurisdictions. This is a foreign account. Note: Binance.US is a separate entity — see below.
- **Crypto.com**: Headquartered in Singapore. Accounts on Crypto.com are with a foreign financial institution.
- **Bybit**: Registered in the British Virgin Islands and operates from Dubai. Foreign.
- **KuCoin**: Incorporated in Seychelles. Foreign.
- **OKX** (formerly OKEx): Headquartered in Seychelles with operations in multiple countries. Foreign.
- **Bitfinex**: Registered in the British Virgin Islands and operated by iFinex Inc. Foreign.
- **Bitstamp**: Founded in Slovenia, now headquartered in Luxembourg. Foreign.

### US-Based Exchanges (NOT Reportable on FBAR)

- **Coinbase**: Incorporated in Delaware, headquartered in the US. Accounts at Coinbase are domestic — not reportable on the FBAR.
- **Kraken** (Payward Inc.): Incorporated in the US, headquartered in San Francisco. Domestic.
- **Gemini**: New York-based, regulated by the New York Department of Financial Services. Domestic.
- **Binance.US** (BAM Trading Services): This is the US-regulated entity, separate from the international Binance platform. Accounts at Binance.US are domestic.

### The Gray Areas

Some exchanges operate through complex corporate structures with entities in multiple jurisdictions. If you're unsure where your exchange is domiciled, check the Terms of Service — they typically identify the contracting entity. The entity you have a legal relationship with determines whether the account is "foreign."

If an exchange has both a US entity and a foreign parent, the relevant question is: which entity holds your account? If your account agreement is with the foreign entity, it's foreign.

## How to Determine Maximum Value for Crypto Accounts

Calculating the maximum value of a cryptocurrency account is conceptually the same as any other financial account, but the volatility of crypto assets makes it more challenging in practice.

Under 31 CFR 1010.350(g)(1), you need the highest value of the account at any point during the calendar year. For a crypto exchange account, this means:

### Step 1: Find Your Peak Portfolio Value

Review your exchange's account history or portfolio snapshots. Many exchanges provide daily portfolio value records. You need the single day (or moment) when your total holdings on that exchange were at their highest USD value.

This includes all assets on the exchange — not just Bitcoin. Your total holdings of BTC, ETH, stablecoins, altcoins, and any fiat balances all contribute to the account value.

### Step 2: Use the USD Value

If your exchange shows portfolio values in USD, use that. If it shows values in another currency (some exchanges default to EUR or GBP), convert to USD.

For the FBAR, you use the Treasury Department's December 31 exchange rate for currency conversions, but since crypto is typically denominated in USD on most exchanges, this step is usually straightforward.

### Example: Alex's Binance Account

Alex, a US citizen, has an account on Binance (international) with the following 2025 activity:

- **January**: Portfolio value ~$15,000 (0.3 BTC, 5 ETH, various altcoins)
- **March**: Added $20,000 in deposits, portfolio grew to ~$45,000
- **May**: Crypto bull run pushes portfolio to peak of **$85,000**
- **August**: Market correction drops portfolio to ~$52,000
- **December 31**: Portfolio at $61,000

Alex's maximum account value for FBAR purposes is **$85,000** — the peak in May. Not the December 31 balance of $61,000.

## Multiple Accounts on the Same Exchange

If you have multiple accounts or sub-accounts on the same exchange, these are generally treated as a single account for FBAR purposes, since they're all with the same financial institution.

However, if you have accounts on multiple exchanges, each exchange is a separate account:

### Example: Taylor's Multi-Exchange Portfolio

Taylor holds crypto on three foreign exchanges in 2025:

1. **Binance**: Peak portfolio value **$85,000** in May
2. **Crypto.com**: Peak portfolio value **$12,000** in September
3. **Bybit**: Peak portfolio value **$3,200** in November

Taylor also has accounts at Coinbase ($45,000) and a Chase checking account ($8,000). The Coinbase and Chase accounts are domestic and not FBAR-reportable.

For FBAR purposes, Taylor reports three foreign accounts with an aggregate maximum value of **$100,200**. Each account's individual maximum value is reported separately on the FBAR.

Taylor would also want to note that even if the Binance account alone ($85,000) well exceeds the $10,000 threshold under 31 USC 5314, all three foreign accounts must be reported once the threshold is met.

## The $10,000 Threshold: Crypto + Traditional Accounts Combined

A critical point many crypto holders miss: the $10,000 aggregate threshold applies across **all** foreign financial accounts, not just crypto accounts.

If you have a foreign bank account with £5,000 ($6,350 USD) and a Binance account that peaked at $4,500, your aggregate is $10,850 — and you need to file an FBAR reporting both accounts.

### Example: Jamie's Mixed Portfolio

Jamie has:
- **Bank of Ireland savings account**: Peak balance €6,000 ($6,600 USD)
- **Revolut account** (headquartered in London): Peak balance £2,500 ($3,175 USD)
- **Binance account**: Peak value $4,000
- **Coinbase account**: Peak value $50,000 (domestic — doesn't count)

Aggregate foreign account maximum values: $6,600 + $3,175 + $4,000 = **$13,775**

Jamie exceeds the $10,000 threshold and must file an FBAR reporting all three foreign accounts. The $50,000 on Coinbase is irrelevant because Coinbase is a US institution.

## DeFi Protocols and Foreign-Domiciled Platforms

Decentralized finance (DeFi) presents the hardest questions for FBAR reporting. The FBAR applies to accounts with "financial institutions" — and whether a DeFi protocol constitutes a financial institution is an open question.

### What We Know

- **Centralized exchanges with DeFi features** (like Binance's DeFi staking or Crypto.com's DeFi wallet with exchange integration): These are likely reportable because the underlying account is with a foreign centralized entity.
- **Pure DeFi protocols** (like Uniswap, Aave, or Compound): These are decentralized smart contracts, not financial institutions in the traditional sense. FinCEN has not issued definitive guidance on whether liquidity positions or lending positions on decentralized protocols are FBAR-reportable.
- **Self-custody wallets** (MetaMask, Ledger, Trezor): These are not accounts with a financial institution. You control the private keys directly. These are generally not FBAR-reportable under current guidance.

### The Conservative Approach

Given the trajectory of FinCEN's rulemaking and the increasing regulatory focus on DeFi, many tax practitioners recommend reporting any significant DeFi position held through a foreign-domiciled platform. The cost of over-reporting is zero additional penalty; the cost of under-reporting could be $12,909 or more per violation.

If you hold $50,000 in a DeFi lending protocol accessed through a foreign platform, the conservative play is to report it. The aggressive play is to argue it's not an "account" with a "financial institution" — but you'd want a tax attorney in your corner for that argument.

## Common Crypto FBAR Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Thinking "I Already Pay Taxes on My Crypto"

The FBAR is not a tax form. It's a Bank Secrecy Act reporting requirement under 31 USC 5314. Even if you report every dollar of crypto gains on your Form 8949 and Schedule D, you still need to file the FBAR separately if you hold crypto on foreign exchanges exceeding the threshold.

### Mistake 2: Using December 31 Balance Instead of Maximum Value

Crypto is volatile. Your December 31 balance could be 40% lower than your peak. The FBAR requires the maximum value during the calendar year. If your Binance portfolio peaked at $120,000 in April but ended the year at $72,000, your maximum account value is $120,000.

### Mistake 3: Not Knowing Which Exchanges Are Foreign

"I use Binance" doesn't tell me enough. Are you using Binance (international) or Binance.US? They're different entities. Similarly, some exchanges have changed domiciles or restructured. Check your account agreement.

### Mistake 4: Forgetting About Stablecoins

Stablecoins count toward your account value. If you hold $30,000 in USDT on Binance, that's $30,000 of foreign account value — even though you might not think of stablecoins as "crypto" in the traditional sense.

### Mistake 5: Ignoring Fiat Balances on Foreign Exchanges

If you deposited $15,000 USD into your Binance account and haven't converted it to crypto yet, it's still $15,000 sitting in a foreign financial institution's account. It counts.

### Mistake 6: Treating NFTs Differently

If you hold NFTs on a foreign exchange platform, the value of those NFTs is part of your account value on that platform. An NFT valued at $8,000 held on a foreign marketplace contributes to your aggregate foreign account total.

## What Happens If You Haven't Been Reporting Crypto Accounts

If you've been holding crypto on foreign exchanges for years without filing FBARs, you need to come into compliance. The options are similar to any other unreported foreign account:

**Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures**: If you have no unreported income (for example, you held but didn't sell crypto), you may be able to file delinquent FBARs with a reasonable cause explanation. The IRS generally doesn't assess penalties under these procedures if there's no corresponding tax understatement.

**Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures**: If you have unreported crypto income (gains from sales, staking rewards, DeFi yields), the Streamlined procedures let you file 3 years of amended returns and 6 years of FBARs with reduced penalties. The penalty under Streamlined Domestic is 5% of the highest aggregate balance — so if your peak foreign account aggregate was $100,000, the penalty would be $5,000.

**Quiet Disclosure** (filing without using a formal program): This is risky. If the IRS discovers unreported foreign accounts before or during an examination, penalties can be assessed at the full statutory rates — up to $12,909 per non-willful violation or the greater of $100,000 or 50% of the account balance per willful violation under 31 USC 5321(a)(5).

## Key Takeaways for Crypto Holders

1. **Foreign crypto exchange accounts are FBAR-reportable** under current FinCEN guidance and proposed rules.
2. **"Foreign" is based on where the exchange is domiciled**, not where you access it. Binance (international) is foreign; Coinbase is domestic.
3. **Maximum value means peak portfolio value** during the year — not the year-end balance. Crypto volatility makes this especially significant.
4. **Crypto and traditional accounts aggregate together** for the $10,000 threshold. A $6,000 foreign bank account plus a $5,000 foreign crypto account means you file.
5. **Stablecoins, fiat balances, and NFTs on foreign exchanges** all count toward account value.
6. **Self-custody wallets are generally not reportable**, but centralized exchange accounts are.
7. **DeFi is a gray area** — the conservative approach is to report positions on foreign-domiciled platforms.
8. **Come into compliance voluntarily** through Streamlined or Delinquent FBAR procedures if you've been missing filings. The penalties for getting caught are far worse than the cost of voluntary disclosure.

# FBAR Green Card Holders: What Permanent Residents Must Know

If you hold a green card, you must file an FBAR. The Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts (FBAR) applies to every US person. Green card holders are US persons under federal law. Therefore, the FBAR rules for green card holders match the rules for US citizens exactly.

Specifically, if your foreign accounts exceeded $10,000 at any point during the year, you must file the FBAR form ([FinCEN Form 114](https://www.fincen.gov/report-foreign-bank-and-financial-accounts)). FBAR green card holders are frequently surprised by this rule. However, once you become a lawful permanent resident, every foreign account you hold becomes reportable. Do green card holders have to pay taxes on foreign income? Yes, and the FBAR is a separate reporting requirement on top of your green card holder tax return. This guide covers your filing obligations, which accounts count, and what to do if you are behind.

## Are Green Card Holders Required to File an FBAR?

Yes. Green card holders are "United States persons" under [31 CFR 1010.350(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). That regulation lists citizens, resident aliens, and lawfully admitted permanent residents as US persons. Additionally, [8 USC 1101(a)(20)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/8/1101) defines a lawful permanent resident as someone who enters the US under the Immigration and Nationality Act. As a result, the same FBAR rules that apply to citizens apply to you.

If your foreign accounts topped $10,000 at any point during the year, you must file with [FinCEN](https://www.fincen.gov/report-foreign-bank-and-financial-accounts). There are no exceptions based on your home country. There are no exceptions based on how long you have held the green card. Furthermore, tax treaty elections do not remove your FBAR obligation. The treaty may reduce your income tax. It does not eliminate FinCEN reporting duties.

## When Does the FBAR Filing Obligation Start for Green Card Holders?

Your FBAR obligation starts the calendar year you receive your green card. The key date is the date on your I-551 or passport stamp. The FBAR covers a full calendar year. In other words, even if you got your green card on December 15, you must check whether your foreign accounts exceeded $10,000 at any point that year.

The FBAR does not prorate based on how many months you held the card. You report the highest balance for the entire year. That includes months before you received the green card.

### Example: Priya's First-Year Filing

Priya got her green card on September 8, 2025. She has a bank account in India with about $18,000 throughout 2025.

Even though Priya held the card for only four months, the FBAR covers the full year. Her Indian account topped $10,000, so she must file an FBAR for 2025. She reports the highest balance for the entire year. The deadline is April 15, 2026. FinCEN grants an automatic extension to October 15, 2026.

## Do Accounts in Your Home Country Count for FBAR?

Yes. All foreign accounts count for FBAR purposes. This includes accounts you opened long before moving to the United States. Under [31 CFR 1010.350(c)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350), any account at a bank outside the US is a foreign financial account. It does not matter when you opened it. It does not matter how often you use it.

For example, you must report an account you held for decades once you become a permanent resident. This surprises filers who assumed old accounts would not count. Additionally, you must report retirement accounts in your home country. Foreign pension plans, provident funds, and superannuation accounts all qualify under FinCEN rules.

## Do Joint Accounts With a Non-US Spouse Need FBAR Reporting?

Yes. You report the full account value, not just your share. If you have a financial interest in or signature authority over a joint account, you report 100 percent of that balance. Under FinCEN's Form 114 instructions, each US person reports the entire maximum value. You do not split the amount. This rule applies even if your spouse has no FBAR obligation. Therefore, do not report only half the balance.

For detailed rules on spousal filing, see our guide on [FBAR joint account reporting rules](/blog/fbar-joint-account-reporting-rules).

### Example: Carlos and Maria

Carlos is a green card holder. His wife Maria is a Mexican citizen with no US immigration status. They share a joint account at Banorte in Mexico. The peak balance in 2025 was about $20,000 USD.

Carlos must file an FBAR and report the full $20,000. Maria has no FBAR obligation because she is not a US person. However, if Maria later gets a green card, she must also file her own FBAR. She would then report the full $20,000 for the same account.

## FBAR Obligations for Green Card Holders vs Other US Persons

Many green card holders wonder if their rules differ from citizens or visa holders. The short answer: they do not. The table below compares FBAR obligations across different types of US persons. As you can see, the rules are identical.

| Obligation | Green Card Holders | US Citizens | Resident Aliens (Substantial Presence) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Must file FBAR? | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Threshold | $10,000 aggregate | $10,000 aggregate | $10,000 aggregate |
| Home-country accounts reportable? | Yes | Yes | Yes |

| Tax treaty exemption? | No | No | No |

## What Are the FBAR Penalties for Green Card Holders Who Do Not File?

Penalties are severe. FinCEN applies them equally to green card holders and US citizens. The government does not distinguish between types of US persons when calculating FBAR penalties. Here is a breakdown of the penalty structure.

**Non-willful penalties**: Up to **$12,909 per violation** per year under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)(i)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). Each unreported account per year counts as one violation. For example, three unreported accounts over two years could mean six violations and $77,454 in penalties.

**Willful penalties**: The greater of **$100,000** or **50% of the account balance** per year under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). The IRS applies this per account, not per report.

**Criminal penalties**: Up to **$250,000** in fines and **5 years** imprisonment under [31 USC 5322](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5322). The DOJ reserves criminal prosecution for cases involving deliberate concealment or tax evasion.

For a full breakdown of penalty scenarios, see our [FBAR penalties guide](/blog/fbar-penalties-what-happens-if-you-dont-file).

## What Should Green Card Holders Do If They Have Not Been Filing?

Green card holders who missed past FBAR filings should come into compliance before the IRS contacts them. The earlier you act, the better your outcome. The [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/report-of-foreign-bank-and-financial-accounts-fbar) offers two main programs for late filers: the Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures and the Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures. Both programs reduce or eliminate penalties for green card holders who did not know about the requirement.

The [IRS Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/streamlined-filing-compliance-procedures) work as follows:

1. File the last **3 years** of delinquent tax returns (if needed)
2. File the last **6 years** of delinquent FBARs
3. Pay a **5% offshore penalty** (Domestic) or **no penalty** (Foreign Streamlined)
4. Certify that your failure was non-willful

For example, a green card holder with $150,000 in unreported accounts pays a $7,500 Streamlined penalty. That is far less than $77,454 in non-willful penalties for six violations.

If your foreign accounts earned no income, check the [Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/delinquent-fbar-submission-procedures). The IRS can waive penalties when you reported all income correctly.

## How Do Green Card Holders File an FBAR?

Green card holders file the FBAR through the BSA E-Filing system at FinCEN. The process takes most filers 15 to 30 minutes once they gather their account statements. You do not file the FBAR with your green card holder tax return. Instead, the FBAR form goes directly to FinCEN, separate from the IRS. Gather the following information for each account:

1. **Your information**: Name, SSN or ITIN, date of birth, address
2. **Each account's details**: Bank name, account number, account type
3. **Maximum value** for the year, in USD using [Treasury exchange rates](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/reports-statements/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/current.html)
4. **Country** where each account is held

File through the [BSA E-Filing system](https://bsaefiling.fincen.gov/main.html) at FinCEN. The deadline is April 15. An automatic extension to October 15 applies. No form is needed to get that extension.

## Frequently Asked Questions

Here are the most common questions about FBAR filing for green card holders. These answers cover filing obligations, deadlines, penalties, and joint filing rules. For questions about your specific situation, consult a qualified tax professional or contact the IRS FBAR hotline at 1-866-270-0733.

**Do green card holders need to file an FBAR?**
Yes. Green card holders are United States persons under [31 CFR 1010.350(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). The FBAR applies to all US persons whose foreign accounts exceed $10,000 in aggregate at any point during the year. There is no exception for permanent residents. The rules are identical to those for US citizens. You must report every foreign account, regardless of location or how long you have held it.

**When does the FBAR filing obligation start for green card holders?**
Your obligation begins the calendar year you receive your green card. The relevant date is the date on your I-551 card or passport stamp. Even if you got your green card in December, you must check whether your foreign accounts exceeded $10,000 at any point during that entire calendar year. FinCEN does not prorate based on months of residency, according to [FinCEN guidance](https://www.fincen.gov/report-foreign-bank-and-financial-accounts).

**Do green card holders report accounts from their home country on the FBAR?**
Yes. Under [31 CFR 1010.350(c)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350), any financial account held at a bank outside the United States is a foreign financial account. This includes accounts you opened before moving to the US. It also includes retirement accounts, pension plans, and savings accounts in your home country. The key factor is the location of the institution, not when you opened the account.

**What are the FBAR penalties for green card holders who don't file?**
Non-willful penalties can reach $12,909 per account per year under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). Willful penalties can reach $100,000 or 50 percent of the account balance, whichever is greater. Criminal penalties include fines up to $250,000 and up to 5 years in prison. The IRS does not distinguish between green card holders and citizens when assessing penalties.

**Can green card holders file the FBAR jointly with a spouse?**
Yes, if both spouses are US persons and all accounts are jointly owned. Both spouses must sign the FBAR. If either spouse holds an account alone, that spouse must file a separate FBAR. The non-filing spouse must complete [FinCEN Form 114a](https://www.fincen.gov/report-foreign-bank-and-financial-accounts) and retain it for at least five years. See our [FBAR joint account reporting guide](/blog/fbar-joint-account-reporting-rules) for more details.

**Does the FBAR apply after I give up my green card?**
Your FBAR obligation ends the year you formally surrender your permanent resident status. However, you must file for every year you held the green card. If you gave it up in 2025, you still need to file the 2025 FBAR for all accounts held during that year.

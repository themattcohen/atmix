## Green Card Holders Are US Persons — Full Stop

If you hold a green card, you are a "United States person" for FBAR purposes. This isn't a gray area. Under 31 USC 5314 and 31 CFR 1010.350(b), a US person includes any individual who is a resident alien — and that's exactly what a green card holder is.

This means the same rules that apply to US citizens apply to you: if the aggregate maximum value of your foreign financial accounts exceeds $10,000 at any point during the calendar year, you must file FinCEN Form 114 (the FBAR).

I work with green card holders every year who are surprised by this. Many come from countries where there's no equivalent reporting requirement. Some have held accounts in their home country for decades without ever thinking about US reporting obligations. But once you become a permanent resident, those accounts become reportable.

## When Does Your Filing Obligation Begin?

Your FBAR filing obligation starts the calendar year you become a lawful permanent resident. The key date is when your green card is issued — the date on your I-551 or the date stamped on your passport.

Here's what catches people: the FBAR is a calendar year filing. Even if you received your green card on December 15, you'd need to evaluate whether your foreign accounts exceeded $10,000 in aggregate at **any point during that entire calendar year**. The FBAR doesn't prorate.

### Example: Priya's First-Year Filing

Priya received her green card on September 8, 2025. She has an account at HDFC Bank in India that held ₹1,500,000 (approximately $18,000 at the December 31 Treasury rate) throughout 2025.

Even though Priya was only a permanent resident for the last four months of 2025, the FBAR covers the full calendar year. Since her Indian account exceeded $10,000 in aggregate, she must file an FBAR for 2025 reporting the account's maximum value during the entire year — including the months before she became a resident.

The filing deadline is April 15, 2026, with an automatic extension to October 15, 2026 under 31 CFR 1010.306(c).

## Accounts in Your Home Country — Yes, They Count

The most common situation I see with green card holders is the "I've had this account forever" scenario. You had a bank account in your home country long before you moved to the United States. It's where your family deposits birthday money. It's where your old salary used to go. You barely think about it.

It's reportable.

Under 31 CFR 1010.350(c), any financial account maintained with a financial institution located outside the United States is a foreign financial account. It doesn't matter when you opened it, how often you use it, or whether you consider it "active."

### Common Account Types by Country

**India**: Savings accounts at SBI, HDFC, ICICI, and other banks. NRE (Non-Resident External) and NRO (Non-Resident Ordinary) accounts are both reportable. Fixed deposits (FDs) are reportable. PPF (Public Provident Fund) accounts are reportable. The National Pension Scheme (NPS) is reportable if it meets the definition of a financial account.

**United Kingdom**: Current accounts, savings accounts, ISAs (Individual Savings Accounts — both Cash ISAs and Stocks & Shares ISAs), SIPPs (Self-Invested Personal Pensions) that hold financial assets. The fact that an ISA is tax-free in the UK is irrelevant — it's still a foreign financial account for FBAR purposes.

**Canada**: Chequing and savings accounts, TFSAs (Tax-Free Savings Accounts), RRSPs (Registered Retirement Savings Plans), and RRIF accounts. Again, the tax-advantaged status in Canada doesn't exempt these from FBAR reporting. A TFSA with C$40,000 (approximately $29,600 USD) is absolutely reportable.

**Australia**: Transaction accounts, savings accounts, term deposits, superannuation accounts that you have the ability to direct investment of (self-managed super funds, or SMSFs, with foreign financial assets).

**Philippines**: Savings and checking accounts at BDO, BPI, Metrobank, and other institutions. OFW (Overseas Filipino Worker) accounts are reportable.

## Joint Accounts With a Non-US Spouse

This scenario comes up constantly. You're a green card holder, your spouse is not a US person, and you share a bank account in your home country.

**If you have signature authority or a financial interest in the account, you report the full value.** Not half. The full value.

Under FinCEN's instructions for Form 114, each US person with a financial interest in or signature authority over a foreign account must report the entire maximum value of that account.

### Example: Carlos and Maria

Carlos received his green card in 2024. His wife Maria is a Mexican citizen with no US immigration status. They maintain a joint account at Banorte in Mexico City with a peak balance of MXN 350,000 during 2025 (approximately $20,000 USD at the December 31 Treasury rate).

Carlos must file an FBAR reporting the full $20,000 value of the Banorte account. Maria has no FBAR filing obligation because she is not a US person.

If Maria later obtains a green card, she would then also need to file her own FBAR reporting the same account at full value.

## Specific Scenarios Green Card Holders Face

### Scenario 1: Indian NRI Accounts

Anand moved to the US on an EB-2 visa and received his green card in 2023. He has:

- NRE savings account at ICICI Bank: peak balance ₹800,000 ($9,581 USD)
- NRO fixed deposit at SBI: peak balance ₹1,200,000 ($14,371 USD)
- PPF account: balance ₹500,000 ($5,988 USD)

Aggregate maximum value: $29,940. Anand must file an FBAR reporting all three accounts.

The NRE/NRO distinction matters for Indian tax purposes, but for US FBAR reporting, both are foreign financial accounts and both are reportable.

### Scenario 2: UK ISA Accounts

Emma, a British citizen with a US green card, has:

- Cash ISA at Nationwide: peak balance £15,000 ($19,050 USD)
- Stocks & Shares ISA at Vanguard UK: peak portfolio value £62,000 ($78,740 USD)
- Current account at Lloyds: peak balance £3,500 ($4,445 USD)

Aggregate maximum value: $102,235. Emma must file an FBAR reporting all three accounts.

Many UK expats don't realize their ISAs are FBAR-reportable because ISAs are tax-exempt in the UK. The FBAR has nothing to do with whether an account is taxable — it's a reporting requirement under the Bank Secrecy Act, not the Internal Revenue Code.

### Scenario 3: Canadian TFSA and RRSP

David, a Canadian citizen and US green card holder, has:

- TFSA at TD Canada Trust: peak value C$88,000 ($65,120 USD)
- RRSP at RBC: peak value C$145,000 ($107,300 USD)
- Chequing account at Scotiabank: peak balance C$6,200 ($4,588 USD)

Aggregate maximum value: $177,008. David must file an FBAR and report all three accounts.

Note: While the RRSP may receive treaty benefits under the US-Canada tax treaty (Article XVIII), the FBAR reporting obligation is separate from any tax treaty provisions. The TFSA gets no US tax treaty benefit and is fully taxable for US income tax purposes — but that's a tax question, not an FBAR question.

## Penalties for Non-Compliance

The penalties for failing to file an FBAR are severe, and they apply equally to green card holders as they do to US citizens.

### Non-Willful Penalties

Under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)(i), the penalty for a non-willful FBAR violation is up to **$12,909 per violation** (2026 inflation-adjusted amount). Each unreported account in each year can constitute a separate violation.

If you had three unreported accounts across two years, that's potentially six violations — up to **$77,454** in penalties even if the IRS considers your failure non-willful.

### Willful Penalties

Under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C), willful violations carry a penalty of the greater of **$100,000** or **50% of the account balance** at the time of the violation. For an account with $200,000, that's a $100,000 penalty per year.

### Criminal Penalties

Under 31 USC 5322, willful failure to file an FBAR can result in criminal penalties: up to **$250,000** in fines and up to **5 years** imprisonment. While criminal prosecution is rare, it does happen — particularly in cases involving large undisclosed accounts and clear evidence of willful concealment.

The IRS has been particularly focused on offshore account enforcement since the OVDP (Offshore Voluntary Disclosure Program) era. Green card holders with substantial home-country accounts are exactly the population that draws scrutiny.

## What If You Haven't Been Filing?

If you've had your green card for several years and didn't know about the FBAR requirement, you have options. The key is to come into compliance before the IRS comes to you.

### IRS Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures

If your failure to file was non-willful — meaning you didn't know about the requirement or had a reasonable misunderstanding — the Streamlined procedures are designed for you. Under the Streamlined Foreign Offshore Procedures (for taxpayers living abroad) or Streamlined Domestic Offshore Procedures (for US-based filers):

- File the last **3 years** of delinquent tax returns (if needed)
- File the last **6 years** of delinquent FBARs
- Pay a **5% miscellaneous offshore penalty** on the highest aggregate balance (Domestic Streamlined) or **no penalty** (Foreign Streamlined)
- Certify under penalty of perjury that your failure was non-willful

For a green card holder with $150,000 in unreported foreign accounts, the Streamlined Domestic penalty would be $7,500 (5% of $150,000) — far less than the $77,454 potential non-willful penalties for six accounts over two years.

### Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures

If you had no unreported income on your foreign accounts (for example, a non-interest-bearing checking account), you may qualify for the Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures. Under these procedures, you file the delinquent FBARs with an explanation, and the IRS generally doesn't impose penalties if there's no underreported income.

## Filing Your FBAR as a Green Card Holder

The filing process is identical whether you're a US citizen or a green card holder. You'll need:

1. **Your identifying information**: Name, SSN or ITIN, date of birth, address
2. **Account details for each foreign account**: Bank name, account number, type of account
3. **Maximum value of each account** during the calendar year, converted to USD using Treasury Department rates
4. **The country** where each account is maintained

The FBAR is filed electronically through the BSA E-Filing system. The deadline is April 15, with an automatic extension to October 15 — no extension form needed (31 CFR 1010.306(c)).

## Key Points for Green Card Holders

- **You are a US person** from the date your green card is issued. FBAR filing obligations apply immediately.
- **Home-country accounts you've held for years** are now reportable. NRE/NRO accounts, ISAs, TFSAs, RRSPs — all of them.
- **Joint accounts with non-US spouses** are reported at full value by the US person.
- **Penalties are substantial**: up to $12,909 per violation for non-willful failures, much more for willful violations.
- **Coming into compliance voluntarily** is far better than waiting for enforcement. The Streamlined procedures exist specifically for situations like yours.
- **The FBAR is a separate filing from your tax return**. Even if you file your 1040 on time, you can still be penalized for a missing FBAR.

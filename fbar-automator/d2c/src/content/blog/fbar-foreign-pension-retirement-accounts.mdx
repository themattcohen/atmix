# FBAR Foreign Pension Accounts: What You Must Report

Many U.S. persons with foreign pension and retirement accounts ask: does my pension need to go on the FBAR? The Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts (FBAR) — FinCEN Form 114 — covers foreign pension accounts in many cases. The answer depends on the pension type, whether you hold a "financial interest" under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350), and whether the account carries a reportable balance.

Under [31 USC 5314](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5314), any U.S. person with a financial interest in foreign financial accounts with an aggregate value exceeding $10,000 at any point during the year must file FinCEN Form 114. Foreign pension FBAR reporting catches many U.S. expats and green card holders off guard. Pension accounts at foreign financial institutions can exceed this threshold.

The key question is not whether an account is labeled a "pension." The question is whether that account constitutes a "financial account" at a foreign financial institution and whether you have a "financial interest" in it under [31 CFR 1010.350(e)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). Understanding these two tests is the foundation for correct FBAR reporting.

## Does the Financial Interest Test Apply to FBAR Foreign Pension Accounts?

Yes, the financial interest test under 31 CFR 1010.350(e) applies to FBAR foreign pension accounts the same way it applies to bank or brokerage accounts. You have a financial interest if you are the owner of record or hold legal title, or if another person holds the account on your behalf. For defined contribution (DC) pensions — where you own individual units or shares — you are the owner of record and you have a financial interest reportable on the FBAR.

### Defined Contribution vs. Defined Benefit Pensions

The distinction between defined contribution (DC) and defined benefit (DB) pensions is the most important factor in your FBAR analysis.

A **defined contribution pension** credits your account with specific contributions and investment returns. You own a measurable account balance. Examples include UK Self-Invested Personal Pensions (SIPPs), UK workplace DC pensions, Australian superannuation funds, and Canadian RRSPs. You have a financial interest and the account is reportable once the $10,000 aggregate threshold is met under 31 CFR 1010.306.

A **defined benefit pension** pays a fixed monthly income based on salary and years of service. No individual account balance belongs to you. The employer bears the investment risk. Most U.S. tax practitioners hold that DB pensions do not give rise to a reportable FBAR interest. There is no "maximum value" to report. See the [IRS FAQ on FBAR](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/report-of-foreign-bank-and-financial-accounts-fbar) for current guidance.

### Government Social Insurance Programs

Government-sponsored social insurance programs are not foreign financial accounts for FBAR purposes. These include:

- **UK State Pension** — a government benefit, not a financial institution account
- **Canada Pension Plan (CPP)** — a statutory government program
- **Australia Age Pension** — a means-tested government benefit
- **U.S. Social Security equivalents abroad**

None of these require FBAR reporting. You report them for income tax purposes but not on FinCEN Form 114.

## Which Foreign Pension Accounts Are FBAR Reportable?

Different pension structures produce different FBAR outcomes. Defined contribution plans with an owned account balance are reportable. Defined benefit plans with no personal balance are generally not. The table below covers the most common pension types by country and their FBAR reportability status.

| Country | Pension Type | Structure | FBAR Reportable? |
|---------|-------------|-----------|-----------------|
| UK | SIPP (Self-Invested Personal Pension) | Defined contribution | Yes — you own the account |
| UK | Workplace DC pension | Defined contribution | Yes — you own the account |
| UK | Workplace DB pension | Defined benefit | Generally no — no owned balance |
| UK | State Pension | Government benefit | No — not a financial account |
| Australia | Superannuation (accumulation phase) | Defined contribution | Yes — you own the balance |
| Australia | Superannuation (pension phase) | Annuity/income stream | Unclear — likely yes |
| Australia | Age Pension | Government benefit | No — not a financial account |
| Canada | RRSP (Registered Retirement Savings Plan) | Defined contribution | Yes — you own the account |
| Canada | RRIF (Registered Retirement Income Fund) | Defined contribution | Yes — you own the account |
| Canada | CPP (Canada Pension Plan) | Government benefit | No — not a financial account |
| Canada | TFSA (Tax-Free Savings Account) | Savings/investment | Yes — you own the account |
| Israel | Kupat Gemel (provident fund) | Defined contribution | Yes — you own the balance |

| France | PER (Plan d'Épargne Retraite) | Individual or collective | Yes for individual PER; DB component unclear |

### UK Pensions and FBAR Reporting

If you have a UK SIPP or workplace DC pension, you must report it on your FBAR when your aggregate foreign balances exceeded $10,000 at any time during the year. The SIPP is held at a UK pension provider. You own the account balance. Your account fund value is the "maximum value" for FBAR purposes. Convert it to U.S. dollars using the [Treasury's year-end exchange rates](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/reports-statements/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/current.html). See our [exchange rate article](/blog/fbar-exchange-rates-treasury-department) for details.

UK workplace DB pensions — such as a final salary pension — present a different picture. No personal account exists. The employer holds assets and pays a benefit in retirement. Most practitioners do not report DB pensions on FBAR. However, if a DB scheme includes an Additional Voluntary Contributions (AVC) sub-account, that AVC account is separately reportable. For UK accounts broadly, see [FBAR reporting for UK bank accounts](/blog/fbar-uk-bank-accounts).

### Australian Superannuation and FBAR

Australian superannuation (called "super") is a DC retirement fund held at an Australian financial institution. Your employer contributes a percentage of your salary into your personal super account. You own that balance. Super funds in accumulation phase are reportable on FBAR under 31 CFR 1010.350(e).

If your super balance was AUD 180,000 at its peak in 2025 and the Treasury rate for AUD on December 31, 2025 was 0.62, the reportable maximum value is about $111,600. That is well above the $10,000 threshold. For comprehensive coverage, see [FBAR reporting for Australian superannuation and bank accounts](/blog/fbar-australia-superannuation-bank-accounts).

### Canadian RRSP and RRIF Reporting

The Canadian Registered Retirement Savings Plan (RRSP) and its converted form, the Registered Retirement Income Fund (RRIF), are both reportable foreign financial accounts. You own the account and its balance. FinCEN confirmed in 2014 that RRSPs and RRIFs are subject to FBAR reporting — even though the Canada-U.S. tax treaty defers income tax on RRSP growth under Article XVIII. The two obligations are separate. See [FBAR reporting for Canadian RRSP and TFSA accounts](/blog/fbar-canada-rrsp-tfsa-reporting).

The Canadian Tax-Free Savings Account (TFSA) is also reportable. It is a financial account at a Canadian institution with a balance you own.

## How Do You Calculate Maximum Value for FBAR Pension Reporting?

For each reportable FBAR foreign pension account, you must determine the maximum value during the calendar year. FinCEN Form 114 asks for the maximum value, not the year-end value. Use the four-step process below.

1. **Find the highest account balance** at any point during the reporting year. Check your statements — the peak balance may occur mid-year.
2. **Convert to U.S. dollars** using the Treasury year-end exchange rate for December 31 of the reporting year. See [how to calculate maximum account value for FBAR](/blog/how-to-calculate-maximum-account-value-fbar) for full instructions.
3. **Aggregate all foreign accounts**. If your UK SIPP is $45,000 and your Canadian RRSP is $30,000 in USD terms, your aggregate is $75,000. Both accounts are reportable.
4. **File FinCEN Form 114** through BSA E-Filing by April 15, with an automatic extension to October 15.

## Are Foreign Pensions Reportable on Form 8938?

Yes, foreign pension accounts that meet the financial interest test under [31 CFR 1010.350(e)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350) are generally also reportable on Form 8938 under FATCA. FATCA requires U.S. persons to file Form 8938 with their federal tax return when foreign financial assets exceed certain thresholds. For single filers in the U.S., the threshold is $50,000 on the last day of the year or $75,000 at any time during the year under [26 USC 6038D](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D). Thresholds are higher for joint filers and for taxpayers abroad ($200,000/$300,000 for single, $400,000/$600,000 for joint).

FBAR and FATCA are separate obligations with different thresholds and different forms. Your RRSP or SIPP may require FBAR reporting even if it falls below the FATCA threshold. For a comparison, see [FBAR vs. FATCA Form 8938 differences](/blog/fbar-vs-fatca-form-8938-differences).

### What About Tax Treaty Exemptions?

U.S. tax treaties with Canada and the UK provide income tax benefits for certain foreign pension accounts. The Canada-U.S. treaty covers RRSPs under Article XVIII. The U.S.-UK treaty addresses UK pensions. These treaty rules affect **income tax**, not FBAR reporting. You still must file FinCEN Form 114 for these accounts.

Treaty-exempt pension accounts may qualify for exclusion from Form 8938. The FBAR obligation under 31 USC 5314 remains regardless of treaty status. Consult a tax professional for the income tax treatment specific to your treaty.

## Real-World Scenarios

### Scenario 1: Michael in London, UK SIPP and DB Pension

Michael is a U.S. citizen working in London. His UK SIPP is worth GBP 85,000 — about $107,000 in USD at the Treasury December 31 rate of 1.26 per 31 CFR 1010.350. He also participates in his employer's final salary (DB) pension plan and holds a UK current account with $3,000 USD equivalent.

Michael reports his SIPP and his UK current account on FBAR. The aggregate is about $110,000 — above $10,000. He does not report his DB pension. No personal account balance exists for that plan. His total foreign assets also exceed $200,000, so he files Form 8938 with his U.S. tax return.

### Scenario 2: Priya in Sydney, Australian Superannuation

Priya is a U.S. green card holder who lived in Australia before returning to the U.S. She retains her Australian super fund. The balance is AUD 145,000. At the Treasury December 31 rate of 0.63, that is about $91,350 USD.

Priya reports her super fund on FBAR each year she is a U.S. person. The balance exceeds $10,000. The account is still a foreign financial account even though she is back in the U.S. and the fund is accumulating without active management.

### Scenario 3: David in Toronto, RRSP and DB Pension

David is a dual U.S.-Canadian citizen. His RRSP is worth CAD 220,000 — about $163,000 in USD at the December 31 rate of 0.74 per 31 CFR 1010.350. He also participates in a Canadian government employee DB pension plan with no personal account balance.

David reports his RRSP on FBAR. His DB government pension is not reportable. There is no account balance attached to it. His aggregate reportable balance is $163,000 — well above $10,000 under 31 CFR 1010.306.

## What Penalties Apply for Failing to Report?

Failing to report a foreign pension on FBAR can result in significant penalties under [31 USC 5321](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). The penalty amount depends on whether the failure was willful or non-willful.

- **Non-willful violation**: Up to $16,117 per account per year under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B) (2025 inflation-adjusted figure).
- **Willful violation**: The greater of $100,000 or 50% of the account balance per year under 31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C).

Penalties can multiply across multiple unreported years. For a full breakdown, see [FBAR penalties: what happens if you don't file](/blog/fbar-penalties-what-happens-if-you-dont-file). If you missed prior years, you may qualify for the [Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures](/blog/fbar-streamlined-filing-compliance-procedures) or [Delinquent FBAR submission procedures](/blog/fbar-delinquent-filing-procedures). These programs can reduce or eliminate penalties for non-willful failures.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I need to report my UK SIPP on the FBAR?**

Yes. A UK SIPP is a defined contribution account held at a UK financial institution. You own the account balance. That meets the financial interest test under 31 CFR 1010.350(e). If your aggregate foreign account balances exceeded $10,000 at any point during the year per 31 CFR 1010.306, you must report your SIPP on FinCEN Form 114 — the FBAR.

**Is a defined benefit pension reportable on FBAR?**

Generally no. A defined benefit pension pays a fixed monthly income in retirement. No individual account balance belongs to you. Because there is no "maximum value" for FBAR purposes and no financial interest under 31 CFR 1010.350(e), most practitioners do not report DB pensions. However, a separate DC component — like an AVC sub-account — is separately reportable on the FBAR.

**Do I report my Canadian RRSP if I have a tax treaty deferral?**

Yes. The Canada-U.S. tax treaty defers income tax on RRSP growth under Article XVIII. But the treaty does not remove the FBAR filing obligation. FBAR is a Bank Secrecy Act requirement under 31 USC 5314. It is separate from income tax law. You must report the RRSP on FinCEN Form 114 regardless of any treaty election. See [FBAR Canada RRSP and TFSA reporting](/blog/fbar-canada-rrsp-tfsa-reporting) for more detail.

**Is Australian superannuation a foreign financial account for FBAR?**

Yes. An Australian superannuation fund in accumulation phase is a defined contribution retirement account at an Australian financial institution. You own the balance and have a financial interest under 31 CFR 1010.350(e). Report it on FinCEN Form 114 when your aggregate foreign balances exceed $10,000. See [FBAR Australia superannuation and bank accounts](/blog/fbar-australia-superannuation-bank-accounts) for full guidance.

**Are foreign pensions reportable on Form 8938 as well as FBAR?**

Yes, in most cases. Foreign pension accounts that are reportable on the FBAR — defined contribution plans with an owned balance — are also reportable on Form 8938 (FATCA) when they exceed the FATCA thresholds under 26 USC 6038D: $50,000 on the last day of the year or $75,000 at any time for single filers in the U.S. FBAR and Form 8938 are separate forms with separate thresholds.

**What exchange rate do I use to value my foreign pension on the FBAR?**

Use the Treasury year-end exchange rate for December 31 of the reporting year. Find these rates at the [Treasury Fiscal Service exchange rate page](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/reports-statements/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/current.html). Even if your account peaked mid-year, convert using the December 31 rate. See [FBAR exchange rates and Treasury rates](/blog/fbar-exchange-rates-treasury-department) for step-by-step instructions.

**What if my pension balance is below $10,000?**

You must aggregate all your foreign financial accounts under 31 CFR 1010.306. If your UK SIPP holds $6,000 in USD and your UK bank account holds $5,000, your aggregate is $11,000 — above $10,000. Both accounts must be reported on FinCEN Form 114 even though each individual account falls below the $10,000 threshold.

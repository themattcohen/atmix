# FBAR First-Time Filer Guide: How to Report Your Foreign Accounts

If you are a US person with foreign financial accounts and you have never filed an FBAR, you are not alone. Thousands of Americans — expats, immigrants, dual citizens, and people who inherited accounts abroad — discover the FBAR requirement every year. The FBAR, formally the Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts, is filed with FinCEN under the Bank Secrecy Act. The good news: filing is straightforward once you understand what is required.

This guide walks you through the entire process as an FBAR first time filer. Specifically, you will go from determining whether you need to file all the way to submitting your report through FinCEN's BSA E-Filing system.

## Do You Need to File an FBAR as a First-Time Filer?

You must file an FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) if you meet **all three conditions**. First, you are a United States person. Second, you held a financial interest in or signature authority over at least one foreign financial account during the year. Third, the aggregate maximum value of all your foreign accounts exceeded $10,000 at any point during the year. All three must apply at the same time. If even one does not apply, you do not have a filing obligation.

Here are the three conditions in detail:

1. **You are a "United States person"** — a US citizen, green card holder, or resident alien who meets the [substantial presence test](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/substantial-presence-test) under [26 USC 7701(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/7701). This also includes US-formed entities: corporations, partnerships, trusts, and LLCs. The definition comes from [31 CFR 1010.350(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350).

2. **You had a financial interest in, or signature authority over, at least one foreign financial account** during the calendar year.

3. **The aggregate maximum value of ALL your foreign accounts exceeded $10,000** at any point during the year, per [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306).

That $10,000 threshold is **aggregate** — meaning you add up the highest values of all your foreign accounts. If you had two accounts that peaked at $6,000 and $5,500 at different times during the year, your aggregate maximum is $11,500, and you must report both accounts.

### Who Counts as a "United States Person"?

The FBAR definition is broader than many people expect:

- **US citizens** — regardless of where you live. A US citizen in London with a Barclays checking account must file.
- **Green card holders** — your FBAR obligation begins when your green card is issued. This applies even if you still maintain accounts in your home country.
- **Resident aliens** — you qualify if you meet the substantial presence test. That means at least 31 US days this year and 183 days over three years using the weighted formula.
- **H-1B, L-1, and other visa holders** — once you meet the substantial presence test, you have FBAR obligations. Many visa holders don't realize this.

One common misconception: tax treaty elections do **not** affect your FBAR status. Even if you elected nonresident alien status under a tax treaty, you remain a US person for FBAR filing under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). The treaty reduces your income tax. It does not remove your FinCEN reporting duty.

## What Accounts Must You Report on the FBAR?

The FBAR covers many types of foreign financial accounts — not just bank accounts. You must report any account at a foreign institution where you have a financial interest or signing rights. This includes brokerage accounts, mutual funds, insurance policies with cash value, and foreign pension plans. Accounts where you have only signing rights — but no ownership — are reportable too. Many first-time filers are surprised to learn their foreign pension counts.

### Reportable Foreign Financial Accounts

| Account Type | Reportable? |
|---|---|
| Foreign bank accounts (checking, savings, CDs) | **Yes** |
| Foreign brokerage and securities accounts | **Yes** |
| Foreign mutual fund accounts | **Yes** |
| Foreign insurance policies with cash surrender value | **Yes** |
| Foreign pension/retirement accounts (Canadian RRSP, Australian super, UK SIPP) | **Yes** |
| Accounts where you have signature authority but no ownership | **Yes** |
| Foreign annuity policies with cash value | **Yes** |

Foreign pension plans are a frequent surprise for immigrants. For example, Canadian RRSPs, Mexican AFOREs, and UK workplace pensions are all reportable on the FBAR. In contrast, the exemption under [31 CFR 1010.350(g)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350) applies only to US-qualified plans (IRAs, 401(k)s, 403(b)s).

### What You Don't Need to Report

- **US domestic accounts** — accounts at US banks, even if denominated in foreign currency
- **Foreign real estate** held directly (not through a financial account)
- **US retirement accounts** — IRAs, 401(k)s, 403(b)s under [IRC §§ 408, 401(a), 403(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/408)
- **Cryptocurrency on foreign exchanges** — FinCEN issued [Notice 2020-2](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/shared/Notice-Virtual%20Currency%20Reporting%20on%20the%20FBAR%20123020.pdf) proposing to require this, but the rulemaking is not finalized. Crypto is **not currently required** on the FBAR. (See our article on [FBAR and cryptocurrency](/blog/fbar-cryptocurrency-foreign-exchanges) for the full analysis.)

## How to Calculate Your Maximum Account Value

The FBAR requires the **maximum value** of each account during the calendar year — not the December 31 balance. This distinction catches many first-time filers off guard. To calculate correctly: find the single highest balance in each account during the year. Then convert it to US dollars using the Treasury's December 31 exchange rate. Finally, add up all the converted peak values. That total determines whether you exceed the $10,000 threshold.

### Step 1: Find the Peak Balance for Each Account

Review your monthly or quarterly account statements for the entire year. Identify the single highest balance reached at any point. Per [FinCEN guidance](https://www.fincen.gov/reporting-maximum-account-value), you can rely on periodic account statements to determine this value.

**Example**: Your UK savings account showed balances of £8,200 in March, £12,500 in July (after a bonus deposit), and £4,100 in December. The maximum value is £12,500, not £4,100.

### Step 2: Convert to US Dollars

Use the **Treasury Department's year-end exchange rate** for December 31 of the reporting year. Find the rates at [fiscal.treasury.gov](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/reports-statements/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/current.html).

For the 2025 tax year (filed in 2026), use December 31, 2025 rates.

If the Treasury doesn't publish a rate for your currency, use another verifiable exchange rate and document the source. For more detail, see our guide on [calculating maximum account value for FBAR](/blog/how-to-calculate-maximum-account-value-fbar).

### Step 3: Add Up All Accounts

Sum the converted maximum values of all your foreign accounts. If the total exceeds $10,000, you must file an FBAR and report **every** foreign account — even those with small balances.

## What Is the FBAR Filing Deadline?

The FBAR filing deadline for the 2025 calendar year is **April 15, 2026**, per [31 CFR 1010.306(c)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306). If you miss the April deadline, you receive an automatic extension to **October 15, 2026**. No form or request is required — the extension is automatic by law. Therefore, unlike IRS income tax returns, you do not need to file a separate extension form. For more details, see our article on [FBAR filing deadlines](/blog/fbar-filing-deadline-2026).

## How to File Your FBAR: Step-by-Step

To file your FBAR, go to FinCEN's [BSA E-Filing system](https://bsaefiling.fincen.gov/) and submit FinCEN Form 114 online. The FBAR is not filed with your tax return and does not go to the IRS. Instead, it goes directly to FinCEN under the Bank Secrecy Act. Most first-time filers complete the process in under 30 minutes after gathering their account information.

### What You'll Need Before You Start

Gather this information for **each** foreign account:

- Financial institution name and address (including country)
- Account number
- Account type (bank, securities, or other)
- Maximum value during the year (converted to USD)
- Your SSN or ITIN

### Filing Steps

**1. Go to the BSA E-Filing system** at [bsaefiling.fincen.gov](https://bsaefiling.fincen.gov/).

**2. Select "File FBAR"** — individuals can use the no-registration option. You do not need to create an account.

**3. Enter your personal information** — name, SSN/ITIN, address, date of birth.

**4. Add each foreign account** — for every account, enter the institution name, address, account number, type, and maximum value in USD.

**5. Click "Validate"** — the system checks for missing required fields and flags errors.

**6. Sign electronically and submit** — no wet signature needed.

**7. Save your confirmation** — within two business days, FinCEN sends an acknowledgment with a 14-digit BSA Identifier (beginning with "310000"). Keep this permanently. Retain all FBAR records for 5 years per [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350).

In general, most people with a few accounts complete the process in 15 to 30 minutes once they have gathered their account information.

**Filing with a spouse?** If both spouses have foreign accounts, you can file one joint FBAR. However, all accounts must be jointly owned to qualify. If either spouse holds an account alone, that spouse must file a separate FBAR. The non-signing spouse must complete [FinCEN Form 114a](https://www.fincen.gov/how-do-i-file-fbar) and keep it for at least 5 years. ## What Happens If You Don't File an FBAR?

FBAR penalties are among the steepest in the US tax code. Non-willful violations carry a maximum penalty of $16,536 per report. Willful violations can reach $165,353 or 50 percent of the account balance, whichever is greater. Moreover, the government can pursue criminal charges for deliberate concealment. However, first-time filers who were unaware and come forward voluntarily face far lower risk. Here is what you face under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321):

### Non-Willful Penalties

If you didn't know about the FBAR requirement or made an honest mistake, the maximum penalty is **$16,536 per report** (2025 inflation-adjusted amount per [31 CFR 1010.821](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.821)).

The Supreme Court clarified this in its 2023 [*Bittner v. United States*](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/22pdf/21-1195_h3ci.pdf) decision. Specifically, the Court ruled that the IRS applies non-willful penalties **per FBAR report**, not per account. Therefore, if you failed to file one year's FBAR covering 10 accounts, the maximum non-willful penalty is $16,536 — not $165,360.

### Willful Penalties

If you knew about the requirement and deliberately didn't file, the penalty jumps to the **greater of $165,353 or 50% of the account balance** at the time of the violation, per [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321).

### Criminal Penalties

Willful violations can also result in criminal prosecution under [31 USC 5322](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5322): fines up to $250,000 and up to 5 years imprisonment. Criminal prosecution is rare and typically reserved for cases involving tax evasion or deliberate concealment — not first-time filers who genuinely didn't know.

### Reasonable Cause Exception

The IRS can waive non-willful penalties if you show reasonable cause for not filing, per [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)(ii)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). Relevant factors include reliance on professional advice, the complexity of the law, and your awareness of reporting obligations. For more on penalties, see our article on [FBAR penalties](/blog/fbar-penalties-what-happens-if-you-dont-file).

## What If You Should Have Filed in Prior Years?

If you have had foreign accounts for years but never filed an FBAR, you have options. The IRS offers two main programs for late filers. The first is the Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures. The second is the Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures. The right choice depends on whether you reported all income from those accounts on your US tax returns. Both programs can reduce or eliminate penalties for first-time filers who were not aware of the requirement.

Here is how each program works:

### Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures

The [IRS Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/delinquent-fbar-submission-procedures) allow you to file late FBARs without penalties if:

- You properly reported all income from the foreign accounts on your US tax returns
- You paid all tax due on that income
- The IRS has not previously contacted you about the unreported accounts

File the delinquent FBARs through BSA E-Filing and include a statement explaining why you're filing late.

### Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures

If you owe additional tax, the [IRS Streamlined Procedures](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/streamlined-filing-compliance-procedures) offer reduced penalties for non-willful taxpayers. The offshore streamlined program charges a 5% penalty on the highest aggregate account balance over the compliance period. That is far less than the standard non-willful penalty of $16,536 per year.

## Common FBAR First-Time Filer Mistakes to Avoid

Most errors made by FBAR first time filers fall into five categories: using the wrong balance, overlooking pension accounts, misunderstanding the FEIE, failing to aggregate accounts, and confusing the FBAR with Form 8938. Each mistake can result in penalties. Knowing these pitfalls in advance lets you file correctly the first time.

**Using the year-end balance instead of maximum value.** If your account peaked at $15,000 in July but dropped to $3,000 by December, your reportable maximum is $15,000 — not the year-end figure.

**Forgetting foreign pension accounts.** Canadian RRSPs, UK workplace pensions, and Australian superannuation are all reportable. The "retirement plan" exemption covers only US-qualified plans.

**Thinking the FEIE eliminates FBAR obligations.** The Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (Form 2555) reduces your income tax. However, it has zero effect on your FBAR filing requirement. The FBAR is a separate FinCEN obligation, not an IRS tax form.

**Not aggregating across accounts.** Two accounts that each peaked below $10,000 can still trigger the filing requirement. If their combined peak values exceed $10,000, you must file. Each account's peak is measured at different times. Then all peaks are summed together.

**Confusing FBAR with FATCA (Form 8938).** The FBAR goes to FinCEN with a $10,000 threshold. Form 8938 goes to the IRS with higher thresholds. For domestic filers, Form 8938 applies at $50,000 on the last day of the year or $75,000 at any time. Expats filing jointly have thresholds of $200,000 and $300,000. You may need to file both. See our article on [FBAR vs. FATCA differences](/blog/fbar-vs-fatca-form-8938-differences) and the [IRS comparison chart](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/comparison-of-form-8938-and-fbar-requirements). See also our guide on [FBAR for green card holders](/blog/fbar-green-card-holders) if you are a permanent resident.

## Frequently Asked Questions

Here are the most common questions from FBAR first time filers. For questions about your specific situation, consult a qualified tax professional or contact the IRS FBAR hotline at 1-866-270-0733.

**How long does it take to file an FBAR for the first time?**
Most first-time filers with a small number of accounts complete the BSA E-Filing process in 15 to 30 minutes. Gathering your account statements and the Treasury exchange rates beforehand speeds up the process.

**Can I file an FBAR on paper?**
No. FinCEN requires all FBARs to be filed online through the BSA E-Filing system. Paper forms are not accepted.

**Do I need to report a foreign account I closed during the year?**
Yes. If the account was open at any point during the calendar year and your aggregate foreign accounts exceeded $10,000, you must report the closed account. Use the maximum value before closure.

**What if I have signature authority over a business account abroad?**
You must report any foreign financial account over which you have signature authority, even if you do not own it. Corporate officers and employees with signing rights on foreign business accounts must file.

**Do joint accounts require both spouses to file?**
If all accounts are jointly owned, spouses can file a single joint FBAR. If either spouse holds an account alone, that spouse must file a separate FBAR. See our article on [FBAR joint account reporting rules](/blog/fbar-joint-account-reporting-rules) for details.

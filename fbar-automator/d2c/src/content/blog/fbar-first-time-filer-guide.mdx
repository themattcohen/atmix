# FBAR First-Time Filer Guide: How to Report Your Foreign Accounts

Thousands of Americans discover the FBAR requirement every year. Expats, immigrants, dual citizens, and people who inherited accounts abroad all face this filing obligation. If you have never filed a Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts under the Bank Secrecy Act, this guide covers what you need to know.

You will learn who must file, which types of accounts to report, how to calculate maximum values, and how to submit your report through the BSA E-Filing system. The process is straightforward once you understand the rules.

## Who Must File an FBAR

You must file an FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) if you meet **all three conditions**:

1. **You are a "United States person."** This includes US citizens, green card holders, and resident aliens who meet the [substantial presence test](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/substantial-presence-test) under [26 USC 7701(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/7701). US-formed entities also qualify: corporations, partnerships, trusts, and any limited liability company formed under US law. The definition comes from [31 CFR 1010.350(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350).

2. **You had a financial interest in, or signature authority over, at least one foreign financial account** during the calendar year.

3. **The aggregate value of your foreign financial accounts exceeded $10,000** at any point during the year, per [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306).

The $10,000 threshold is **aggregate**. You total the highest values of all your foreign accounts. Two accounts that peaked at $6,000 and $5,500 at different times produce an aggregate maximum of $11,500. You must report both accounts.

### United States Person Definition

The FBAR definition of "United States person" is broader than many people expect:

- **US citizens** — regardless of where you live. A US citizen in London with a Barclays checking account must file.
- **Green card holders** — your FBAR obligation begins when your green card is issued. This applies even if you maintain accounts in your home country.
- **Resident aliens** — you qualify if you pass the substantial presence test. The test requires at least 31 days of US presence this year and 183 days over three years using the weighted formula.
- **H-1B, L-1, and other visa holders** — once you meet the substantial presence test, you have FBAR obligations. Many visa holders are unaware of this rule.

One common misconception: tax treaty elections do **not** affect your FBAR status. Even if you elected nonresident alien status under a tax treaty for income tax purposes, you remain a US person for FBAR filing under [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350).

## Types of Accounts You Must Report

### Reportable Foreign Financial Accounts

The FBAR covers many types of accounts held outside the United States:

| Account Type | Reportable? |
|---|---|
| Foreign bank accounts (checking, savings, CDs) | **Yes** |
| Foreign brokerage and securities accounts | **Yes** |
| Foreign mutual fund accounts | **Yes** |
| Foreign insurance policies with cash surrender value | **Yes** |
| Foreign pension or retirement accounts (Canadian RRSP, Australian retirement fund, UK SIPP) | **Yes** |
| Accounts where you have signature authority but no ownership | **Yes** |
| Foreign annuity policies with cash value | **Yes** |

Foreign pension plans are commonly overlooked by immigrants. Canadian RRSPs, Mexican AFOREs, UK workplace pensions, and similar accounts are all reportable on the FBAR. The exemption for retirement plans under [31 CFR 1010.350(g)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350) applies only to US-qualified plans. This includes individual retirement account (IRA), 401(k), and 403(b) plans.

### Accounts You Do Not Need to Report

- **US domestic accounts** — accounts at US banks, even if held in foreign currency
- **Foreign real estate** held directly (not through a financial account)
- **US retirement accounts** — IRAs, 401(k)s, 403(b)s under [IRC §§ 408, 401(a), 403(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/408)
- **Crypto on foreign exchanges** — The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) issued [Notice 2020-2](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/shared/Notice-Virtual%20Currency%20Reporting%20on%20the%20FBAR%20123020.pdf) proposing to require this reporting. However, the rulemaking is not finalized. Crypto is **not currently required** on the FBAR. See our article on [FBAR and cryptocurrency](/blog/fbar-cryptocurrency-foreign-exchanges) for the full analysis.

## How to Calculate Your Maximum Account Value

The FBAR requires the **maximum value** of each account during the calendar year — not the December 31 balance. This distinction catches many first-time filers off guard.

### Step 1: Find the Peak Balance for Each Account

Review your monthly or quarterly account statements for the entire year. Identify the single highest balance at any point. Per [FinCEN guidance](https://www.fincen.gov/reporting-maximum-account-value), you can rely on periodic account statements to determine this value.

**Example:** Your UK savings account showed these balances:

- £8,200 in March
- £12,500 in July (after a bonus deposit)
- £4,100 in December

The maximum value is £12,500, not £4,100.

### Step 2: Convert to US Dollars

Use the **Treasury Department's year-end exchange rate** for December 31 of the reporting year. You can find the rates at [fiscal.treasury.gov](https://fiscal.treasury.gov/reports-statements/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/current.html).

For the 2025 tax year (filed in 2026), use December 31, 2025 rates.

If the Treasury does not publish a rate for your currency, use another confirmed exchange rate and document the source. For more detail, see our guide on [calculating maximum account value for FBAR](/blog/how-to-calculate-maximum-account-value-fbar).

### Step 3: Total All Accounts

Sum the converted maximum values of all your foreign accounts. If the total value of your foreign financial accounts exceeds $10,000, you must file an FBAR. You must report **every** foreign account — even those with small balances.

## Filing Deadline and Automatic Extension

Your FBAR for the 2025 calendar year is due **April 15, 2026**, per [31 CFR 1010.306(c)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306). If you miss the April deadline, you receive an automatic extension to **October 15, 2026**. No form or request is required for this extension.

Congress created this automatic extension through the Surface Transportation and Veterans Health Care Choice Improvement Act of 2015. Unlike IRS tax returns, you do not need to file an extension form. The FBAR must be timely filed by October 15 at the latest.

For more on filing deadlines per IRS and FinCEN guidance, see our article on [FBAR filing deadlines](/blog/fbar-filing-deadline-2026).

## How to File Your FBAR: Step-by-Step

You file the FBAR online through the [BSA E-Filing system](https://bsaefiling.fincen.gov/) operated by the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN). You do not file the FBAR with your tax return. The report goes to FinCEN under the Bank Secrecy Act, not to the IRS.

### What You Need Before You Start

Gather this information for **each** foreign account:

- Financial institution name and address (including country)
- Account number
- Account type (bank, securities, or other)
- Maximum value during the year (converted to USD)
- Your SSN or ITIN

### Filing Steps

1. **Go to the BSA E-Filing system** at [bsaefiling.fincen.gov](https://bsaefiling.fincen.gov/).
2. **Select "File FBAR."** You can use the no-registration option. You do not need to create an account.
3. **Enter your personal information:** name, SSN/ITIN, address, and date of birth.
4. **Add each foreign account.** Enter the institution name, address, account number, type, and maximum value in USD.
5. **Click "Validate."** The system checks for missing fields and flags errors.
6. **Sign online and submit.** No wet signature is needed.
7. **Save your confirmation.** Within two business days, you will receive an acknowledgment with a 14-digit BSA Identifier (beginning with "310000"). Store this record permanently. You must retain FBAR records for 5 years per [31 CFR 1010.350](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350).

Most people with a small number of accounts complete the filing in 15 to 30 minutes after gathering their account information.

### Filing with a Spouse

If both spouses have foreign accounts, you can file jointly on a single FBAR. To qualify, all accounts must be jointly owned. If either spouse holds an account in their name alone, that spouse must file a separate FBAR.

The spouse who does not sign the FBAR must complete [FinCEN Form 114a](https://www.fincen.gov/how-do-i-file-fbar) (Record of Authorization to File FBARs Online). Keep this form for at least 5 years. FinCEN may request it during an examination.

## FBAR Penalties for Non-Filing

FBAR penalties are among the steepest in US tax law. Here is what you face under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321):

### Non-Willful Penalties

If you did not know about the FBAR requirement or made an honest mistake, the IRS can assess a maximum penalty of **$16,536 per report**. This is the 2025 inflation-adjusted amount per [31 CFR 1010.821](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.821).

The Supreme Court clarified this in its 2023 [*Bittner v. United States*](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/22pdf/21-1195_h3ci.pdf) decision. The Court ruled that the IRS assesses non-willful penalties **per FBAR report**, not per account. If you failed to file one year covering 10 accounts, the maximum non-willful penalty is $16,536 — not $165,360.

### Willful Penalties

If you knew about the filing requirement and chose not to file, the penalty increases to the **greater of $165,353 or 50% of the account balance** at the time of the violation, per [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321).

### Criminal Penalties

The government can also pursue criminal prosecution for willful violations under [31 USC 5322](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5322). Penalties include fines up to $250,000 and up to 5 years in prison. Criminal cases are rare. Prosecutors typically reserve them for tax evasion or deliberate concealment, not for first-time filers who were unaware of the rule.

### Reasonable Cause Exception

The IRS can waive non-willful penalties if you had reasonable cause for not filing, per [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)(ii)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). Factors include reliance on professional advice, the complexity of the law, and your awareness of reporting obligations. For more on penalties, see our article on [FBAR penalties](/blog/fbar-penalties-what-happens-if-you-dont-file).

## Late Filing Options for Prior Years

If you have had foreign accounts for years but never filed an FBAR, you have two main options:

### Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures

The [IRS Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/delinquent-fbar-submission-procedures) allow you to file late FBARs without penalties if:

- You reported all income from the foreign accounts on your US tax returns
- You paid all tax due on that income
- The IRS has not contacted you about the unreported accounts

File the delinquent FBARs through BSA E-Filing. Include a statement explaining why you are filing late.

### Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures

If you owe additional tax, the [IRS Streamlined Procedures](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/streamlined-filing-compliance-procedures) offer reduced penalties for non-willful taxpayers. The offshore streamlined program requires a 5% penalty on the highest aggregate account balance over the compliance period. This is far less than the standard non-willful penalty of $16,536 per year.

## Common First-Time Filer Mistakes

**Using the year-end balance instead of maximum value.** If your account peaked at $15,000 in July but dropped to $3,000 by December, your reportable maximum is $15,000.

**Overlooking foreign pension accounts.** Canadian RRSPs, UK workplace pensions, and Australian retirement funds are all reportable. The "retirement plan" exemption covers only US-qualified plans like IRAs and 401(k)s.

**Assuming the FEIE eliminates FBAR obligations.** The Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (Form 2555) reduces your income tax. The FBAR is a separate FinCEN obligation under the Bank Secrecy Act. The exclusion has zero effect on your FBAR filing requirement.

**Failing to aggregate across accounts.** Two accounts that each peaked below $10,000 can still trigger the filing requirement. If their combined peak values exceed $10,000, you must file. Each account's maximum value is measured separately at different times during the year. You then sum all the maximums.

**Confusing FBAR with FATCA (Form 8938).** The FBAR goes to FinCEN with a $10,000 threshold. Form 8938 goes to the IRS with higher thresholds. For domestic filers, Form 8938 applies at $50,000 on the last day of the year or $75,000 at any time. Expats filing jointly have thresholds of $200,000 and $300,000. You may need to file both reports. They serve different purposes and go to different agencies. See the [IRS comparison chart](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/comparison-of-form-8938-and-fbar-requirements) for details.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How long does it take to file an FBAR?**
Most filers with a few accounts complete the BSA E-Filing form in 15 to 30 minutes. Gather your account statements and exchange rates before you start.

**Can I file an FBAR on paper?**
No. FinCEN requires all FBARs to be filed online through the BSA E-Filing system. Paper forms are not accepted.

**Do I need to report a foreign account I closed during the year?**
Yes. If the account was open at any point during the calendar year and your aggregate foreign financial accounts exceeded the $10,000 threshold, you must report the closed account. Use its maximum value before closure.

**What if I have signature authority over a business account abroad?**
You must report any foreign financial account over which you have signature authority. This applies even if you do not own the account. Corporate officers and employees with signing rights on foreign business accounts must file.

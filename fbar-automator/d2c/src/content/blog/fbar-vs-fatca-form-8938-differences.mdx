# FBAR vs FATCA Form 8938: Key Differences Every Filer Should Know

Many US taxpayers with foreign accounts confuse the FBAR with FATCA. Both require reporting of foreign financial accounts, and both carry steep penalties for non-compliance. However, they differ in almost every detail: who enforces them, where you file, what thresholds trigger the obligation, and what penalties apply.

The FBAR — formally the Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts (FinCEN Form 114) — falls under the [Bank Secrecy Act](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5314) and goes to FinCEN. FATCA reporting through [Form 8938](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-8938) falls under the [Internal Revenue Code](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D) and goes to the IRS. Filing one does not satisfy the other. Therefore, most people with foreign accounts above certain thresholds must file both.

This article breaks down each reporting obligation. You will learn the thresholds, deadlines, penalties, covered accounts, and the most common mistakes filers make.

## What Is the FBAR vs FATCA Form 8938 Difference?

The FBAR (Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts) and FATCA Form 8938 are two separate foreign account reporting obligations. You file the FBAR with FinCEN under the Bank Secrecy Act; it has a flat $10,000 threshold. You file Form 8938 with the IRS under the Internal Revenue Code; it has higher, status-based thresholds starting at $50,000. You must file both independently — one does not replace the other.

## How Do FBAR vs FATCA Form 8938 Requirements Compare Side by Side?

The FBAR and FATCA Form 8938 differ in governing agency, filing location, threshold, and penalty structure. The FBAR goes to FinCEN under the Bank Secrecy Act with a flat $10,000 threshold. Form 8938 goes to the IRS under the Internal Revenue Code with thresholds that vary by status and residence. Use the table below to compare the key requirements side by side.

## What Does the FBAR Cover?

### Reportable Account Types

The FBAR (Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts) covers accounts held at foreign financial institutions:

- Bank accounts (checking, savings, time deposits)
- Securities and brokerage accounts
- Mutual fund accounts
- Insurance policies with cash surrender value
- Foreign pension and retirement accounts
- Accounts where you have signature authority but no ownership interest

The FBAR does not cover foreign stocks held in a US brokerage account. A share of Nestle stock in your Fidelity account is not reportable on the FBAR because Fidelity is a US institution.

### Who Files the FBAR?

A "United States person" under [31 CFR 1010.350(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350) includes:

- US citizens (regardless of residence)
- Lawful permanent residents (green card holders)
- Resident aliens who meet the substantial presence test
- US-formed entities (corporations, partnerships, LLCs, trusts)

If you hold a green card and keep accounts in your home country, you have an FBAR obligation. Our article on [FBAR filing for green card holders](/blog/fbar-green-card-holders) covers that topic in detail.

### FBAR Filing Threshold

The FBAR has a single, flat threshold: **$10,000 in aggregate** at any point during the calendar year. Marital status, filing status, and country of residence do not affect this number. Whether you live in Ohio or Oslo, the threshold stays at $10,000.

This low threshold catches many people off guard. Foreign pension accounts, inheritance proceeds sitting in a parent's bank, and even a foreign account you rarely use all count toward the aggregate. See our guide on [how to calculate maximum account value for the FBAR](/blog/how-to-calculate-maximum-account-value-fbar).

### FBAR Deadline

The FBAR for the 2025 calendar year is due **April 15, 2026**. If you miss that date, you receive an automatic extension to **October 15, 2026** under [31 CFR 1010.306(c)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306). No form or request is needed. Read more in our article on the [2026 FBAR filing deadline](/blog/fbar-filing-deadline-2026).

### FBAR Penalties

The IRS enforces FBAR penalties on behalf of FinCEN. The penalty structure under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321) breaks into tiers:

- **Non-willful violation**: Up to **$12,909 per violation** (2026 inflation-adjusted amount). Following the Supreme Court's ruling in *United States v. Bittner* (2023), non-willful penalties apply per report, not per account.
- **Willful violation**: The greater of **$100,000** or **50% of the account balance** at the time of the violation, per account, per year, under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321).
- **Criminal violation**: Up to **$250,000** in fines and **5 years** imprisonment under [31 USC 5322](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5322).

For a deeper analysis, see our article on [FBAR penalties and what happens if you do not file](/blog/fbar-penalties-what-happens-if-you-dont-file).

## What Does FATCA Form 8938 Cover?

### FATCA Scope and Assets

FATCA — the Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act — was enacted in 2010. Under [26 USC 6038D](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D), US taxpayers must report specified foreign financial assets on Form 8938. Additionally, Form 8938 requires reporting of:

- Stock or securities issued by a non-US person
- Financial instruments or contracts with non-US issuers or counterparties
- Interests in foreign entities (partnerships, corporations, trusts, estates)
- Any foreign financial account maintained by a foreign financial institution

### Who Files Form 8938?

Only US persons who file a federal income tax return must file Form 8938 under [IRC § 6038D](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D). Specifically, if your income falls below the filing threshold for a tax return and you do not file one, you have no Form 8938 obligation for that year. The [IRS instructions for Form 8938](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8938) list the following filers:

- US citizens
- Resident aliens
- Nonresident aliens who elect to file a joint return with a US spouse
- Certain nonresident aliens who are bona fide residents of American Samoa or Puerto Rico

### FATCA Filing Thresholds

Unlike the FBAR's flat $10,000, FATCA thresholds vary based on your filing status and where you live. The thresholds come from [26 USC 6038D(a)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D) and [IRS guidance](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/corporations/summary-of-fatca-reporting-for-us-taxpayers):

**Living in the United States:**

- **Single or married filing separately**: File if total value exceeds $50,000 on the last day of the tax year, or if assets exceeded $75,000 at any time during the year.
- **Married filing jointly**: File if total value exceeds $100,000 on the last day of the tax year, or if assets exceeded $150,000 at any time during the year.

**Living outside the United States:**

- **Single or married filing separately**: File if total value exceeds $200,000 on the last day of the tax year, or if assets exceeded $300,000 at any time during the year.
- **Married filing jointly**: File if total value exceeds $400,000 on the last day of the tax year, or if assets exceeded $600,000 at any time during the year.

### FATCA Deadline

Form 8938 attaches to your federal income tax return. The deadline matches your 1040 deadline: **April 15, 2026** for most filers per [IRC § 6038D](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D). If you file for an extension using IRS Form 4868, Form 8938 extends with it to **October 15, 2026**.

### FATCA Penalties

The penalty structure for Form 8938 under [26 USC 6038D(d)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D) works differently from FBAR penalties:

- **Failure to file**: $10,000 penalty per violation.
- **Continued failure after IRS notice**: An additional $10,000 for each 30-day period during which the failure continues, up to $50,000 maximum.
- **Total maximum**: **$60,000** for a single year of non-compliance.
- **Underpayment penalty**: 40% on the understatement attributable to undisclosed foreign assets under [26 USC 6662(j)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6662).

### Statute of Limitations

Another difference involves the statute of limitations. The IRS has **three years** from the date you file your return to assess additional tax. However, if you omit more than $5,000 of income from specified foreign assets, the statute extends to **six years** under [26 USC 6501(e)(1)(A)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6501). For the FBAR, the statute of limitations is **six years** from the due date of the report per [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306).

## When Do You Need to File Both?

Most US taxpayers with foreign accounts above certain levels must file both the FBAR and Form 8938. Filing one does not satisfy the obligation to file the other. Consequently, the [IRS comparison chart](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/comparison-of-form-8938-and-fbar-requirements) treats these as independent requirements.

Here are common scenarios:

### Scenario 1: Expat With $250,000 in Foreign Accounts

Maria is a US citizen living in Germany with bank and brokerage accounts totaling $250,000.

- **FBAR**: Required. Her accounts exceed the $10,000 aggregate threshold.
- **Form 8938**: Required. She is single and living abroad. Her assets exceed the $200,000 year-end threshold.

Maria files the FBAR through BSA E-Filing and attaches Form 8938 to her 1040.

### Scenario 2: Green Card Holder With $30,000 Abroad

Raj is a green card holder living in New Jersey with $30,000 in a savings account at a bank in India.

- **FBAR**: Required. His account exceeds $10,000.
- **Form 8938**: Not required. Raj resides in the US and files as single. His $30,000 falls below the $50,000 year-end threshold.

### Scenario 3: Married Couple With $120,000 Abroad

Tom and Lisa file jointly and reside in Chicago with $120,000 in a UK bank account.

- **FBAR**: Required. They may file jointly under [31 CFR 1010.350(g)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350) or separately.
- **Form 8938**: Required. Their $120,000 exceeds the $100,000 year-end threshold for married filing jointly.

## What Are the Most Common FBAR vs FATCA Mistakes?

### Mistake 1: Thinking One Filing Covers Both

The FBAR goes to FinCEN. Form 8938 goes to the IRS with your tax return. Filing your FBAR does not satisfy the Form 8938 obligation. In other words, each report must go through its own channel to the appropriate agency.

### Mistake 2: Using the Wrong Threshold

The FBAR's $10,000 threshold catches many filers who assume the higher FATCA thresholds apply to all foreign account reporting. A single person in the US with $60,000 in foreign accounts still owes an FBAR — the $75,000 FATCA threshold applies only to Form 8938.

### Mistake 3: Forgetting That FBAR Thresholds Do Not Change

FATCA thresholds vary based on filing status and residence. The FBAR threshold does not vary. A married couple living in London still faces the same $10,000 FBAR threshold as a single person in Kansas.

### Mistake 4: Reporting Only Bank Accounts

Both filings cover more than bank accounts. The FBAR includes securities accounts, mutual funds, and foreign pension plans. Furthermore, Form 8938 covers foreign stock holdings, partnership interests, and contracts with foreign counterparties.

### Mistake 5: Not Converting to US Dollars

Both filings require values in US dollars. For the FBAR, use the [Treasury Department's year-end exchange rate](https://fiscaldata.treasury.gov/datasets/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/treasury-reporting-rates-of-exchange) published for December 31.

### Mistake 6: Confusing Cryptocurrency Rules

FinCEN has signaled that [virtual currency accounts on foreign exchanges are reportable on the FBAR](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/shared/Notice-Virtual%20Currency%20Reporting%20on%20the%20FBAR%20123020.pdf). Our article on [FBAR and cryptocurrency on foreign exchanges](/blog/fbar-cryptocurrency-foreign-exchanges) covers this topic.

## How Do You File Each Report?

Each report goes to a different agency, so you must complete both separately. The FBAR uses the BSA E-Filing system; Form 8938 attaches to your federal income tax return. Both are due April 15, with automatic or requested extensions to October 15.

1. **Determine whether you meet each threshold.** Use the $10,000 aggregate rule for the FBAR and the status-based FATCA thresholds from the comparison table above.
2. **Gather your account information.** Collect foreign institution names, account numbers, and maximum values for the calendar year.
3. **File the FBAR online.** Submit the Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts through the [BSA E-Filing system](https://bsaefiling.fincen.treas.gov/) operated by FinCEN. Paper submission is not available.
4. **Attach Form 8938 to your tax return.** Form 8938 transmits with your 1040 when you e-file. If you paper file, include it in the mailing.
5. **Convert all values to US dollars.** Use the [Treasury exchange rate](https://fiscaldata.treasury.gov/datasets/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/treasury-reporting-rates-of-exchange) for December 31 for both filings.
6. **Meet the deadlines.** Both the FBAR and Form 8938 are due April 15, with extensions available to October 15.

## How Do FBAR vs FATCA Form 8938 Penalties Compare?

The FBAR and FATCA Form 8938 carry separate penalties that can apply simultaneously to the same unreported account. FBAR willful penalties can reach 50% of the account balance per year under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321), while FATCA penalties are capped at $60,000 per year under [26 USC 6038D(d)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D). These penalties do not offset each other — they accumulate.

| Penalty Type | FBAR ([31 USC 5321](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321)) | Form 8938 ([26 USC 6038D(d)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D)) |
|---|---|---|
| **Non-willful** | Up to $12,909 per violation | $10,000 per violation |
| **Continued failure** | N/A | Additional $10,000 per 30-day period (max $50,000) |
| **Willful** | Greater of $100,000 or 50% of balance | Same $10,000 + $50,000 maximum |
| **Criminal** | Up to $250,000 and/or 5 years ([31 USC 5322](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5322)) | General tax fraud penalties |
| **Accuracy penalty** | N/A | 40% on underpayment from undisclosed assets ([26 USC 6662(j)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6662)) |

## What Options Exist for Voluntary Disclosure and Catch-Up?

Voluntary disclosure programs let taxpayers who missed FBAR or Form 8938 filings come forward and reduce their penalty exposure. The IRS offers three main catch-up paths. These programs require you to act before the IRS contacts you — once the agency initiates an examination, these options are no longer available.

- **Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures**: For non-willful filers. You file three years of amended returns and six years of FBARs. Domestic filers pay a 5% miscellaneous offshore penalty. Those living abroad may qualify for zero penalties. [IRS Streamlined Procedures page](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/streamlined-filing-compliance-procedures).
- **Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures**: If you have no unreported income, you can submit late FBARs with a reasonable cause statement. The IRS will not impose penalties if you owed no additional tax. [IRS Delinquent FBAR page](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/delinquent-fbar-submission-procedures).
- **Delinquent International Information Return Submission Procedures**: For late Form 8938 filings when reasonable cause exists. [IRS Delinquent Return page](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/delinquent-international-information-return-submission-procedures).

## FAQ

### Do I need to file both the FBAR and Form 8938?

If your foreign accounts exceed both thresholds, both filings are required. A single person living in the US with $80,000 in foreign accounts must file an FBAR under [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306) (over $10,000) and Form 8938 under [IRC § 6038D](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D) (assets exceeded $75,000 at any time). Filing one does not replace the other. Each goes to a separate agency through a separate channel.

### Can the IRS penalize me under both statutes for the same account?

Yes. FBAR penalties under [31 USC 5321](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321) and FATCA penalties under [26 USC 6038D(d)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D) are separate. The IRS can impose both sets of penalties for the same unreported foreign account in the same tax year. The penalties do not offset each other.

### Does Form 8938 replace the FBAR?

No. Congress created the Form 8938 obligation under FATCA in addition to the existing FBAR obligation. The [IRS explicitly states](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/comparison-of-form-8938-and-fbar-requirements) that Form 8938 does not replace the FBAR. Both remain independent filing obligations with separate enforcement.

### What if I only have a foreign pension account?

A foreign pension account — such as a Canadian RRSP, UK SIPP, or Australian retirement fund — qualifies as a foreign financial account for FBAR purposes. If the pension value plus any other foreign accounts exceeds $10,000, you must file an FBAR per [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306). For Form 8938, the pension is also a specified foreign financial asset under [IRC § 6038D](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D).

# FBAR vs FATCA Form 8938: Key Differences Every Filer Should Know

Many US taxpayers with foreign accounts confuse the FBAR with FATCA. Both require reporting of foreign financial accounts. Both carry steep penalties for non-compliance. They differ, however, in almost every detail: who enforces them, where you file, what thresholds trigger the obligation, and what penalties apply.

The FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) falls under the [Bank Secrecy Act](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5314) and goes to the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN). FATCA reporting through [Form 8938](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-8938) falls under the [Internal Revenue Code](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D) and goes to the IRS. Filing one does not satisfy the other. Most people with foreign accounts above certain thresholds must file both.

This article breaks down each reporting obligation. You will learn the thresholds, deadlines, penalties, covered accounts, and the most common mistakes filers make.

## FBAR: FinCEN Form 114

### What the FBAR Covers

The FBAR requires US persons to report foreign bank and financial accounts when the aggregate maximum value exceeds $10,000 at any point during the calendar year. This threshold comes from [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306). Filers commonly ask when the filing requirements apply — the answer turns on aggregate value, not the balance of any single account.

The $10,000 threshold applies to the combined total of all foreign accounts, not each account on its own. Two accounts peaking at $6,000 each at different time during the year produce an aggregate of $12,000. Both accounts become reportable.

For a full walkthrough of the filing process, see our [FBAR first-time filer guide](/blog/fbar-first-time-filer-guide).

### Who Files the FBAR

A "United States person" under [31 CFR 1010.350(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350) includes:

- US citizens (regardless of residence)
- Lawful permanent residents (green card holders)
- Resident aliens who meet the substantial presence test
- US-formed entities (corporations, partnerships, LLCs, trusts)

If you hold a green card and keep accounts in your home country, you have an FBAR obligation. Our article on [FBAR filing for green card holders](/blog/fbar-green-card-holders) covers that topic in detail.

### Reportable Account Types

The FBAR covers accounts held at foreign financial institutions:

- Bank accounts (checking, savings, time deposits)
- Securities and brokerage accounts
- Mutual fund accounts
- Insurance policies with cash surrender value
- Foreign pension and retirement accounts
- Accounts where you have signature authority but no ownership interest

The FBAR does not cover foreign stocks held in a US brokerage account. A share of Nestle stock in your Fidelity account is not reportable on the FBAR because Fidelity is a US institution.

### FBAR Filing Threshold

The FBAR has a single, flat threshold: **$10,000 in aggregate** at any point during the calendar year. Marital status, filing status, and country of residence do not affect this number. Whether you live in Ohio or Oslo, the threshold stays at $10,000.

This low threshold catches many people off guard. Foreign pension accounts, inheritance proceeds sitting in a parent's bank, and even a foreign account you rarely use all count toward the aggregate. Any of these can push your total to exceed $10,000 and trigger the FBAR obligation.

To understand how to determine whether you meet this threshold, see our guide on [how to calculate maximum account value for the FBAR](/blog/how-to-calculate-maximum-account-value-fbar).

### FBAR Deadline

The FBAR for the 2025 calendar year is due **April 15, 2026**. If you miss that date, you receive an automatic extension to **October 15, 2026** under [31 CFR 1010.306(c)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306). No form or request is needed. Read more in our article on the [2026 FBAR filing deadline](/blog/fbar-filing-deadline-2026).

### FBAR Penalties

The IRS enforces FBAR penalties on behalf of FinCEN. The penalty structure under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321) breaks into tiers:

- **Non-willful violation**: Up to **$12,909 per violation** (2026 inflation-adjusted amount) under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). Following the Supreme Court's ruling in *United States v. Bittner* (2023), non-willful penalties apply per report, not per account.
- **Willful violation**: The greater of **$100,000** or **50% of the account balance** at the time of the violation, per account, per year, under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321).
- **Criminal violation**: Up to **$250,000** in fines and **5 years** imprisonment under [31 USC 5322](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5322).

A single unreported account holding $200,000 can generate a willful penalty of $100,000 per year under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). Sustained over three years, the cumulative exposure reaches $300,000. For a deeper analysis, see our article on [FBAR penalties and what happens if you do not file](/blog/fbar-penalties-what-happens-if-you-dont-file).

## FATCA: Form 8938

### What FATCA Covers

FATCA — the Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act — was enacted in 2010 as part of the HIRE Act. The Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act operates on two levels. One side requires foreign financial institutions to report US account holders to the IRS. The other side, under [26 USC 6038D](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D), requires US taxpayers to report specified foreign financial assets on Form 8938.

The full title of the form is Form 8938 Statement of Specified Foreign Financial Assets. Beyond bank and brokerage accounts, Form 8938 also requires reporting of:

- Stock or securities issued by a non-US person
- Financial instruments or contracts with non-US issuers or counterparties
- Interests in foreign entities (partnerships, corporations, trusts, estates)
- Any foreign financial account maintained by a foreign financial institution

This broader scope means Form 8938 can capture assets the FBAR misses. If you own 25% of a foreign corporation, you report that interest on Form 8938. The FBAR would not cover that ownership interest unless the corporation holds a financial account where you have a financial interest.

### Who Files Form 8938

Only US persons who file a federal income tax return must file Form 8938 under [IRC § 6038D](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D). This represents a key difference from the FBAR. If your income falls below the filing threshold for a tax return and you do not file one, you have no Form 8938 obligation for that tax year.

The [IRS instructions for Form 8938](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8938) list the following filers:

- US citizens
- Resident aliens
- Nonresident aliens who elect to file a joint return with a US spouse
- Certain nonresident aliens who are bona fide residents of American Samoa or Puerto Rico

US entities may also have Form 8938 obligations under [IRS Notice 2013-10](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-13-10.pdf), though the IRS has not yet finalized regulations for domestic entities.

### FATCA Filing Thresholds

Unlike the FBAR's flat $10,000, FATCA thresholds vary based on your filing status and where you live. The thresholds come from [26 USC 6038D(a)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D) and [IRS guidance](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/corporations/summary-of-fatca-reporting-for-us-taxpayers):

**Living in the United States:**

- **Single or married filing separately**: File if total value exceeds $50,000 on the last day of the tax year, or if assets exceeded $75,000 at any time during the year.
- **Married filing jointly**: File if total value exceeds $100,000 on the last day of the tax year, or if assets exceeded $150,000 at any time during the year.

**Living outside the United States (qualifying under the bona fide residence test or physical presence test):**

- **Single or married filing separately**: File if total value exceeds $200,000 on the last day of the tax year, or if assets exceeded $300,000 at any time during the year.
- **Married filing jointly**: File if total value exceeds $400,000 on the last day of the tax year, or if assets exceeded $600,000 at any time during the year.

A married couple living in London who files jointly needs **$400,000** in foreign assets on December 31 before Form 8938 applies. That same couple would still need to file an FBAR if their foreign bank accounts exceeded $10,000 at any point.

### FATCA Deadline

Form 8938 attaches to your federal income tax return. The deadline matches your 1040 deadline: **April 15, 2026** for most filers per [IRC § 6038D](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D). If you file for an extension using IRS Form 4868, Form 8938 extends with it to **October 15, 2026**.

This differs from the FBAR. The FBAR extension is automatic and does not depend on a tax return extension. The Form 8938 extension only applies if you separately extend your tax return.

### FATCA Penalties

The penalty structure for Form 8938 under [26 USC 6038D(d)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D) works differently from FBAR penalties:

- **Failure to file**: $10,000 penalty per violation under [IRC § 6038D(d)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D).
- **Continued failure after IRS notice**: An additional $10,000 for each 30-day period (or part of a period) during which the failure continues after the IRS sends notice, per [IRC § 6038D(d)(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D). The maximum additional penalty is $50,000.
- **Total maximum**: $10,000 + $50,000 = **$60,000** for a single year of non-compliance.
- **Underpayment penalty**: If the failure to file results in an underpayment of tax, the IRS adds a 40% penalty on the understatement of tax attributable to undisclosed foreign assets under [26 USC 6662(j)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6662).

FATCA penalties are lower per violation than willful FBAR penalties. A willful FBAR violation on a $500,000 account produces a penalty of $250,000 (50% of balance) under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). The maximum FATCA penalty for the same year is $60,000 under [IRC § 6038D(d)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D). FATCA's 40% accuracy-related penalty on unreported income can also produce significant amounts.

### Statute of Limitations

Another difference involves the statute of limitations. The IRS has **three years** from the date you file your return to assess additional tax. If you omit more than $5,000 of income from specified foreign assets, the statute extends to **six years** under [26 USC 6501(e)(1)(A)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6501).

For the FBAR, the statute of limitations is **six years** from the due date of the report per [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306), regardless of whether income was omitted. Under [31 USC 5321(b)(1)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321), this six-year period runs from the date the report was required to be filed.

If you fail to file Form 8938 entirely, the statute of limitations on your income tax return does not start running for that year under [26 USC 6501(c)(8)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6501). The IRS can assess tax at any time. This open-ended exposure is a strong reason to file.

## When You Need to File Both

Most US taxpayers with foreign accounts above certain levels must file both the FBAR and Form 8938. Filing one does not satisfy the obligation to file the other. The [IRS comparison chart](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/comparison-of-form-8938-and-fbar-requirements) makes this clear. The two forms serve separate agencies and operate under separate statutory frameworks.

Here are common scenarios:

### Scenario 1: Expat With $250,000 in Foreign Accounts

Maria is a US citizen living in Germany. She has bank and brokerage accounts totaling $250,000.

- **FBAR**: Required. Her accounts exceed the $10,000 aggregate threshold.
- **Form 8938**: Required. She is single and living abroad. Her assets exceed the $200,000 year-end threshold.

Maria files the FBAR through BSA E-Filing and attaches Form 8938 to her 1040.

### Scenario 2: Green Card Holder With $30,000 Abroad

Raj is a green card holder living in New Jersey. He maintains $30,000 in a savings account at a bank in India.

- **FBAR**: Required. His account exceeds $10,000.
- **Form 8938**: Not required. Raj resides in the US and files as single. His $30,000 falls below the $50,000 year-end threshold and below the $75,000 any-time threshold.

Raj files the FBAR but has no Form 8938 obligation.

### Scenario 3: Married Couple With $120,000 Abroad

Tom and Lisa file jointly and reside in Chicago. They maintain $120,000 in a UK bank account.

- **FBAR**: Required. The account exceeds $10,000. Each spouse may file a separate FBAR, or they may file a joint FBAR under [31 CFR 1010.350(g)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.350). When each FBAR is filed separately, each spouse submits an individual report.
- **Form 8938**: Required. They live in the US and file jointly. Their $120,000 exceeds the $100,000 year-end threshold for married filing jointly.

Both filings cover the same UK account, but each serves a different agency.

### Scenario 4: Person With $8,000 Abroad

Susan has a Canadian bank account that peaked at $8,000 during the year.

- **FBAR**: Not required. The aggregate value never reached $10,000.
- **Form 8938**: Not required. The amount falls below all FATCA thresholds.

Neither filing obligation applies to Susan.

## Common Mistakes Filers Make

### Mistake 1: Thinking One Filing Covers Both

The FBAR goes to FinCEN. Form 8938 goes to the IRS with your tax return. Filing your FBAR does not satisfy the Form 8938 obligation. Filing Form 8938 does not satisfy the FBAR obligation. Each report must be submitted through its own channel to the appropriate agency.

### Mistake 2: Using the Wrong Threshold

The FBAR's $10,000 threshold catches many filers who assume the higher FATCA thresholds apply to all foreign account reporting. A single person living in the US with $60,000 in foreign bank accounts might skip the FBAR after seeing the $75,000 FATCA threshold. That $75,000 threshold applies only to Form 8938. The FBAR threshold is $10,000, regardless of filing status or residence.

### Mistake 3: Forgetting That FBAR Thresholds Do Not Change

FATCA thresholds vary based on filing status and residence. The FBAR threshold does not vary. A married couple living in London still faces the same $10,000 FBAR threshold as a single person living in Kansas. The $200,000 or $400,000 FATCA thresholds for expats have no bearing on the FBAR filing requirements.

### Mistake 4: Reporting Only Bank Accounts

Both filings cover more than bank accounts. The FBAR includes securities accounts, mutual funds, and foreign pension plans held at foreign financial institutions. Form 8938 goes further and includes foreign stock holdings, partnership interests, and contracts with foreign counterparties. Missing these assets produces an incomplete filing.

### Mistake 5: Not Converting to US Dollars

Both filings require values in US dollars. For the FBAR, use the [Treasury Department's year-end exchange rate](https://fiscaldata.treasury.gov/datasets/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/treasury-reporting-rates-of-exchange) published for December 31. For Form 8938, the IRS accepts the same Treasury rate or another verifiable exchange rate. Reporting in the foreign currency or using an incorrect rate is a common error.

### Mistake 6: Confusing Cryptocurrency Rules

Foreign crypto exchange accounts add complexity. FinCEN has signaled that [virtual currency accounts on foreign exchanges are reportable on the FBAR](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/shared/Notice-Virtual%20Currency%20Reporting%20on%20the%20FBAR%20123020.pdf). For Form 8938, cryptocurrency on a foreign exchange may also constitute a specified foreign financial asset. Our article on [FBAR and cryptocurrency on foreign exchanges](/blog/fbar-cryptocurrency-foreign-exchanges) covers this topic.

## How to File Each Report

### Filing the FBAR

You file the FBAR online through the [BSA E-Filing system](https://bsaefiling.fincen.treas.gov/) operated by the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network. Paper submission is not available. The FBAR is a standalone filing that does not attach to your tax return.

The process involves entering each foreign account, the financial institution name, account number, maximum value, and account type. If you have many accounts or complex holdings, preparing the filing can take time.

### Filing Form 8938

You file Form 8938 by attaching it to your federal income tax return (Form 1040). If you e-file, Form 8938 transmits with your return. If you paper file, you include it in the mailing.

Form 8938 requires more detailed information about each asset than the FBAR. You must describe the type of asset, its maximum value, income earned, and the name and address of the foreign institution or issuer. Most tax preparation software supports Form 8938.

## Penalties Compared: FBAR vs Form 8938

The penalty regimes differ significantly. Here is a focused comparison:

| Penalty Type | FBAR ([31 USC 5321](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321)) | Form 8938 ([26 USC 6038D(d)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D)) |
|---|---|---|
| **Non-willful** | Up to $12,909 per violation ([31 USC 5321(a)(5)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321)) | $10,000 per violation ([IRC § 6038D(d)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D)) |
| **Continued failure** | N/A | Additional $10,000 per 30-day period (max $50,000) ([IRC § 6038D(d)(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D)) |
| **Willful** | Greater of $100,000 or 50% of balance ([31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321)) | Same $10,000 + $50,000 maximum |
| **Criminal** | Up to $250,000 and/or 5 years ([31 USC 5322](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5322)) | General tax fraud penalties |
| **Accuracy penalty** | N/A | 40% on underpayment from undisclosed assets ([26 USC 6662(j)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6662)) |

FBAR willful penalties can substantially exceed FATCA penalties. An account holding $1,000,000 generates a potential willful FBAR penalty of $500,000 per year under [31 USC 5321(a)(5)(C)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321). The maximum FATCA penalty for the same year is $60,000 under [IRC § 6038D(d)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D). FATCA's 40% accuracy penalty on related unreported income adds another layer of exposure.

Both penalty regimes apply simultaneously. If you fail to file both reports, the IRS and the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network can each assess penalties under their respective statutes for the same account balance in the same year. These penalties do not offset each other — they accumulate.

## Voluntary Disclosure and Catch-Up Options

If you missed prior filings, several IRS programs can help reduce penalties:

- **Streamlined Filing Compliance Procedures**: For non-willful filers. You file three years of amended returns and six years of FBARs. Domestic filers pay a 5% miscellaneous offshore penalty. Those living abroad may qualify for zero penalties. [IRS Streamlined Procedures page](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/streamlined-filing-compliance-procedures).
- **Delinquent FBAR Submission Procedures**: If you have no unreported income, you can submit late FBARs with a reasonable cause statement. The IRS will not impose penalties if you owed no additional tax. [IRS Delinquent FBAR page](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/delinquent-fbar-submission-procedures).
- **Delinquent International Information Return Submission Procedures**: For late Form 8938 filings when reasonable cause exists. [IRS Delinquent Return page](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/delinquent-international-information-return-submission-procedures).

The IRS expects voluntary disclosure before it initiates contact. If the IRS discovers non-compliance first, these programs become unavailable.

## FAQ

### Do I need to file both the FBAR and Form 8938?

If your foreign accounts exceed both thresholds, both filings are required. A single person living in the US with $80,000 in foreign accounts must file an FBAR under [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306) (over $10,000) and Form 8938 under [IRC § 6038D](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D) (assets exceeded $75,000 at any time). Filing one does not replace the other. Each goes to a separate agency through a separate channel.

### Can the IRS penalize me under both statutes for the same account?

Yes. FBAR penalties under [31 USC 5321](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5321) and FATCA penalties under [26 USC 6038D(d)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D) are separate. The IRS can impose both sets of penalties for the same unreported foreign account in the same tax year. The penalties do not offset each other.

### Does Form 8938 replace the FBAR?

No. Congress created the Form 8938 obligation under FATCA in addition to the existing FBAR obligation. The [IRS explicitly states](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/comparison-of-form-8938-and-fbar-requirements) that Form 8938 does not replace the FBAR. Both remain independent filing obligations with separate enforcement.

### What if I only have a foreign pension account?

A foreign pension account — such as a Canadian RRSP, UK SIPP, or Australian retirement fund — qualifies as a foreign financial account for FBAR purposes. If the pension value plus any other foreign accounts is enough to exceed $10,000, you must file an FBAR per [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306). For Form 8938, the pension is also a specified foreign financial asset under [IRC § 6038D](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6038D). Apply the FATCA threshold for your filing status and residence to determine whether Form 8938 applies.

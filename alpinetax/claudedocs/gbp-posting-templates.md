## Deadline Reminders

### Template 1: Q1 Estimated Tax Deadline
**Category:** Deadline Reminder
**Best Month:** March (post 2–3 weeks before April 15)
**Suggested Image:** Calendar graphic highlighting April 15, or a clean desk with a calculator and planner

If you're self-employed or own an S-Corp or partnership, April 15 isn't just the individual filing deadline — it's also the due date for your Q1 estimated tax payment.

Missing this payment doesn't just mean a penalty. It can throw off your cash flow for the rest of the year and create a bigger surprise at tax time.

Here's what you should know:

**Who needs to pay?** Anyone who expects to owe $1,000 or more in federal taxes and doesn't have sufficient withholding — that includes most S-Corp owner-employees and freelancers.

**How much do you pay?** The IRS offers a "safe harbor" calculation: pay at least 100% of last year's tax liability (110% if your income was over $150,000), and you'll avoid underpayment penalties even if you owe more at filing.

**How do you pay?** IRS Direct Pay or EFTPS are the fastest options. Your state likely has a similar online portal — Colorado uses Revenue Online.

### Template 2: October 15 Extension Deadline
**Category:** Deadline Reminder
**Best Month:** September (post 2–3 weeks before October 15)
**Suggested Image:** Clock approaching midnight, or a calendar with October 15 circled

October 15 is one of the most commonly missed deadlines in the tax calendar — and one of the most consequential.

If you filed a tax extension back in April, your extended deadline is October 15. That extension gave you more time to *file* — but not more time to *pay*. Any taxes owed since April have been accruing interest and possibly penalties.

Here's what to do before the deadline:

**Gather your documents.** Year-end W-2s, 1099s, K-1s from any partnerships or S-Corps, mortgage interest statements, and records of deductible business expenses.

**Don't wait for everything to be perfect.** Filing with an estimate and amending later is far better than missing the deadline. Failure-to-file penalties are 10x higher than failure-to-pay penalties.

**Get professional help if you haven't already.** If your situation involves S-Corp distributions, self-employment income, rental properties, or business deductions, the complexity makes professional preparation worth every dollar.

### Template 3: Year-End Tax Planning
**Category:** Deadline Reminder / Seasonal Planning
**Best Month:** October–November
**Suggested Image:** Person reviewing documents at a laptop, autumn lighting, or a calendar showing Q4

December 31 isn't a deadline you can extend.

Every year, small business owners and S-Corp shareholders leave money on the table simply because they ran out of time to act. Year-end tax planning works — but only if you start before the year ends.

Here are three moves worth considering before December 31:

**Max out retirement contributions.** Solo 401(k), SEP-IRA, and defined benefit plans can dramatically reduce your taxable income. S-Corp owners have unique options worth discussing with a tax professional.

**Accelerate deductible expenses.** Equipment purchases, software subscriptions, professional development — if you plan to make these purchases anyway, doing them before December 31 means the deduction applies to *this* tax year.

**Review your S-Corp reasonable compensation.** If your salary as an S-Corp owner-employee needs adjustment, year-end is the time to make it. Waiting until January means it applies to next year's taxes.

### Template 4: Documents to Gather Before Filing
**Category:** Filing Tips
**Best Month:** January–February
**Suggested Image:** Organized desk with labeled folders, or a checklist graphic

Tax season goes smoother when you're not hunting for documents at the last minute.

Whether you're an individual filer, an S-Corp owner, or a partnership member, here's what to pull together before your first meeting with a tax professional:

**For individuals:**
- W-2s from all employers
- 1099s (freelance income, interest, dividends, retirement distributions)
- Form 1098 (mortgage interest)
- Charitable contribution receipts
- Prior year tax return

**For S-Corp and small business owners:**
- Year-end profit and loss statement
- Business bank and credit card statements
- Payroll records (W-2s issued to yourself and employees)
- Receipts for major deductions: home office, vehicle, equipment
- K-1s from any partnerships you're invested in

**For partnership returns:**
- Partner capital account balances
- Contributions and distributions during the year
- Ownership percentage changes (if any)

Missing documents are one of the biggest causes of filing delays and errors. Starting organized means finishing faster.

### Template 5: Common Filing Mistakes to Avoid
**Category:** Filing Tips
**Best Month:** February–March
**Suggested Image:** Red X marks over common tax forms, or a magnifying glass over a tax return

Most tax errors aren't complicated. They're the same mistakes made year after year — and they're almost entirely avoidable.

Here are the ones we see most often at Alpine Tax & Consulting LLC:

**Misclassifying income.** If you received a 1099-NEC (contractor income) but reported it as a hobby or left it off entirely, the IRS notices. Every 1099 the IRS receives is matched to your return.

**Missing S-Corp basis tracking.** S-Corp shareholders who claim losses need to have sufficient basis to deduct them. If basis isn't tracked correctly each year, you may be claiming deductions you're not entitled to.

**Incorrect reasonable compensation.** S-Corp owner-employees who underpay themselves (or pay nothing) create significant audit risk. The IRS scrutinizes this closely.

**Forgetting estimated tax payments.** If you made Q1–Q4 estimated tax payments, those amounts must be entered on your return. Omitting them means you'll overpay — or your refund will be delayed while the IRS reconciles.

**Filing status errors.** This is more common than it sounds, especially after life changes: marriage, divorce, a new dependent, or a change in household.

## S-Corp Education

### Template 7: S-Corp Benefits Overview
**Category:** S-Corp Education
**Best Month:** September–October (S-Corp election planning season)
**Suggested Image:** Clean graphic comparing LLC vs S-Corp tax savings, or business owner reviewing documents

Most small business owners know S-Corps can save money on taxes. Fewer understand exactly how — or whether it applies to their situation.

Here's the core concept: when you operate as a sole proprietor or single-member LLC, every dollar of profit is subject to self-employment tax (15.3% up to the Social Security wage base). An S-Corp changes that.

As an S-Corp owner-employee, you pay yourself a reasonable salary — which is subject to payroll taxes. But profits distributed above that salary are passed through to your personal return without payroll taxes. That's where the savings happen.

**A simplified example:** A business generating $120,000 in profit. As a sole proprietor, the full amount is subject to self-employment tax. As an S-Corp with a $65,000 reasonable salary, only the salary portion triggers payroll taxes — potentially saving $5,000–$8,000+ annually depending on your situation.

S-Corp taxation isn't right for everyone. The benefits are most clear when annual net profit consistently exceeds $40,000–$50,000. Below that threshold, the administrative costs of S-Corp status — payroll, annual filings, accounting — can outweigh the savings.

### Template 8: Reasonable Compensation Basics
**Category:** S-Corp Education
**Best Month:** January–February (before payroll is set for the year) or October (year-end review)
**Suggested Image:** Salary scale graphic, or a business owner reviewing payroll documents

If you own an S-Corp, "reasonable compensation" is one of the most important concepts to understand — and one of the IRS's most active areas of scrutiny.

Here's the issue: S-Corp owner-employees are required to pay themselves a reasonable salary for the services they perform. This salary is subject to payroll taxes. Some S-Corp owners underpay themselves to minimize payroll taxes, keeping more income as distributions.

The IRS is aware of this. Audits targeting S-Corps frequently focus on whether the owner's salary is genuinely reasonable given their role, industry, and the revenue they generate.

What counts as "reasonable"? The IRS looks at factors including:
- What similar businesses pay for similar roles
- How much time the owner spends working in the business
- The profitability of the business
- The owner's qualifications and experience

There's no magic formula, but there are reasonable benchmarks. A common approach is to target a salary that represents 40–60% of the S-Corp's net profit, adjusted for your specific role and industry.

Getting this wrong can trigger back taxes, penalties, and interest. Getting it right is a legitimate, IRS-sanctioned tax strategy.

### Template 9: S-Corp Election Timing
**Category:** S-Corp Education
**Best Month:** October–November (planning for next year) or January (just in time for current year)
**Suggested Image:** Calendar with key dates marked, or a business owner reviewing IRS Form 2553

If you're considering electing S-Corp status for your business, timing is everything.

To be taxed as an S-Corp for a given calendar year, you need to file IRS Form 2553 either:
- **Before March 15** of the tax year, for it to apply to that year
- **By December 31 of the prior year**, if you want clean, full-year S-Corp treatment

Miss that window, and your election defaults to the following year. That's a year of potential tax savings that can't be recovered.

There is an option for late elections: the IRS allows relief for "reasonable cause" failures to file on time. This is available in many cases, but it adds complexity, and it's not guaranteed.

**Is now the right time to elect?**

S-Corp election makes the most financial sense when:
- Your business generates consistent net profit of $40,000+ annually
- You're currently paying self-employment tax on all your business income
- You have the bandwidth to manage payroll (or work with a payroll provider)
- Your state allows S-Corp treatment and the benefits outweigh state fees

## Seasonal Planning

### Template 12: Mid-Year Tax Check-In
**Category:** Seasonal Planning
**Best Month:** June–July
**Suggested Image:** Mid-year calendar graphic, or a business owner reviewing a profit and loss report at a laptop

You don't have to wait until January to know where you stand on taxes.

Mid-year is actually one of the best times to take a quick look at your tax position — especially if you're a small business owner, S-Corp shareholder, or self-employed. Here's why it matters now:

**Your estimated taxes should reflect your actual income.** If business is up significantly from last year, your Q1 and Q2 payments may not be enough. A mid-year check prevents a large underpayment bill in April.

**Year-end planning opportunities start now.** Retirement contributions, equipment purchases, hiring decisions — many of the most effective tax strategies require runway. Knowing your approximate income by July gives you six months to act.

**Reasonable compensation adjustments for S-Corp owners.** If your business income has shifted significantly from last year, it may be time to revisit your salary. Adjustments made mid-year are cleaner than retroactive corrections.

**Life changes affect your tax picture.** Marriage, a new dependent, a new side business, buying property — any of these can shift your expected tax liability for the year.

A mid-year check-in doesn't need to be a full consultation. It can be a 30-minute Zoom conversation to review where you stand and confirm you're on track.

Most small business owners leave tens of thousands of dollars on the table every year — not because they're doing anything wrong, but because they haven't optimized their business structure. The S-Corp election is one of the most powerful tax strategies available to self-employed people and small business owners, yet it's widely misunderstood, misapplied, or ignored entirely.

This guide covers everything you need to know about S-Corp taxation: how it works, what it costs to operate, what you can deduct, when it makes sense, and what mistakes to avoid. If your business earns $50,000 or more in net profit, this is worth your full attention.

---

## What Is an S-Corporation?

An S-corporation is not a separate business entity type — it's a **tax election**. You still form a standard corporation (or in some cases, an LLC) under your state's laws. The "S-Corp" designation simply tells the IRS how you want that entity taxed.

By default, a single-member LLC is taxed as a sole proprietor. A multi-member LLC is taxed as a partnership. A corporation is taxed as a C-Corp. An S-Corp election changes that default — it instructs the IRS to treat your corporation or LLC like a pass-through entity with special rules around self-employment tax.

### Pass-Through Taxation

The defining feature of an S-Corp is **pass-through taxation**: the business itself does not pay federal income tax. Instead, the corporation's profits (and losses) pass through to the shareholders' personal tax returns. Each shareholder receives a Schedule K-1 showing their share of income, deductions, and credits, which flows onto their Form 1040.

This is different from a **C-Corporation**, where the company pays corporate income tax on profits, and then shareholders pay personal income tax again when dividends are distributed — the so-called "double taxation" problem.

### S-Corp vs. C-Corp

| | S-Corp | C-Corp |
|---|---|---|
| Federal income tax | Pass-through to shareholders | Entity-level, 21% flat rate |
| Dividend taxation | Generally not taxed again | Qualified dividends taxed at capital gains rates |
| Shareholders | Max 100, US persons only | Unlimited, any entity |
| Stock classes | One class only | Multiple classes allowed |
| Best for | Small/mid-sized owner-operated businesses | Businesses planning VC funding or complex ownership |

For the overwhelming majority of small business owners with a handful of owners, an S-Corp beats a C-Corp on taxes.

## How S-Corp Taxation Works

Here's the core tax advantage of an S-Corp, illustrated simply:

If you operate as a **sole proprietor or single-member LLC** and earn $120,000 in net profit, you pay **self-employment tax (15.3%)** on all $120,000 — that's $18,360 in SE tax before you even get to income tax.

As an **S-Corp owner-employee**, you split that $120,000 into two buckets:
- **W-2 salary**: $70,000 (your "reasonable compensation")
- **S-Corp distribution**: $50,000

You pay payroll taxes (FICA: 15.3%, split between employer and employee) only on the $70,000 salary. The $50,000 distribution is not subject to payroll taxes. That's roughly **$7,650 in payroll tax savings** — in a single year.

That savings grows as your income grows. At $200,000 net income with a $90,000 salary, the distribution of $110,000 avoids approximately $14,000+ in FICA taxes (above the Social Security wage base, only Medicare applies, so actual savings vary).

### The Schedule K-1

Each year, the S-Corp files **Form 1120-S** (the S-Corp tax return) and issues a **Schedule K-1** to each shareholder. The K-1 shows:
- Ordinary business income or loss
- Separately stated items (Section 179 deductions, charitable contributions, investment income, etc.)
- Basis adjustments

You attach the K-1 to your personal return. Your accountant or tax preparer reconciles it against your W-2 wages to calculate your total tax liability.

### The Reasonable Compensation Requirement

The IRS is not naive. If S-Corp owners could take all income as distributions and pay zero payroll taxes, everyone would do it. That's why the IRS requires owner-employees to pay themselves a **reasonable salary** — a W-2 wage comparable to what you'd pay someone else to do your job.

This is one of the most nuanced aspects of S-Corp taxation and a frequent audit trigger if ignored. We cover it in depth in our guide to [S-Corp reasonable compensation](/blog/scorp-reasonable-compensation-guide).

## Electing S-Corp Status

To be taxed as an S-Corp, you file **Form 2553** ("Election by a Small Business Corporation") with the IRS. You're not creating a new entity — you're changing how your existing entity is taxed.

### Eligibility Requirements

Before filing, confirm your business meets all S-Corp eligibility rules:
- **Must be a domestic corporation or eligible LLC**
- **No more than 100 shareholders**
- **Only one class of stock** (LLCs: only one class of membership interest)
- **All shareholders must be U.S. citizens or permanent residents** (no foreign individuals, no corporations, no partnerships as shareholders — though certain trusts qualify)

Most small business owners qualify without issue.

### Timing: The January 15 Rule

To have your S-Corp election apply for a given tax year, you must file Form 2553:
- **By March 15** of that tax year (for calendar-year entities), OR
- **Within 75 days of the start of the tax year** if electing for the current year

Miss the deadline? You're looking at S-Corp status starting the following year — unless you qualify for late election relief.

### Late Election Relief

The IRS allows late S-Corp elections under **Revenue Procedure 2013-30** if you can show "reasonable cause" for missing the deadline. In practice, the IRS grants this relief fairly often, especially when the entity has operated as if it were an S-Corp (filed 1120-S, issued K-1s, run payroll). The late election paperwork is filed with Form 2553 and a written statement explaining the circumstances.

A detailed walkthrough of the election process, including the LLC to S-Corp election, will be covered in a future post. For now, if you're considering electing, don't wait — the sooner you do it, the sooner the savings start.

## S-Corp vs. LLC: Tax Comparison

This is the most common question we hear: "Should I stay as an LLC or elect S-Corp?" The answer depends entirely on your net income.

An LLC (taxed as a sole proprietor or partnership) is simpler and cheaper to operate. An S-Corp costs more to run — payroll, a separate tax return, additional compliance — but saves more in taxes as income rises.

Here's a side-by-side comparison at different income levels:

| Annual Net Income | LLC (Sole Prop) SE Tax | S-Corp Payroll Tax | Estimated S-Corp Savings |
|---|---|---|---|
| $40,000 | ~$5,650 | ~$5,000 | Minimal (may not offset costs) |
| $80,000 | ~$11,300 | ~$7,500 | ~$3,800/year |
| $120,000 | ~$16,950 | ~$10,000 | ~$6,950/year |
| $180,000 | ~$22,000 | ~$12,000 | ~$10,000/year |

*Estimates based on 2025 FICA rates and typical salary-to-distribution splits. Individual results vary.*

The S-Corp also introduces costs: payroll processing (typically $50–$150/month with a service like Gusto or QuickBooks Payroll), a separate corporate tax return (Form 1120-S), and additional bookkeeping complexity.

## S-Corp Payroll Requirements

This is where many new S-Corp owners get into trouble. Operating as an S-Corp is **not optional** when it comes to payroll — if you are an owner-employee who works in the business, you must run payroll and pay yourself a W-2 salary.

### Why Payroll Is Mandatory

The IRS treats shareholder-employees who provide services to the S-Corp as employees for FICA tax purposes. Taking distributions in lieu of salary is a red flag that can result in the IRS recharacterizing distributions as wages — plus penalties and back taxes.

### Payroll Taxes You Owe

As an S-Corp employer, you handle two types of payroll taxes:

**FICA (Social Security + Medicare)**
- Social Security: 6.2% employer + 6.2% employee = 12.4% (on wages up to $176,100 in 2025)
- Medicare: 1.45% employer + 1.45% employee = 2.9% (no wage cap)
- Additional Medicare: 0.9% on employee wages above $200,000 (single filer)

**FUTA (Federal Unemployment Tax)**
- 6% on the first $7,000 of each employee's wages (reduced to as low as 0.6% with state credits)

**State payroll taxes** — Colorado has state income tax withholding plus state unemployment (SUTA).

### Payroll Frequency

Most small S-Corps run **biweekly or semimonthly payroll**. The IRS requires you to deposit payroll taxes based on a schedule tied to your deposit history (monthly or semiweekly depositor). Miss a deposit and you'll face penalties ranging from 2% to 15% of the unpaid amount.

Payroll services like **Gusto, QuickBooks Payroll, or ADP** handle the calculations, deposits, and filings automatically. The cost is almost always worth the compliance protection.

### Common Payroll Mistakes

- **Paying yourself quarterly instead of regularly**: The IRS scrutinizes infrequent payroll as a red flag
- **Not withholding enough for income taxes**: Your W-2 withholding should cover your expected income tax liability
- **Forgetting quarterly payroll returns**: Form 941 is due by the last day of the month following each quarter

## S-Corp Tax Deductions

One of the lesser-appreciated benefits of S-Corp status is access to certain deductions that aren't available (or are less advantageous) to sole proprietors.

### Health Insurance Premiums

If you pay for your own health insurance as an S-Corp shareholder-employee who owns more than 2% of the company, the company can deduct the premiums as a business expense AND you include the premiums as W-2 income — which then allows you to deduct 100% of the premium on your personal return (Schedule 1, Line 17).

This effectively gives you a deduction that reduces income tax without affecting your payroll tax savings. It's one of the cleanest tax wins for self-employed people.

**Example**: You pay $8,400/year in health insurance. The S-Corp adds it to your W-2 as income ($8,400 more in Box 1), then you deduct $8,400 on your 1040. Net effect: zero income tax impact, and it's properly structured for the IRS.

### Retirement Plan Contributions

S-Corp owners can participate in employer-sponsored retirement plans with higher contribution limits than individual IRAs:

**SEP-IRA**
- Employer contribution up to 25% of W-2 compensation
- If your salary is $80,000, max SEP contribution = $20,000
- Easy to set up, no employee contribution option

**Solo 401(k)**
- Employee contribution: up to $23,500 (2025), or $31,000 if age 50+
- Employer contribution: up to 25% of W-2 wages
- Combined limit: $70,000 (2025)
- Best for maximizing retirement contributions as a single-employee S-Corp

**Example**: S-Corp owner with $90,000 salary contributes $23,500 as employee + $22,500 as employer match = $46,000 in tax-deferred retirement savings. That's $46,000 less in taxable income.

### Home Office Deduction

S-Corp home office is more complex than for sole proprietors but still achievable. The cleanest approach is an **accountable plan**: the S-Corp reimburses you for home office expenses based on the square footage of your office relative to your home. The reimbursement is deductible by the S-Corp and tax-free to you.

The S-Corp cannot take a direct home office deduction on Form 1120-S (that's for sole proprietors on Schedule C). Reimbursement via accountable plan is the correct structure.

### Vehicle Expenses

Same principle applies: you can either have the S-Corp own the vehicle directly, or reimburse you for business use of your personal vehicle using the **IRS standard mileage rate** (67 cents/mile in 2024, 70 cents/mile in 2025) or actual expense method — through an accountable plan.

If the S-Corp owns the vehicle, it deducts depreciation, fuel, insurance, and maintenance directly. Consult your tax preparer on which approach makes more sense given your vehicle's value and usage.

### Ordinary Business Expenses

All ordinary and necessary business expenses remain deductible at the S-Corp level:
- Software subscriptions and tools
- Professional development and education
- Business insurance
- Professional services (legal, accounting)
- Marketing and advertising
- Office supplies and equipment (including Section 179 expensing for larger purchases)

## Quarterly Tax Obligations

Running an S-Corp means more tax deadlines than a simple sole proprietorship. Miss one and you'll owe penalties. Here's what to track:

### Payroll Tax Deposits

Payroll taxes must be deposited with the IRS on a specific schedule — **not just when you file your quarterly return**:

- **Monthly depositors**: Taxes due by the 15th of the following month
- **Semiweekly depositors**: Most larger employers; deposits due within 3 business days of payroll

Most small S-Corps start as monthly depositors. Check your IRS deposit schedule notice each November for the following year.

### Quarterly Payroll Returns

| Return | What It Covers | Due Date |
|---|---|---|
| Form 941 | Federal income tax + FICA withholding | April 30, July 31, Oct 31, Jan 31 |
| Form 940 | Annual FUTA (filed once/year) | January 31 |
| State payroll returns | Varies by state | Varies |

### Estimated Income Tax Payments

The S-Corp itself doesn't pay federal income tax, but *you* still owe tax on your K-1 income throughout the year. That's handled through **estimated tax payments** on Form 1040-ES, due quarterly:

- **Q1**: April 15
- **Q2**: June 16
- **Q3**: September 15
- **Q4**: January 15 (following year)

Calculate your estimated payments using either the **safe harbor method** (pay 100% of last year's tax liability — 110% if your AGI exceeded $150,000) or estimate your current year's actual liability.

### Annual S-Corp Return

**Form 1120-S** (the S-Corp tax return) is due **March 15** for calendar-year entities — one month before individual returns. If you need more time, file **Form 7004** for an automatic 6-month extension (to September 15), but this does not extend your payment deadline.

For more detail on the full calendar of Colorado small business tax deadlines, see our post on [Denver small business tax deadlines](/blog/denver-small-business-tax-deadlines).

## Common S-Corp Tax Mistakes

### 1. Underpaying Reasonable Compensation

This is the number-one S-Corp audit trigger. If you're taking $150,000 in distributions and paying yourself a $20,000 salary, that's a red flag. The IRS can reclassify distributions as wages, charge back payroll taxes plus interest, and assess a 20% accuracy penalty.

A reasonable salary is roughly what you'd pay a market-rate employee to do your work. For a consultant earning $200,000, a reasonable salary might be $90,000–$120,000. Document your reasoning and review it annually.

### 2. Missing Payroll Tax Deposit Deadlines

The failure-to-deposit penalty is severe: 2% for deposits 1–5 days late, up to 15% for amounts more than 10 days overdue. Use a payroll service with automatic deposits to eliminate this risk entirely.

### 3. Commingling Business and Personal Funds

Your S-Corp must have a separate bank account. Distributions should be recorded as such. Paying personal expenses directly from the S-Corp account is treated as additional compensation by the IRS — and could be taxable income you didn't report.

### 4. Filing the S-Corp Election Late

Missing the March 15 election deadline means you're a C-Corp or default LLC for that entire tax year. Late election relief is available but requires paperwork and a good explanation. Don't procrastinate on this.

### 5. Ignoring State-Level Requirements

An S-Corp election is a **federal election**. States handle it differently. Some states automatically recognize the federal election. Others (like New York) require a separate state filing. Colorado recognizes the federal S-Corp election, but you still need to register your entity with the Secretary of State and file annual reports.

### 6. Skipping the S-Corp Return

Form 1120-S is a separate filing with a March 15 deadline. Failure to file results in a **$235 per shareholder per month** penalty — even if no taxes are owed at the entity level.

## When to Consider S-Corp Status

S-Corp status is not right for every business. Here's how to think about it:

### The Income Threshold

The general rule of thumb: S-Corp status makes financial sense when your **net self-employment income exceeds $40,000–$50,000 per year**. Below that threshold, the compliance costs (payroll service, separate tax return) often outweigh the FICA savings.

At $80,000 net income, you might save $3,000–$5,000 in payroll taxes. If your added compliance costs are $2,000/year, you're still ahead — but the margin is narrow. At $120,000+, the savings become more compelling.

### When S-Corp Makes Sense

- You're running a **profitable service-based business** (consulting, coaching, design, legal, real estate, etc.)
- Your net income consistently exceeds $50,000
- You're already running payroll for employees (adds minimal complexity)
- You want to maximize retirement plan contributions
- You plan to operate the business for multiple years

### When S-Corp Is NOT Worth It

- **Net income below $40,000**: The compliance cost exceeds the tax savings
- **High retirement contributions already**: If you're maxing out a Solo 401(k) as a sole proprietor, your effective SE tax on net income is already lower
- **You're planning to sell or raise investment**: Investors often prefer C-Corp structure for equity flexibility
- **You don't want to run payroll**: The mandatory payroll requirement is a real operational commitment
- **Your industry has specific licensing considerations**: Some professional licensing boards or state bars have rules about business structure

The right answer depends on your specific numbers, industry, and long-term plans. A personalized analysis is always more reliable than a general rule.

## Colorado-Specific S-Corp Considerations

Most S-Corp rules are federal, but Colorado has its own nuances worth knowing.

### Colorado Flat Income Tax

Colorado taxes pass-through income at a **flat 4.4% rate** (as of 2024). This applies to your S-Corp income shown on your K-1 — there's no graduated state rate to optimize around, unlike some other states. Your Colorado return mirrors your federal return in most respects.

### State S-Corp Recognition

Colorado **automatically recognizes** the federal S-Corp election. You do not need to file a separate Colorado S-Corp election form. However, you must still register your entity with the Colorado Secretary of State and maintain a registered agent in the state.

Colorado S-Corps file **Form DR 0106** (the Colorado Partnership and S-Corporation Tax Return) annually, due the same day as the federal return (March 15 for calendar-year filers). If you owe Colorado income tax — which S-Corp owners typically do based on their K-1 income — you must make **Colorado estimated tax payments** as well, due on the same federal estimated tax schedule.

### Denver Occupational Privilege Tax

If you or your employees work in **Denver**, the city imposes an **Occupational Privilege Tax (OPT)** — sometimes called the "head tax." The employee portion is $5.75/month, and the employer portion is $4.00/month per employee. If you pay yourself a W-2 salary and work in Denver, you owe OPT on both sides.

This is a small dollar amount but a real compliance obligation. Denver OPT returns are filed monthly.

### Colorado LLC vs. Corporation

If you're deciding whether to form a Colorado LLC (then elect S-Corp status) or form a Colorado corporation, the LLC route is generally simpler and less expensive. Colorado LLCs have fewer formalities (no board meetings, no corporate minutes requirements), and you can still elect S-Corp status with Form 2553 once the LLC is formed.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much can an S-Corp save me on taxes?**

It depends on your net income and what you pay yourself as a salary. In general, expect to save $4,000–$15,000+ per year in self-employment/payroll taxes on net income between $80,000–$200,000. The savings increase with income and decrease as a percentage once wages exceed the Social Security wage base ($176,100 in 2025).

**Can a single-member LLC elect S-Corp status?**

Yes. A single-member LLC is eligible to elect S-Corp taxation by filing Form 2553. The election does not change the LLC's legal structure — you retain the liability protection and flexible operating agreement of an LLC while being taxed under S-Corp rules.

**What happens if I don't pay myself a reasonable salary?**

The IRS can audit your return and recharacterize distributions as wages. If they do, you'll owe back payroll taxes (both employer and employee share), interest, and potentially a 20% understatement penalty. In egregious cases, they can assess trust fund recovery penalties personally against you.

**Do I need a separate bank account for my S-Corp?**

Yes. Commingling personal and business funds is one of the fastest ways to lose your liability protection and create accounting problems. Open a dedicated business checking account for the S-Corp immediately.

**When is the S-Corp tax return due?**

Form 1120-S is due **March 15** for calendar-year S-Corps. You can file an automatic 6-month extension (to September 15) using Form 7004. Note: this extends the filing deadline, not your payment deadline — any taxes owed should still be estimated and paid by March 15.

**Can my spouse be a co-owner of the S-Corp?**

Yes. A married couple can both be shareholders, and in community property states there are special rules around spousal ownership. In non-community property states (like Colorado), spouses who both own stock are treated as separate shareholders, each counted toward the 100-shareholder limit.

**What if my S-Corp loses money?**

S-Corp losses pass through to shareholders just like income. You can deduct losses on your personal return — but only up to your **tax basis** in the S-Corp (your investment plus loans you've made to the company). Losses above your basis are suspended and carried forward until you have sufficient basis to absorb them.

**Do I still need to file a personal return if my S-Corp passes through all income?**

Yes, always. The S-Corp files Form 1120-S and issues you a K-1. You then report that K-1 income (and your W-2 wages) on your personal Form 1040. There is no scenario where an S-Corp election eliminates your personal filing requirement.

**Is S-Corp status permanent once I elect it?**

No. You can revoke an S-Corp election, though there are restrictions. Generally, if you revoke it, you cannot re-elect S-Corp status for five years. Termination also occurs automatically if you violate eligibility requirements (e.g., exceed 100 shareholders, add an ineligible shareholder).

**Can I convert my C-Corp to an S-Corp?**

Yes, but with caveats. Built-in gains tax may apply for 5 years after the conversion on assets that appreciated while the company was a C-Corp. For businesses with significant appreciated assets, this is an important planning consideration.

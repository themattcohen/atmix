The entire tax advantage of an S-Corp rests on splitting your income between salary and distributions. Salary gets hit with payroll taxes. Distributions don't. So there's a natural temptation to pay yourself as little salary as possible — or nothing at all.

The IRS knows this. And they have a name for the rule that stops it: **reasonable compensation**.

Understanding this requirement isn't optional if you're operating as an S-Corp. Getting it wrong triggers payroll tax assessments, penalties, and interest — sometimes years after the fact.

## What "Reasonable Compensation" Actually Means

The IRS requires that S-Corp shareholder-employees who perform services for the corporation receive a salary that is "reasonable" for the work they perform. This comes from **Rev. Rul. 74-44**, which established that compensation cannot be artificially low simply to reduce payroll taxes.

In plain terms: you can't pay yourself $1 in salary and take $200,000 in distributions just because it's legal to set up payroll that way.

The IRS defines reasonable compensation as the amount a similar business would pay an unrelated employee to perform the same services. It's a market-rate standard, not a formula.

## Factors the IRS Uses

When auditors evaluate whether a shareholder-employee's salary is reasonable, they look at a specific set of factors. Courts have used this framework consistently:

**Training and experience.** A licensed professional with 20 years in their field commands a higher market rate than someone just starting out. Your credentials, certifications, and track record matter.

**Duties and responsibilities.** What do you actually do in the business? If you're the sole operator running client relationships, doing the work, managing billing, and handling operations, that scope of work justifies a higher salary than a part-time administrative role.

**Time and effort devoted.** Full-time involvement means a full-time salary is expected. Working 5 hours a week is different from 50.

**Comparable salaries.** The IRS and courts look at what similar businesses pay employees in comparable roles. Industry salary surveys, Bureau of Labor Statistics data, and expert testimony all play a role in disputes.

**Company size and complexity.** A $2M revenue business has more demands on its leadership than a $150K sole-operator shop. Larger operations typically support higher reasonable compensation.

**Dividend history.** If the company is consistently profitable and distributions are large relative to salary, that pattern draws scrutiny. A 90/10 distribution-to-salary split on $400K in income is a red flag.

## The Case You Need to Know: Watson v. United States

In 2012, the Eighth Circuit Court affirmed a landmark case involving a tax firm owner named David Watson. Watson paid himself a $24,000 annual salary while taking over $200,000 in S-Corp distributions in two separate years.

The IRS reclassified a substantial portion of those distributions as wages and assessed back payroll taxes, penalties, and interest. The court agreed with the IRS — the salary was unreasonably low given Watson's experience, the revenue he generated, and what comparable professionals earned.

The total cost to Watson far exceeded what he would have owed by simply paying himself a reasonable salary from the start.

This case is frequently cited in audits and is the reason any competent tax professional will push back if your salary is dramatically below your distributions.

## How to Document Your Salary Decision

Documentation won't guarantee you win an audit, but it's your primary defense if one happens. Keep records that support why your salary is what it is.

**Maintain a written salary analysis.** Note the comparable salaries you researched, the factors you weighed, and the number you landed on. Update this annually.

**Use third-party data.** BLS Occupational Employment data, Robert Half salary guides, and industry-specific surveys are sources auditors recognize. Screenshot and save what you referenced.

**Record the board resolution.** An S-Corp should have corporate minutes reflecting the compensation decision. Even as a solo owner, document it formally.

**Be consistent.** If your salary changes dramatically from year to year without a corresponding change in your role or the business's performance, it raises questions.

## Common Mistakes

**Paying too little.** The Watson case is the classic example. A $20,000–$30,000 salary on $300,000+ in income almost never holds up to scrutiny.

**Paying nothing.** Some owners skip payroll entirely in early years, especially if the business has a loss. If the business has net income and you're working in it, zero salary is not defensible.

**Paying too much.** Yes, this is also a mistake — though less common. Overpaying yourself in salary increases your payroll tax burden unnecessarily. The goal is accuracy, not the lowest possible number.

**Setting it once and forgetting it.** Your salary should be reviewed annually. If your revenue grows significantly, your salary should grow with it.

## How to Set Your S-Corp Salary in Practice

Start with what the market pays for your role. Use BLS data or salary surveys for your specific occupation and region. Then adjust based on the factors above — your experience, how much time you spend, and whether you wear multiple hats.

A rough framework many practitioners use:

- Identify the market rate for your primary function (e.g., financial consultant, graphic designer, software developer)
- Adjust upward if you handle multiple roles (sales + operations + delivery)
- Adjust downward slightly if you're part-time or the business is early-stage
- Set a salary within a defensible range — not the absolute minimum

For most owner-operators in professional services netting $75,000–$300,000 annually, reasonable compensation often falls between $45,000 and $120,000 depending on the specifics. There's no universal answer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does the IRS have a specific formula for reasonable compensation?**
No. The IRS uses a facts-and-circumstances test based on the factors above, not a fixed percentage of revenue or profit. This is why documentation and comparables matter — your salary has to be defensible based on your specific situation.

**What happens if the IRS reclassifies my distributions as wages?**
They'll assess the unpaid payroll taxes on the reclassified amount, plus a failure-to-deposit penalty (up to 15%), plus interest. In some cases, a 20% accuracy-related penalty applies as well. The total can be significant — and it applies retroactively.

**Can I change my salary mid-year?**
Yes, but changes should be documented with a board resolution and should reflect a genuine change in role or business circumstances. Frequent adjustments without clear rationale look opportunistic to an auditor.

## What an Enrolled Agent is

An Enrolled Agent is a tax practitioner who has been granted enrolled agent status by the IRS. It is a federal designation, and it authorizes the holder to represent taxpayers before the IRS.

Enrolled Agents have what the IRS calls unlimited practice rights. That means an Enrolled Agent can represent any taxpayer, on any tax matter, before any IRS office. This includes audits, collection matters, and appeals.

## How the status is earned

There are two routes to enrolled agent status.

- **The Special Enrollment Examination (SEE).** The exam has three parts covering individual taxation, business taxation, and representation, practices, and procedures. A candidate must pass all three parts.
- **Qualifying IRS experience.** Certain former IRS employees with relevant technical experience can qualify without taking the exam.

Either way, the applicant must also pass an IRS background check, which includes a review of their own tax compliance history.

## Staying current

Enrolled Agents must complete 72 hours of continuing education every three years, with a minimum of 16 hours each year. They also renew their enrollment with the IRS on a regular cycle. Tax law changes every year, and these requirements exist to keep practitioners current on it.

## How this differs from a preparer who only has a PTIN

Anyone who is paid to prepare federal tax returns must have a Preparer Tax Identification Number, or PTIN. A PTIN is a registration with the IRS. It does not by itself reflect any exam or any authority to represent a client.

Some preparers also complete the IRS Annual Filing Season Program. Participants who earn a Record of Completion get limited representation rights. In general, those rights apply only to returns the preparer prepared and signed, and only before certain IRS personnel, such as revenue agents and customer service representatives. They do not extend to appeals or collections matters.

An Enrolled Agent does not have those limits. If you want a full description of any preparer's authority, the IRS publishes a directory of federal tax return preparers with credentials, and you can ask any preparer to explain what they are authorized to do.

## When it is useful

For a simple return, the credential may not change much. It is most useful when something goes beyond filing:

- You receive an IRS notice, such as a CP2000 underreporter notice, and want someone to respond for you.
- Your return is selected for audit.
- You owe a balance and want to discuss payment options with the IRS.
- You want one person who prepared your return to also speak for you if the IRS has questions about it.

In each case, representation is done with your written authorization. You decide what the person can do on your behalf.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is an Enrolled Agent the same as a CPA?**
No. A CPA is licensed by a state board of accountancy. An Enrolled Agent holds a federal status granted by the IRS. Both can represent taxpayers before the IRS, but they are different credentials. Vinnie is an Enrolled Agent, not a CPA.

**Do I need an Enrolled Agent to file my return?**
No. Any preparer with a valid PTIN can prepare and sign a return. The credential is mainly useful if you want representation before the IRS.

**Can an Enrolled Agent represent me even if they did not prepare my return?**
Yes. Unlimited practice rights do not depend on who prepared the return.

The One Diligence Item Buyers Skip—That Can Wreck Their Transition

Created on 2025-08-03 18:09

Published on 2025-08-08 13:30

When you're buying a business, due diligence can feel like drinking from a fire hose. Between reviewing financials, analyzing margins and add-backs, and making sure the numbers pencil out, it’s easy to overlook the so-called “small stuff.”

SO buckle up for a light diligence horror story.

But I’m here to tell you: one of the most commonly missed diligence items—reviewing the seller’s employee handbook—can absolutely wreck your post-close transition if you’re not careful.

Why Buyers Skip the Handbook (and Why They Shouldn’t)

Most buyers I work with focus heavily on financial due diligence. That makes sense—it’s an asset purchase, right? You’re not inheriting the liabilities, so why spend time reviewing policies that might get wiped away?

Here’s the catch: even if it’s an asset deal, the cultural and operational expectations baked into an employee handbook don’t vanish just because you form a new entity.

Case in point? A recent client of mine almost inherited a very expensive paid maternity leave policy… by accident.

The 70-Page Handbook That Could Have Tanked the Deal

This was a small company with less than fifteen employees. The seller had pulled together a 70-page handbook from various online templates and former employers. In doing so, she unknowingly committed the company to follow policies that are usually only required for companies with 50+ employees—like the Family Medical Leave Act (FMLA).

Also buried in that handbook? A promise of 12 weeks of paid parental leave. Not unpaid. Not optional. Paid.

The kicker? The seller had no idea it was in there. But I’m pretty sure the employees did—and understandably so. That’s a benefit anyone would quietly make note of.

The Post-Close Fallout Buyers Never Expect

In this case, the buyer couldn’t speak to the employees before closing. But once the deal went through, they had a real decision to make:

Do they match the 12-week paid policy (a cripplingly huge cost for a small business)?

Or do they scale it back and risk alienating key employees?

Either way, the oversight required urgent renegotiation and serious cash flow modeling. Not a great place to be as a new owner.

What Should Buyers Do Instead?

📌 Always review the employment handbook during diligence—yes, even if it’s an asset deal.

📌 Look out for accidental and not required adoption of major employment laws like FMLA, ADA, or state-specific rules.

📌 Flag anything that could set costly expectations for existing or future employees.

Buyers often don’t find out about these issues until after closing—when it’s too late. But catching it early gives you a chance to renegotiate the deal terms or account for the risk in your projections.

Bottom Line: It’s Not Just About the Financials

Your target firm can have a beautiful EBITDA multiple and still end up with a deal that eats you alive if you miss operational liabilities like this. Culture, policy, and people are just as important in small business acquisitions—and they deserve a few spots on your diligence checklist.

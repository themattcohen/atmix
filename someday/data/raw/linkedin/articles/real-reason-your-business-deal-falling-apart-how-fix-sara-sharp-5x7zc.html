The Real Reason Your Business Deal Is Falling Apart (and How to Fix It)

Created on 2025-10-08 21:16

Published on 2025-10-10 13:30

If you’ve ever been in the middle of a business deal that suddenly turned sideways — where one minute you’re exchanging friendly emails and the next you’re three weeks into stone-cold silence and passive-aggressive redlines — you’re not alone. As a deal attorney who helps people buy and sell businesses (especially accounting firms), I see this all the time.

And I can tell you that nine times out of ten, the problem isn’t legal.

It’s emotional.

More specifically, it’s a lack of empathy.

Now, I know. “Empathy” is not a word most people expect to hear from their lawyer. You’re here for leverage and protection, right? You want that iron-clad indemnification clause, the tightly defined reps and warranties, the fallback positions and “just-in-case” scenarios. And I’m here for all of that too — I draft them every day.

But if you’re trying to close a deal that actually survives contact with real humans, you need to understand what’s really at stake. And that means thinking beyond the paperwork.

Let’s talk about why empathy — not clever lawyering — is your secret weapon in dealmaking.

The Business Behind the Business

Here’s something I say often: you can’t run a professional services firm like it’s a widget company. When you’re buying or selling an accounting practice (or any business that relies on client relationships), the transition isn’t just transactional. It’s deeply personal.

Clients aren’t buying a tax return — they’re buying trust. They’ve often been with the same accountant for decades. They know the receptionist’s name. Their spouse sends Christmas cards to the clients. The seller is not just handing over financials. They’re handing over people’s trust, their own legacy, and often, a big piece of their identity.

That means the seller isn’t just “emotional” — they’re being human. And if you, the buyer, don’t get that? Good luck retaining clients after close.

Why Good Deals Go Bad

Deals fall apart for all sorts of reasons, but here are a few of the most common — and every one of them can be traced back to a lack of empathy:

1. The Vanishing Buyer

You think you’re being ghosted. In reality? The buyer was in a car accident, or their lender pulled funding, or their kid got hospitalized. I’ve seen all of these happen. Jumping to conclusions (“They’re wasting my time!”) doesn’t just poison the deal — it often gets you completely wrong.

2. The Nitpicky Redlines

You spent hours crafting your agreement. The other side sent back edits that feel like a slap in the face. It’s easy to get defensive. But here’s the reality: those changes probably came from their lawyer. And their lawyer may not have explained what they even mean. Your counterparty might be just as confused (and irritated) as you are.

3. The Earnout Fight

You want a retention adjustment to protect your downside. They want to get paid regardless. This is the most common impasse. But the truth is, both sides are trying to manage risk — you just haven’t translated that into shared goals yet.

Empathy as a Strategic Tool

Empathy doesn’t mean giving in. It means understanding the why behind the other person’s position.

Let’s say you’re a buyer who wants to delay discussing employee benefits until you’re in the end of the diligence process and can see what makes sense. Totally fair.

But the seller may know that one long-time employee — the one running payroll for 20 years — has a spouse battling cancer. That seller isn’t pushing for you to commit to providing benefits because they want to be difficult. They’re doing it because they feel a deep responsibility to their team.

And honestly? That’s the kind of seller you want to buy from. If they care that much about their people, odds are they ran a tight, ethical business. Which is exactly what you want to inherit.

Real Talk: The Indemnity Clause

Another common empathy test? Indemnification. Buyers want protection from anything that went wrong under the seller’s watch. Sellers want to avoid being on the hook for problems they didn’t even know about. This is normal.

But here’s where things go sideways: when you start negotiating “fundamental representations,” and it suddenly feels like one side doesn’t trust the other to even own the business they’re selling. That’s rarely true.

What’s usually happening is this: the seller is scared. Scared of signing something they don’t fully understand. Scared of making a mistake that could haunt them in retirement. Empathy in this moment means pausing the power play and asking: “What’s your concern here? Let’s unpack it.”

The Buyer’s Blind Spots

Buyers — especially first-time ones — can get tunnel vision. You’ve been searching for months. You’ve invested money in legal and financial diligence. You’ve got deal fatigue. So it’s easy to forget that the seller has been living this business for years, maybe decades. They know the clients, the staff, the quirks, the history.

If you walk in thinking your shiny new SOPs and aggressive growth plans are going to be welcomed with open arms, you’re setting yourself up for resistance.

Empathy doesn’t mean holding back — it means acknowledging what you’re stepping into. And building trust before you start changing everything.

Sellers, You’re Not Off the Hook

Empathy goes both ways. If you’re the seller, you’ve got to remember that your buyer is walking into a dark room. They don’t know which light switches work or where the furniture is lurking. And they’re usually terrified they’re going to step in something unpleasant.

Buyers ask a lot of questions because they’re trying to make sense of something that’s second nature to you. Try not to get offended. Try not to roll your eyes. Think about what you would have needed when you started out — and try to offer that.

Seller, remember yourself 15 years ago. You’d probably think that person has a lot to learn as well and you’re the very same person with the same goals and desires. Of course an entirely different person is going to see things a bit different than you.

How to Use This in Practice

Here’s my quick-start guide to bringing empathy into your next deal:

  • When someone makes a change you don’t like, ask why. Assume it’s not personal.

  • When someone goes quiet, ask if everything’s okay. Don’t assume bad faith.

  • When you’re feeling annoyed, stop and make the other side’s argument in your head.

  • When you’re about to draw a hard line, take one minute and ask, “What’s their concern here?”

Better yet — write it down. Or have your lawyer (hi, that’s me) write it out for you. Just go through the exercise of seeing it from their side.

Bottom Line

Deals are about risk and reward. But the best deals are also about people. And people make decisions based on emotion, fear, pride, and hope — not just logic or line items.

If you want to get more deals done, faster and with less drama, then empathy isn’t optional. It’s your best asset.

Use it.
